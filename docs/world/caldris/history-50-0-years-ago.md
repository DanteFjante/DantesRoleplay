# Caldris: The Roads That Lead to Today

*50 years ago to the campaign present · Authored world lore · Includes GM secrets*

Continues [The Years of Returning Footsteps](history-100-50-years-ago.md). Dates count backwards from the campaign present, **643 AS**. The final entry describes the world's starting condition. It does not begin a scene, move a character, reveal knowledge to the party, or decide the outcome of an available quest. **GM truth** paragraphs are private story material.

The twelve chronological entries, six mapped locations, historical accounts, and separate GM story material are published in live Caldris. Map anchors illustrate placement within parent locations and do not establish travel distances. The campaign clock and character knowledge remain unchanged.

## A peace becoming ordinary

Fifty years ago, the Three Banner War had been over for thirty-three years. By the campaign present, eighty-three years have passed since the Lantern Sea Accord. That distance has changed who explains the peace: first negotiators and returning soldiers, then their pupils and children, and increasingly officials whose experience begins with institutions already functioning.

The twelve polities remain distinct. Queen Adela II's Alderwick, the northern kingdoms, the Veyr Marches, and the states and cities around the Lantern Sea have different laws, interests, and memories. Their shared arrangements make travel possible without making every border simple. The older Highmead chronicles belong within this wider atlas.

This chapter closes the historical sequence without closing the world. Some mysteries have answers; others retain uncertainty that a future story can investigate. Ancient lives are not all secretly continuing, and present trouble does not all descend from one conspiracy. Ordinary people continue keeping gardens, teaching music, carrying letters, and feeding travellers while larger institutions argue about the terms under which they may do so.

## 49–46: The Witness Who Corrected the Monument

A proposed memorial inscription described a wartime convoy as having reached its destination intact. **Nera Coal**, the dwarven former quartermaster working with the Names House, supplied records of losses, replacements, and drivers who had completed the journey after the original wagons failed.

The organisers feared that changing the inscription would diminish the achievement. Nera argued that getting the supplies through had required precisely the work their wording erased. The revised text named repairers and substitute crews alongside the escort commander.

Some original participants disliked her account, and not every disputed detail could be settled. The Names House retained the evidence separately from the inscription. A public monument could commemorate the journey without becoming the sole record of everything that happened.

**Traces and stories:** Rejected inscription drafts, convoy accounts, and letters from households seeking recognition. A present commission can identify an omitted worker or establish why a familiar monument differs from an old photograph or drawing.

**GM truth:** Nera initially misremembered which crossing the replacement wagons used. A younger copyist corrected her using dated receipts. Her willingness to amend her own recollection is part of her value as a witness, not evidence that all testimony from her must be discarded.

## 45–42: The Toll Collected after the Repairs

A charge introduced to restore a damaged approach remained in force after the contracted work was complete. Local collectors argued that future maintenance justified retaining it. Carriers objected that the original authorisation described a temporary payment, while neighbouring crossings worried about losing traffic if they charged less.

**Carrier advocate Elna Vask** obtained a comparison of collections and actual expenditure. One district replaced the old charge with a smaller, explicitly reviewed contribution. Others retained their arrangements, citing different grants or postponed work.

The dispute produced a useful local precedent and no universal reform. Over subsequent decades, the practice of keeping temporary tolls became familiar in more places. Travellers learned to ask when a charge had last been reviewed, although the answer seldom made a cart move faster that day.

**Traces and stories:** Completed repair accounts, collection tallies, and notices with their original expiry terms. A present investigation may establish an obligation to hold a hearing rather than an immediate right to refuse every charge.

**GM truth:** Elna's own carrier association supported a similar charge elsewhere because its members benefited from the funded storehouse. Comparing both positions can expose selective argument without invalidating the evidence behind the successful challenge.

## 41–38: The Lists Carried by Water

In Namarra, difficulties following a disastrous census left some inhabited delta communities absent from official rolls. This period's accounts follow boat crews carrying supplies and household messages between places that administrative copies no longer described reliably.

At **Reedname Landing**, **boat organiser Sera Nemm** kept a practical list of departures, deliveries, and people seeking relatives. Her record was useful precisely because it concerned where people actually lived. It did not automatically settle their tax position or restore legal recognition.

The landing became one place where a traveller could ask careful questions about a changing delta. Some households welcomed documentation; others feared that renewed attention would bring seizures before protection. The difficulty survived beyond the immediate census dispute.

**Traces and stories:** Boat tallies, forwarding instructions, and maps whose named channels no longer match the water. A present journey can establish a community's continuity through several independent records. Publishing names indiscriminately may expose the households the investigation is meant to help.

**GM truth:** The established hidden history remains intact: water captains concealed communities with quiet royal consent so emergency grain would not be seized for debt. The public landing does not disclose the protected households' identities. Sera's complete list was divided among custodians rather than displayed for casual inspection.

## 37–34: The Hearing at the Closed Launch

After the remembered Grain Fleet Refusal in Carrowmere, an inquiry revisited the condition of vessels held ashore during a famine emergency. Shipwrights produced repair assessments showing that urgency had not made the hulls seaworthy. Officials disputed costs, authority, and responsibility for delayed supplies.

**Shipwright Bera Noll** preserved measurements and witness accounts in a small shelter beside the slip called **the Closed Launch**. The site became associated with the argument that workers who understood a vessel should have a meaningful way to stop an unsafe departure.

Several local contracts gained clearer inspection terms. Those gains required later enforcement and renewal; they did not permanently resolve disputes over hours, injury support, or the approaching season's grain fleet. The current Carrowmere controversy has its own participants and circumstances.

**Traces and stories:** Hull measurements, hearing copies, and amendments in later shipbuilding contracts. A present commission might recover a witness's notes, establish which protections survived renewal, or inspect a vessel whose paperwork looks better than its timber.

**GM truth:** The earlier clerk who quietly altered deadlines to prevent arrests remains missing from the formal record, as established elsewhere in Caldris. Bera possessed evidence of the altered dates but no reliable account of the clerk's later life. This chapter does not resolve that quest by inventing a convenient confession.

## 33–30: The Tables after the Council Meeting

In the Seven Lamps League, interpreters and representatives arriving from distant communities often had little time to compare what they had heard in formal sessions. **Host Jori Amat** rented **the Long Table Rooms**, offering meals, modest lodging, and a place to discuss the day's proceedings in several languages.

Some delegates remembered the older compromise known as the Meal Between Minutes. Their informal meals echoed that incident without restoring the procedural protection later chairmen had removed. A conversation over supper could prepare a petition; it could not guarantee that the council would hear it.

The rooms became useful to small delegations unable to hire permanent agents. They also attracted intermediaries selling introductions. Jori's household had to distinguish paid services from claims that buying a meal purchased influence.

**Traces and stories:** Meal accounts, interpreter bookings, and private drafts of public petitions. A present visitor can find a useful contact, discover an altered translation, or trace the older meal-pause provision through copies held elsewhere.

**GM truth:** One intermediary advertised access that Jori had never promised. Several customers nevertheless obtained useful help from ordinary interpreters working in the rooms. The fraud lies in the claimed privileged connection, not in every service the visitors received.

## 29–26: The Orchard that Counted Birds

Growers in Bellafont established **Swallowwatch Orchard**, a working plot where **gardener Tessa Brin** recorded flowering, insect activity, bird arrivals, and yields. Most entries were mundane. Neighbours contributed observations because comparisons helped them understand why adjoining plots behaved differently.

The records included mistakes, missed days, and disagreements over names for insects and plants. Tessa retained corrections alongside the first observations. Income came from fruit and propagation stock, with a small contribution for keeping the shared notes.

The orchard became a pleasant stopping place for travellers interested in growing things. Its most useful legacy was a run of ordinary years against which later disruption could be compared. Nobody founded it knowing that the Summer Without Swallows would make such comparisons urgent.

**Traces and stories:** Seasonal notebooks, graft labels, and observations supplied by neighbouring households. A present investigation can recover missing years or test whether a claimed pattern actually appears across several gardens.

**GM truth:** Tessa favoured one pruning method and initially interpreted ambiguous results in its favour. A neighbour's separate notes prompted a correction. The collection is valuable because it can be checked, not because its first keeper was incapable of bias.

## 25–22: Two Workshops through One Winter

Ashglass House in Istravel and keepers of the older light panes near Seln exchanged repair notes through carriers already moving ordinary goods. A shipment of compatible backing material reached the northern workshops late, delaying work until the coldest part of the season.

**Glass repairer Mera Dorr** and **keeper Ilren Sorr** agreed to complete a smaller repair that existing materials could support. The restored pane gave limited daylight at shared worktables. A donor who had expected a spectacular reopening instead attended a meal served beside ordinary lamps.

Both workshops kept copies of what had actually been altered. Their correspondence also included recipes, complaints about freight charges, and news of pupils. A practical exchange became a friendship between households that had never met in full.

**Traces and stories:** Paired repair diagrams, carrier receipts, and letters separating proposed work from completed work. A present malfunction can be understood by tracing the last repair rather than assuming the oldest surviving instructions describe the current object.

**GM truth:** The successful alteration reduced the pane's maximum brightness in exchange for reliability. Later publicity omitted the reduction. Restoring the advertised brilliance could undo the repair that allowed the pane to remain useful.

## 21–16: The Open Ledger Room

At Highmead, disputes over old obligations brought clerks, craftspeople, and household representatives together in **the Open Ledger Room**. **Reader Samin Vell** taught visitors how to compare dates, seals, units, renewals, and copies before drawing conclusions from an impressive document.

Some younger officials admired the Lantern Concord's apparent administrative consistency. The room's collections showed useful shared practices alongside evidence of coercion, exclusion, and claims made stronger simply because they fitted the accepted forms.

The teaching did not produce agreement about the Concord. It gave people better questions. A seven-sided seal could identify a tradition, but authority still depended on provenance, adoption, jurisdiction, and later history. The difference became increasingly important as questionable sealed instruments circulated.

**Traces and stories:** Teaching exercises, comparative seal casts, and documents whose impressive appearance conceals weak authority. A present case can begin with an ordinary reader noticing a mismatch that a patron's experts overlooked or chose not to discuss.

**GM truth:** One genuine old seal was copied legitimately for teaching. At least one later impression associated with the teaching copy was used outside that purpose. This creates a specific provenance question; it does not establish that all current counterfeit seals came from this room or share a single maker.

## 15–10: The Porch at the Last Mile

Near Bramblebridge's approach on the Lantern Road, **host Dessa Kell** added a covered porch where carriers could wait, repair straps, and compare local information before entering town. **The Last Mile Porch** served a practical arrival point at the road's end; it did not extend the Lantern Road beyond Bramblebridge under the same name.

Visitors exchanged directions, warnings about temporary closures, and news of households farther away. A message board carried requests for deliveries and repairs. Hosts removed claims they could show were false while marking uncertain reports rather than presenting every rumour as fact.

The porch grew familiar through repeated use. A traveller might remember a conversation there more clearly than the important building they had come to visit. Its household depended on paid food and lodging nearby, and keeping the waiting space available required labour.

**Traces and stories:** Message slips, repairs paid for by passing households, and directions corrected after local changes. A present errand can begin with a modest delivery or a question about someone who passed through recently.

**GM truth:** Dessa once suppressed an accurate complaint about a supplier on whom her business depended. She later restored it with a written correction. Her descendants have inherited a useful information place, not a guarantee that every keeper always preferred truth to convenience.

## 9–4: The Summer Without Swallows

The event remembered as the **Summer Without Swallows** disrupted familiar seasonal expectations. In affected districts, growers reported altered insect activity and reduced bird arrivals. Some places experienced the absence sharply; reports from other places were incomplete or contradictory.

Swallowwatch's older notebooks became valuable comparison material. **Observer Enna Brin** requested accounts of ordinary years as well as dramatic failures. Prophets and sellers of expensive remedies preferred simpler explanations, and several public claims travelled farther than the evidence supporting them.

The loss affected work, food, and the feeling of a season arriving correctly. People who seldom agreed about politics noticed the quiet beneath familiar eaves. Returning birds in some places brought relief without proving that every disrupted pattern had recovered.

**Traces and stories:** Nest counts, growing records, remedy purchases, and contrasting village accounts. A present investigation can test a specific local explanation, expose a fraudulent remedy, or identify habitat and route changes worth addressing.

**GM truth:** The regional pattern is not established as one supernatural event with a single hidden culprit. Local causes and incomplete evidence must be investigated on their merits. Existing prophecy and fraud plots remain available, but the absence itself does not verify them.

## 3–1: Two Funerals and Many New Claims

Two unrelated royal heirs died within eighteen months of one another in separate accidents. The deaths altered calculations about marriages, succession, and lesser claims. Households far from either court encountered the consequences through postponed agreements, new envoys, and requests to locate old papers.

Public grief and political opportunity occupied the same rooms. Some participants sincerely feared instability; others promoted claims that had attracted little attention when succession seemed settled. Rumour joined the deaths into a single pattern even where the available evidence did not.

These events did not resolve Alderwick's concerns over its childless queen or the Veyr Marches' separate questions about the dying High Warden. They added pressure to an already complicated political landscape rather than collapsing every disputed future into one inheritance.

**Traces and stories:** Cancelled arrangements, revised genealogies, envoy expenses, and records distinguishing acknowledged kinship from exaggerated claims. A present commission might authenticate a document or trace a witness without requiring the party to decide an entire kingdom's succession.

**GM truth:** The two deaths remain unrelated accidents, as the established world history states. People can exploit their coincidence without having caused them. A forged claim, manipulated report, or opportunistic marriage negotiation can provide a substantial mystery without retrospectively turning both funerals into assassinations.

## 0–0: Caldris at the Threshold

It is **643 AS**, eighty-three years after the Three Banner War ended. Trade continues and harvests remain broadly adequate, although burdens and local shortages are uneven. Temporary tolls persist, counterfeit seven-sided seals support questionable claims, and physical dragon evidence is appearing in different regions for different reasons.

The Lantern Sea Accord congress is due **next year at Aster Quay**, whose presiding chair is missing. Carrowmere shipwrights have justified concerns about the autumn grain fleet. Political households consider uncertain inheritances; village keepers consider roofs, fuel, and whether the next delivery will arrive before rain.

Children still learn adapted versions of old tunes. Letters wait for carriers, gardens preserve fragile varieties, and repaired objects pass into new hands. At Bramblebridge, roads and households already have histories before any adventurer enters them. The campaign's opening remains unplayed.

**Traces and stories:** A posting for a delivery, a disputed repair, a missing correspondent, an old seal needing comparison, or a request to assess a route can each begin a story. The scale may grow through what the party discovers and chooses; these beginnings do not prescribe a single chain of quests.

**GM truth:** These are world conditions, not newly triggered campaign incidents. The existing Measure-related material and independent local mysteries retain their own explanations. Dragon evidence, bird disruption, succession politics, and counterfeit documents must not automatically be treated as one cause merely because they coexist.

## Places to add to the world

### Reedname Landing — where the boats remember inhabited places

**Present-day form:** A modest public landing in Namarra with boat moorings, a covered waiting place, and a forwarding service. Its working records track changing channels and actual deliveries. The landing's visibility does not make confidential household lists public or settle the legal recognition of the communities its crews serve.

**A person to meet:** Boat organiser **Miri Nemm** needs a route checked after water levels change. She can explain how to reach the next agreed meeting point without exposing every household in her records.

**Adventure:** Deliver supplies, trace a missing boat, or gather independent evidence that a community has remained inhabited. Agree on how information will be used before turning a search into a published list of vulnerable people.

### The Closed Launch — the slip where a refusal became evidence

**Present-day form:** A shipyard slip and small record shelter in Carrowmere associated with the historical Grain Fleet Refusal and later hearings. Working vessels and timber stores have changed repeatedly. Surviving measurements and contract copies are kept alongside later inspection records, rather than treated as substitutes for examining a current hull.

**A person to meet:** Shipwright **Tessa Noll** wants an independent comparison of a disputed vessel's condition and the work its owner has actually paid for. Her concerns about safe work and injury support are practical and justified.

**Adventure:** Inspect timber, recover a missing assessment, or trace later use of the altered deadlines associated with the old clerk. The current labour dispute can be investigated without assuming that either its costs or its risks are imaginary.

### The Long Table Rooms — supper for people awaiting a hearing

**Present-day form:** Lodging and dining rooms in the Seven Lamps League used by small delegations, interpreters, and travellers conducting civic business. Meals and language services have stated prices. Informal discussion can prepare a formal case but confers no automatic authority over council proceedings.

**A person to meet:** Host **Bera Amat** needs help resolving a complaint against an intermediary claiming influence through her household. She wants to protect visiting delegates without dismissing the useful work of independent interpreters.

**Adventure:** Compare translations, find the missing copy of a petition, or connect visitors with a credible witness. A shared meal can introduce competing interests and useful friendships without obliging everyone to agree afterward.

### Swallowwatch Orchard — ordinary years kept for comparison

**Present-day form:** A working orchard and observation plot in Bellafont. Its notebooks compare weather, flowering, insect activity, birds, and yields across several decades. Trees and keepers have changed; corrections and gaps remain visible. Its observations address particular places and do not automatically explain every regional blight or failed migration.

**A person to meet:** Observer **Enna Brin** is gathering reliable accounts of the Summer Without Swallows and wants ordinary records from neighbouring gardens as well as striking stories.

**Adventure:** Recover missing notebooks, compare sightings, investigate a costly remedy, or help obtain healthy propagation stock. A useful result may be a modest change supported by evidence rather than a grand explanation of every absence.

### The Open Ledger Room — old documents read in good company

**Present-day form:** A public reading and teaching room in Highmead with comparative seal casts, local measures, and copies of disputed obligations. Original records remain with their custodians unless specifically deposited. Staff distinguish teaching examples, authenticated evidence, and unanswered questions.

**A person to meet:** Reader **Samin Vell** still works here with younger assistants. He wants to establish how an impression associated with a teaching copy entered circulation, while refusing to blame everyone who attended his lessons.

**Adventure:** Trace a seal impression, compare dates and units, or locate the later renewal behind an old claim. A genuine object may have been used misleadingly, and a copied document may preserve an authentic agreement.

### The Last Mile Porch — a place to arrive before the story begins

**Present-day form:** A covered waiting and message space at Bramblebridge's Lantern Road approach, supported by nearby food and lodging. Travellers mend straps, arrange deliveries, and compare current directions. Posted notices distinguish confirmed information from reports still awaiting a witness.

**A person to meet:** Keeper **Ossa Kell** maintains the board and asks messengers to remove outdated requests. She knows who has left a message, not necessarily whether every story behind it is true.

**Adventure:** Take an ordinary delivery, locate a recent traveller, bring back a route report, or return a borrowed object. These are optional starting opportunities; no notice has been accepted, no character has arrived, and no encounter has begun merely because the porch exists.

## Two thousand years behind the next doorway

Caldris's authored historical sequence now reaches from two thousand years before the campaign to its present threshold. It contains old communal stores, vanished households, revised charters, surviving local wonders, wars, repairs, and choices that later people have understood imperfectly.

Ysra has been dead for **594 years**, and Orven for **279**. Aurr's death is settled. Aveline, Leris, and Nen retain their established identities and shared history. Nera Coal remains a living dwarven witness whose knowledge follows her experience. Nivren remains eligible to appear, but his present location and participation are still unset; no unfinished ancient life is required to make the present interesting.

Little Lastlight, the Siltwife, the Mile Hound, ember herons, rainbell pears, and old light panes have particular histories and limits. Their current condition can be established through local evidence and later maintenance records. They are neither universal solutions nor automatic manifestations of one hidden power.

The quest situations attached to these chapters remain opportunities. Their people may seek help, decline involvement, disagree, or change their minds when play begins. The history supplies things worth finding and reasons for people to care; it does not decide what the party will value, whom they will trust, or where they will go.

The campaign clock remains at its starting point. The next footsteps belong to the story still to be played.
