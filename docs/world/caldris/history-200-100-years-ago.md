# Caldris: The Century of Gathering Roads

*200–100 years before the campaign present · Authored world lore · Includes GM secrets*

Continues [The Century of Distant Hearths](history-300-200-years-ago.md). Dates count backwards from the campaign present. Historical events belong to the world's past; present-day people and adventure premises are not campaign actions already underway. Explanations labelled **GM truth** are private story material.

The twelve chronological entries, six mapped locations, historical accounts, and separate GM story material are published in live Caldris. Map anchors illustrate placement within parent locations and do not establish travel distances. Character knowledge and campaign progress are unchanged.

## A world becoming familiar

Two hundred years ago, many roads and institutions recognizable in present-day Caldris already existed, although their boundaries, owners, and practical influence continued to change. The older Crownmaking remained centuries in the past. Highmead's regional chronicles described local rulers and obligations within that larger political history, not a separate empire governing both continents.

Growing trade linked places whose interests did not always coincide. Towns wanted dependable grain, landholders wanted predictable revenues, and carriers wanted permission to choose routes that were actually passable. A promise of protection could reassure one community while making its neighbour fear encirclement.

The Three Banner War is remembered as ending eighty-three years before the present. This chapter develops pressures that later participants could exploit; it does not move that ending or presume that every disagreement already belonged to the war. Choices, accidents, and attempts at peace still matter.

For most people, the century was also a succession of harvests, apprenticeships, marriages, repairs, and departures. Its traces lie close enough to the present to appear in family papers and buildings still in use. Some long-lived witnesses remain possible, but no witness remembers an entire century equally well.

## 199–192: The Ferry Village at Rookwater

A shifting channel in the Vey Lowlands made a minor ferry unexpectedly useful. **Ferry keeper Sella Rusk** added a covered waiting place, and a wheelwright and two trading households settled nearby. The cluster acquired the name **Rookwater**, after birds nesting above the landing.

Traffic brought work and disputes. Older grazing households objected when temporary cart yards became permanent enclosures. A local agreement reserved access to the water and required the ferry business to contribute to maintenance of the muddy approach. It was revised after the first serious flood demonstrated how little the new settlement controlled the river.

Rookwater became a modest stopping place rather than a great port. Its first school occupied a room rented above a harness shop, and its market depended on travellers choosing this particular crossing.

**Traces and stories:** Ferry permits, grazing agreements, flood marks, and a school exercise naming the earliest households. A present quarrel over access may turn on later renewals of the reserved river path. An old mooring ring can reveal where the working channel once lay.

**GM truth:** Sella understated the expected cart traffic when negotiating the first agreement. The affected households subsequently secured better terms because their observations proved accurate. Her importance to the village does not make every complaint against her a misunderstanding.

## 191–184: The Voices Beneath Glasswinter Tarn

Surveyors in the Lornesse uplands reported faint speech and music beneath the first clear ice of **Glasswinter Tarn**. **Surveyor Bren Tallow** returned with a local guide and found that certain sounds made beside the water reappeared after freezing. A shouted name might return broken into syllables; a tune might lose every pause.

Visitors treated the phenomenon as an oracle. Two people died crossing ice whose apparent clarity disguised dangerous thin patches. The nearest households established a viewing place on firm ground and refused to guide paying visitors onto the tarn merely because they believed the voices were calling them.

Careful notebooks distinguished echoes of recent visitors from unexplained older fragments. The tarn became a place of curiosity, mourning, and occasional music, with winters when it produced no discernible voices at all.

**Traces and stories:** Weather notes, copied fragments, and the paths used by the rescue party. A present investigation can identify a recorded speaker or compare a melody with regional songs. Observing several freeze cycles may be more useful than an expensive attempt to demand an answer.

**GM truth:** The effect preserves fragments of physical sound under particular water and freezing conditions. It contains no souls, knows no future, and cannot hold a conversation. A few older fragments remain unexplained because their original speakers were never identified, not because an unseen intelligence is necessarily withholding the truth.

## 183–176: The Narrow Bells of Highmead

**Bell founder Jessa Varr** developed a compact bell suitable for roadside houses and small civic towers. **The Narrow Bell Foundry** obtained several contracts after demonstrating that communities could hear distinct signals without paying for a monumental casting.

Different customers assigned different meanings to the same number of strokes. Travellers mistook a fire signal for a market announcement, and one carrier group approached a closed crossing after hearing what it understood as permission to proceed. Several road districts agreed on a limited shared signal for immediate danger while preserving local customs for ordinary business.

Foundry marks spread farther than Jessa's actual production. Later bells copied the shape, some competently and some with concealed cracks. A recognizable silhouette became useful evidence, but never complete proof of origin.

**Traces and stories:** Casting accounts, signal tables, and bells with mismatched clappers. A present commission can establish why a warning was misunderstood, identify a dangerous replacement, or recover the local meaning of a forgotten sequence.

**GM truth:** A foreman sold one rejected casting to settle a private debt. The resulting dispute nearly destroyed the young foundry. Jessa replaced the bell but concealed the foreman's identity, leaving a compensation payment that later readers could mistake for an admission that every bell in the batch was defective.

## 175–168: The Orchard of Departures

At Boughmeet, households leaving for new work gave grafts to a nursery plot kept by **gardener Ilva Penn**. The trees were labelled with destinations as well as names. Some families sent letters reporting success; others returned, moved again, or ceased writing.

The plot became **the Orchard of Departures**. It preserved useful varieties and offered a place to remember people whose graves, if any, lay far away. Ilva refused to remove the tree of a family that had returned in debt. Departure had been part of their lives even though the journey had not produced the story their neighbours expected.

One annual gathering paired fruit from the orchard with dishes described in travellers' letters. The food was often adapted to local ingredients, producing traditions that belonged fully to neither place of origin.

**Traces and stories:** Graft labels, forwarding addresses, and recipes copied from letters. A quest can trace an absent household, identify a living tree under a changed name, or restore a lost variety without assuming that every silence conceals a crime.

**GM truth:** One family stopped writing because its members disagreed about whether to return. They remained alive elsewhere during this period. Later descendants may have inherited several incompatible accounts of an ordinary but painful separation.

## 167–160: The Host Who Hung No Banner

The orc host **Havra Renn** opened **the Lantern Hospice** on a Veyr road used by carriers, pilgrims, and seasonal workers. Its name described hospitality rather than a hospital or a claim to cure illness. Havra accepted travellers from rival districts and declined to display an exclusive patron's banner above the door.

A sponsor withdrew support, and the house survived its next winter through smaller contributions, paid lodging, and work shared among residents. Havra enforced rules against violence and intimidation while allowing arguments that other hosts preferred to silence.

The house's reputation grew after it sheltered a stranded messenger whose employers were unpopular locally. That welcome did not make the messenger admirable, innocent of every accusation, or grateful forever. It established that a meal and a dry place need not depend on complete agreement.

**Traces and stories:** Lodging lists, cancelled sponsorship terms, and repairs funded by many small households. A present dispute can test which parts of the welcome remain affordable and what limits are necessary to protect other guests.

**GM truth:** Havra quietly refused one traveller who had repeatedly threatened workers and later claimed political persecution. The founding custom was conditional hospitality with enforceable conduct rules, not an obligation to expose residents to anyone who demanded entry.

## 159–152: Three Copies of One Promise

Delegates concerned about escorts, grain passage, and emergency aid exchanged agreements meant to clarify mutual support. Their copies used similar language but differed in how they described territorial defence, protection of shipments, and the obligations of successor rulers.

**Clerk Mera Senn** circulated a comparison identifying the differences. The delegates accepted practical arrangements for the next season and postponed a complete reconciliation. Carriers benefited immediately; advisers later cited the same papers in support of incompatible positions.

These agreements became part of the background to later political tension. They did not compel a future war. Several renewals narrowed disputed terms, and local cooperation continued despite the unresolved language.

**Traces and stories:** Divergent copies, Mera's comparison, and later qualifications attached to individual renewals. A missing page may explain why two households sincerely remember different promises. Authenticity alone cannot decide which version an institution actually adopted.

**GM truth:** Mera's comparison was received and read. The problem was not that nobody noticed a hidden clause; influential delegates preferred an incomplete agreement to losing the immediate benefits. Later claims of pure surprise conceal that conscious tradeoff.

## 151–144: The Hound Beside the Milestone

Travellers near the Veyr approaches reported a dark, shaggy hound appearing beside a damaged milestone during heavy weather. It followed stragglers toward a sheltered turn and disappeared before reaching the nearest house. **Carrier Tovin Kell** recorded several sightings without being able to find an owner.

Hosts left water and repaired the path. Some travellers credited the **Mile Hound** with rescues; others never saw it despite searching. A small roadside custom developed around checking whether anyone had fallen behind before leaving the milestone.

When a later flood changed the approach, sightings shifted to the surviving stretch of older road. Guides who insisted that the creature must appear beside the stone itself began leading customers away from the most reliable route.

**Traces and stories:** Sighting notes, old path surfaces, and descriptions of a distinctive white patch beneath the jaw. A present encounter can reward attention to the road and its travellers. The hound's behaviour may help locate an overlooked shelter, but it is not a guaranteed escort.

**GM truth:** The Mile Hound is a local spirit associated with repeated journeys along a particular surviving route. It is not a transformed lost child or a creature anyone can own by taking the milestone home. Its continued activity in the present depends on later conditions that a quest can investigate.

## 143–136: The Maps That Were Never Sold

At Leth Sarai, **chart copyist Dena Orrel** assembled conflicting harbour and road surveys in a rented hall. Customers expected a definitive atlas. Dena instead marked uncertainty, seasonal restrictions, and information supplied by witnesses she could not yet corroborate.

Several patrons withdrew orders when the maps failed to endorse routes they wanted advertised. The collection became known as **the Hall of Unsent Maps**, although its keepers continued selling useful extracts and correcting them as reports arrived.

One chart showed a tempting coastal shortcut that had never been surveyed throughout its entire length. Dena left the missing section blank. A later decorative copy filled it with an attractive line, which circulated more widely than the qualified original.

**Traces and stories:** Annotated drafts, cancelled commissions, and later copies with different confidence marks. A present expedition can investigate a missing section rather than assume that a beautifully drawn road exists. Finding the original may explain where a previous traveller went wrong.

**GM truth:** Dena removed one accurate landing from publicly sold extracts after learning it was being used to locate fleeing debtors and their families. The restricted original survives in the collection's custody history. Its accuracy does not settle whether publishing the location would be a good decision now.

## 135–128: The Last Crossing of Aurr

Aurr left the Veyr uplands with visiting members of another giant household. Human neighbours helped carry smaller possessions and provisions to the lower meeting place. The giant did not finish the stair or appoint a permanent guardian over it.

A winter later, a returning visitor brought word that Aurr had died among kin. **Keeper Sela Renn** recorded the report beside the Winter Latch's older rescue account. The departure itself had been witnessed locally; knowledge of the death arrived through trusted testimony rather than a human burial procession.

Households maintained the lower refuge for their own journeys and continued respecting the older burial shelf. The practice no longer depended on hoping its giant protector would come back to enforce it.

**Traces and stories:** A provisions list, accounts of the farewell, and the visitor's recorded message. A quest can establish what became of Aurr's tools or trace the later giant household. The abandoned upper works remain a practical and historical question.

**GM truth:** The report is accurate: Aurr died naturally after the departure, and this chapter closes the question of survival into the present. The tools travelled to kin. A later claim that they were hidden beneath the stair is a treasure story, not an unresolved fact waiting to be confirmed.

## 127–120: The Grain Houses with Empty Lofts

Several towns paid advances for grain expected from the same producing district. An adequate harvest concealed losses during storage and transport, and a dispute over payment delayed releases from surviving stocks. Consumers saw rising prices while merchants circulated assurances based on quantities that no longer existed in full.

**Storehouse examiner Ilren Vask** persuaded local authorities to compare physical stock with pledged amounts. Emergency purchases and reduced releases prevented the worst shortages in some places, but poorer households still ate less and sold tools they later needed for work.

The response encouraged inspection rights in several renewed supply agreements. It also made officials more anxious about controlling grain passage, contributing another pressure that later political actors could amplify or restrain.

**Traces and stories:** Loft measurements, spoiled-grain disposal records, and duplicated pledges. A present claim may require distinguishing deliberate concealment from a shipment lost after an honest agreement. Recovering a measuring tool can help interpret an account written in an obsolete local unit.

**GM truth:** Ilren initially understated losses at a storehouse managed by a friend. A second examiner corrected the report. The final useful reform emerged from disagreement and checking, not from a solitary official whose first judgement was always right.

## 119–112: The Duet Played Without Its Visitor

Nivren Sael promised to return for a small musical gathering near Three-Reed Bridge. Flooded routes and an illness delayed his journey. **Musician Ossa Kett**, who had prepared the other part, performed with a younger neighbour rather than leaving an empty chair at the centre of the occasion.

Nivren arrived after the gathering and heard the revised duet in a kitchen. He disliked discovering that his absence had improved one transition, then asked for a copy. Ossa told him that waiting had once been an act of friendship; making every future gathering depend on him would become an obstacle to living.

Their correspondence continued for several years. The episode became a local story about preparing a welcome that could survive the guest arriving late.

**Traces and stories:** Two arrangements, a flood-delayed itinerary, and letters kept in different households. A quest can reunite the parts or trace a copy carried elsewhere. Neither version needs to be exposed as a fraud for the difference between them to matter.

**GM truth:** Ossa was angry as well as understanding. Nivren's delay was real, but he had also left too little time for an ordinary journey to go wrong. His long life did not exempt him from needing to plan around other people's shorter opportunities.

## 111–100: The Spring of Unspent Muster Money

Anxiety over disputed obligations led several districts to set aside money for escorts and possible levies. Some advisers pressed for immediate mobilisation. Others argued that calling workers away during planting would create the shortages officials claimed to fear.

**Delegate Havren Dorn** secured a limited inspection and messenger arrangement in place of one proposed levy. The decision freed money for repairs and avoided disrupting several communities that season. Rival advocates called the delay weakness; the households planting their fields experienced it as a reprieve.

At the century's close, the political questions remained unresolved. Cooperation had achieved something real without settling succession, grain passage, or mutual defence for all future rulers. Later conflict should not erase the people who successfully prevented an earlier one.

**Traces and stories:** Unissued equipment orders, messenger payments, and redirected repair funds. A present historian might recover evidence that an apparent failed mobilisation was deliberately cancelled. An institution claiming never to have compromised may have depended on exactly such a decision.

**GM truth:** Havren was willing to support a narrower armed response if the inspection failed. The achievement was a specific practical choice, not proof that one delegate could have prevented every later war. Several local agreements survived the episode and may matter when reconstructing how the subsequent conflict spread.

## Places to add to the world

### Rookwater — the ferry village beside the changing channel

**Present-day form:** A small settlement in the Vey Lowlands with a ferry landing, harness workshop, modest market, and houses repeatedly adapted to flooding. The working channel has shifted since its foundation. Public access to the water survives through later agreements that residents must still maintain and interpret.

**A person to meet:** Ferry keeper **Bera Rusk** needs an alternate landing assessed before paying for permanent works. She is also trying to keep access open while neighbouring owners dispute where the reserved path now runs.

**Adventure:** Trace flood marks and later agreements, recover an old mooring survey, or investigate a missing supply cart. A solution must work for the current river rather than reproduce the first settlement's map.

### Glasswinter Tarn — the lake that keeps fragments of sound

**Present-day form:** An upland tarn in Lornesse, approached by a marked path and a viewing place on firm ground. Local notes record irregular acoustic phenomena during certain freezes. Conditions vary, and clear ice is not treated as proof that the surface can bear travellers.

**A person to meet:** Guide **Mera Tallow** wants help comparing current observations with older notebooks. She refuses commissions that require leading visitors onto untested ice to satisfy a theory.

**Adventure:** Identify a fragment of speech or music, locate a lost observation notebook, or determine whether a claimed recording actually originated here. The answers can be historical and personal without turning the tarn into an oracle.

### The Narrow Bell Foundry — small bells with widely travelled voices

**Present-day form:** A working foundry in Highmead with later furnaces and a retained collection of casting marks, failed pieces, and signal tables. Its reputation rests on inspection and repair as much as new bells. Objects attributed to its founders require assessment rather than automatic acceptance.

**A person to meet:** Bell founder **Samin Varr** is investigating a dangerous casting sold under the old mark. A good account of its travels would help him decide which repairs and compensation claims the workshop should accept.

**Adventure:** Trace a replacement clapper, authenticate a bell, or recover the local meaning of a signal before a public ceremony. The wrong sound can reveal an error, an attempted deception, or a forgotten change in civic practice.

### The Orchard of Departures — grafts named for distant homes

**Present-day form:** A nursery and memorial plot in Boughmeet with propagated descendants of some older trees and replacements for others. Labels distinguish documented continuity from commemorative replanting. The associated collection keeps forwarding notes and recipes contributed by travelling households.

**A person to meet:** Keeper **Enna Penn** wants to correct several labels before an anniversary gathering. A patron hopes for a simple celebration of successful journeys; Enna wants returning families and incomplete stories included too.

**Adventure:** Trace a household, recover a variety, or reunite a recipe with the people who still make it. An absent family may have made a new life rather than vanished under sinister circumstances.

### The Lantern Hospice — a welcome without an exclusive banner

**Present-day form:** A roadside hospitality house in the Veyr Marches offering paid lodging and a limited number of supported places. Its rooms and stores have been rebuilt repeatedly. Conduct rules apply to patrons as well as guests, and its founders' refusal of exclusive sponsorship remains a debated tradition.

**A person to meet:** Host **Dessa Renn** must decide whether to accept a generous donation carrying restrictions on who may stay. She wants practical alternatives, not praise for sacrificing the household's winter supplies.

**Adventure:** Secure supplies, mediate between guests, or establish whether a patron's claimed founding rights appear in any recognised later agreement. Keeping the house open may require several modest commitments rather than one heroic donor.

### The Hall of Unsent Maps — an atlas with honest blank spaces

**Present-day form:** A chart collection and copying business in Leth Sarai. Working extracts are updated as reports arrive; historical drafts retain their original qualifications. Restricted charts have custody records, and decorative copies are identified separately from surveys intended for travel.

**A person to meet:** Copyist **Teren Orrel** needs a disputed coastal section surveyed. He is wary of an investor who wants a completed route drawn before anyone has travelled it.

**Adventure:** Investigate a missing section, trace a misleading copy, or retrieve observations from a failed expedition. Accurate information may still require judgement about its circulation and the people affected by it.

## At 100 years ago

Ysra has been dead for 494 years and Orven for 179. Aurr's death is now established, closing another long thread. Nivren remains eligible to appear in the campaign present, but this history leaves his current location and willingness to become involved unset.

The Three Banner War's known ending lies seventeen years ahead of this chapter's closing boundary. Its exact escalation and course belong to the remaining history. This century leaves authentic attempts at cooperation as well as pressures that later leaders and communities will confront.

Rookwater's ferry, Highmead's smaller bells, roadside hospitality, and imperfect charts have become part of ordinary life. Records are close enough to the present to connect with living institutions and relatively recent family histories, but age alone still proves neither ownership nor obligation.

The next century can bring Caldris fully to the campaign threshold: the war and its aftermath, surviving friendships, disputed inheritances, the maintenance of local wonders, and the particular people who will be there when the story finally begins.
