# Caldris: The Hills Beyond the Map

*900–600 years before the campaign present · Working setting lore · Includes GM secrets*

Continues [The Tides of Promise](history-1200-900-years-ago.md). Dates count backwards from the campaign present. The opening describes the world at 900 years ago; the dated periods begin with the following year.

This chapter adds freely revisable history, locations, and people for the setting before play begins, as authorised by the user. Its core chronology, mapped locations, historical accounts, and separately scoped GM story material are now published in live Caldris. Present-day situations are adventure premises, not events already occurring. The historical narratives can become discoverable lore; **GM truth** paragraphs should remain separate when preparing player-facing material.

## Where the familiar road ended

A map sold at Alderwake might show every authorised toll between the town and the sea, yet leave the hills north of the Roven Marches almost empty. People lived in those hills. They had orchards, quarrels, winter stores, and detailed opinions about travellers who mistook an unfamiliar country for an uninhabited one.

Southward, Highmead's Merrow rulers governed through old agreements and new compromises. The former river kingdom remained divided between Veymouth, the Reedbank communities, and South Vey. Beyond Morrow Strait, households in Serevan and Selvar traded with inland neighbours whose lives rarely appeared in Chalklands histories.

The previous centuries had left a fragile peace, useful roads, and institutions that worked best when someone maintained them. Families inherited records of losses they no longer fully understood. Traders inherited routes whose hazards had changed. Small spirits inherited neighbours who had forgotten their grandparents' promises.

Ysra Leth, born 1,310 years before the campaign present, had recently departed north at about 410 years of age. She carried botanical notes, a much-repaired fiddle associated with her late friend Jalen, and enough experience to know that a stranger's invitation was not the same as permission to stay indefinitely.

Over the next three centuries, unfamiliar countries would become less unfamiliar, a spectacular flood would alter the riverlands, and stories about the past would become valuable enough to counterfeit. The history remains regional: it follows the Chalklands, its connected waterways and roads, and an expanding circle of neighbours rather than assigning the same experiences to all Caldris.

## First century: The Country Beyond the Pass — 900–800 years ago

### 899–888: The Valley Already Named

Ysra's expedition crossed beyond the northern routes described in its copies of the Book of Roads Left Open. Its members expected difficult travel and unknown plants. They found **Aunholt**, a high valley with stone-lined irrigation channels, scattered hamlets, and a seasonal assembly held beneath an enormous timber roof.

Their guide, **Rovan Pell**, prepared a formal declaration naming the valley after the expedition's patron. **Speaker Meret Ulan** politely supplied its existing name and asked which household would pay for the expedition's fodder. Ysra entered both conversations in her notebook.

The visitors spent a season exchanging knowledge with local growers. Some plants they considered rare were ordinary garden crops; others were protected because collecting them damaged grazing or water sources. The expedition's most useful achievement was arranging future visits on terms the valley's residents understood and accepted.

**Traces and stories:** Two incompatible expedition maps; botanical names followed by local corrections; fodder payments omitted from the patron's public account. A modern route hunt can succeed by seeking the local name rather than the famous expedition name.

**GM truth:** Aunholt had traded westward for generations. Contact with these particular southern travellers widened an existing network; it did not awaken an isolated people to the existence of the world.

### 887–875: The Orchard of Winter Apples

Aunholt's best-known export was a small, sharp apple that stored well in cool conditions. Carriers called it the **winter lantern** for its yellow skin. Buyers in the lower valleys paid unusually high prices after discovering that a sound harvest could survive much of the winter journey.

**Grower Hella Vorr** travelled south with grafting stock, expecting to establish a profitable nursery. Some grafts flourished; others failed in warmer, wetter ground. She stayed several years to compare soils and rootstocks with Boughmeet's growers, while her siblings maintained the original orchards.

The exchange produced useful varieties and extravagant claims. A Highmead merchant advertised apples that never spoiled. His customers received ordinary fruit and an instructive disappointment. Songs later connected the winter lantern to Harth's sleeping Guest, although its cultivation belonged to a different history.

**Traces and stories:** Nursery labels preserving several names for the same stock; storage-pit plans; an apple-shaped shop sign. An old graft lineage can matter when a modern disease harms more fashionable varieties.

**GM truth:** The fruit was naturally well suited to storage when handled correctly. It was not enchanted, and the Guest had neither created it nor promised to protect its growers. Agricultural knowledge deserves its own place among the setting's achievements.

### 874–862: The Singing Wood of Seln

Farther along Aunholt's western trade route stood **Seln**, a woodland community known for instrument timber and a stand of hollow-trunked trees that sounded in particular winds. Local musicians tuned pipes against those tones before cutting or drying new wood.

Ysra visited with **Edan Rill**, an Aunholt carrier learning to make instruments. They heard a melody the residents called the Trees' Welcome. Its changing notes belonged partly to wind and wood, partly to the musicians answering them from a sheltered platform.

A small place-spirit, **the Listener in the Fork**, could hold a recently heard note for a little while after a performer stopped. It neither composed on command nor preserved centuries of music. When visiting patrons demanded performances in unsuitable weather, the sound was unimpressive and the Listener withdrew.

**Traces and stories:** Timber marks on older instruments; a platform foundation; tuning notes accompanied by weather observations. Restoring an instrument may require understanding the living conditions in which its makers worked, not simply obtaining more rare wood.

**GM truth:** The grove's beauty arose from a collaboration among natural acoustics, musicians, and a modest spirit. The claim that every tree contained a dead singer was a later invention that encouraged destructive relic collecting.

### 861–849: The Crown Carried in a Bread Cart

Highmead's **Queen Deryn Merrow** travelled through the northern estates during a period of poor roads and unsettled loyalties. A damaged ceremonial wagon forced her household to move several chests into an ordinary baker's cart. One held the travelling crown used at public audiences.

At Tallow Ford, a crowd saw household guards anxiously protecting a wagon that smelled of bread. The story became a favourite court comedy. Its more consequential aftermath was Deryn's discovery that her appointed escort had been commandeering provisions without receipts.

She ordered compensation from the royal travelling budget and dismissed two officers. The affected households received different amounts and waited different lengths of time. Her progress helped renew practical agreements without ending every dispute between the crown and the northern districts.

**Traces and stories:** Compensation rolls; a painted bread cart in a civic hall; a ceremonial crown confused with several later replacements. A family's apparently absurd account of feeding a royal retinue may be supported by an overlooked payment entry.

**GM truth:** The baker, **Orris Den**, charged a fair hiring fee and refused a permanent court appointment. He preferred a predictable business and his own bed. His refusal was neither an insult nor evidence of secret allegiance.

### 848–836: The Market of Borrowed Tongues

Trade between Selvar, Serevan, and the farther inland road brought more languages into **Namar Vale**. Interpreters had long worked privately, but a dispute over a bulk cloth order led to a market practice of naming the interpreter and retaining a short summary in both parties' languages.

**Interpreter Saha Mir** organised a shared workroom at **Seven Scripts Court**, where translators could compare difficult terms and consult specialist traders. The court never contained exactly seven languages at every period; the name came from its first commissioned sign.

Some mistakes were innocent. Others involved an interpreter pressured by a wealthy client, or a buyer pretending a familiar trade term was incomprehensible. Publicly recording who had explained what helped, while creating new disputes over liability when circumstances changed.

**Traces and stories:** Parallel-language summaries; tablets listing disputed cloth grades; marginal explanations written for apprentices. A supposed secret code may be an ordinary mixture of languages used by people who shared a trade but not a first language.

**GM truth:** No single enchanted translation device made this work unnecessary. Skilled interpretation remained about context, intent, custom, and accountability as well as vocabulary.

### 835–823: The Parcel That Had Arrived Somewhere Else

A record copied at Seven Scripts Court attracted the attention of **archivist Fenn Arul**. It described a small consignment whose workshop mark matched the inventory later found beneath the Seventh Hearth. Further comparisons connected it to Aveline's supposedly undelivered parcel.

The new evidence showed that the parcel had reached a successor to its intended Istravel workshop and had been opened **1,338 years before the campaign present**, seventy-two years after Aveline's death. The original recipient had already died. The acknowledgement was addressed through an obsolete carrier and never reached the records used by her executors.

The opening account listed a personal letter, corrected preparation notes, and a small glass reference piece wrapped for protection. The recipients incorporated the useful material into their workshop collection instead of preserving a sealed curiosity. Copies of Fenn's findings reached Sallowmere by different routes.

**Traces and stories:** An opening receipt; corrections attributed through a workshop rather than a famous author; a failed delivery address. A present search can follow the dispersed contents and the missing personal letter rather than pursue a parcel still waiting unopened.

**GM truth:** The reference piece was a marked calibration blank for comparison during glass preparation, not a key or recorded farewell. The letter concerned an earlier disagreement and an unfinished act of thanks. This resolves the parcel's arrival while leaving its full wording and the later custody of its contents available for discovery.

### 822–810: The Goat Path and the Painted Road

Aunholt and the Roven Marches negotiated a southern carrier route. The patron financing its map wanted a line suitable for wagon traffic. Local hauliers warned that one section could support pack animals but not carts without extensive work.

The published ceremonial map showed a grand continuous road. The working copy, prepared by **surveyor Nerin Oss**, marked unloading points, narrow turns, and shelter limits. Early travellers who bought the wrong edition arrived angry, delayed, and unexpectedly dependent on local carriers.

The resulting arrangement kept the difficult section as a managed pack route. A small transfer settlement, **Goat's Ledger**, grew where goods moved between carts and animal trains. Its first warehouse was smaller than the road depicted on the patron's wall map.

**Traces and stories:** Different line conventions in related maps; pack-frame fragments; warehouse fees recording mixed transport. Reopening an old route may require local hauling services rather than engineering a grand road nobody can afford to maintain.

**GM truth:** Nerin did not sabotage the project. Her warnings were accurate. Later critics blamed her caution for the patron's embarrassment, while the smaller route continued supporting useful trade.

### 809–800: The First Feast of Two Harvests

Growers from Aunholt and the Chalklands celebrated a successful season of exchanged rootstocks, storage methods, and seeds at Boughmeet. The event became known as the **Feast of Two Harvests**, though participants came from more than two districts and their results varied considerably.

Ysra returned for the gathering, now about five centuries old. She carried drawings of Seln's instrument wood and a melody she had learned there, played on Jalen's increasingly repaired fiddle. Several listeners knew versions of Jalen's songs without knowing who he had been.

The feast's most durable outcome was an agreement to record failures alongside successful cultivation trials. A Harth delegation attended without claiming to speak for its sleeping Guest. The orchard communities had lives, expertise, and hospitality of their own.

**Traces and stories:** Trial beds bearing regional names; a recipe combining dried fruit with local grain; musical notation beside plant records. A forgotten failed trial may explain why a tempting modern planting would harm a particular soil or water arrangement.

**GM truth:** The gathering produced no universal crop or perfect harmony. Several visitors returned home disappointed. Its success lay in continuing exchange after people learned what would not work.

## Second century: The River That Changed Its Mind — 799–700 years ago

### 799–788: The Spring of Floating Doors

Heavy upland rain, snowmelt, and damaged river defences combined in a major flood across parts of the Vey basin. The water overtopped banks, damaged crossings, and carried household doors downstream among timber and wrecked boats.

Some Reedbank communities evacuated early through maintained channels. Others had fewer boats, outdated warnings, or nowhere safe to move their animals. The flood's uneven harm became a source of bitter argument once the immediate danger passed.

At **Doorhaven**, a temporary camp on firm ground, **boatwoman Edda Rusk** used salvaged doors as makeshift tables and raised sleeping platforms. The camp's name preserved that practical improvisation. People brought missing-person descriptions, wet account books, hens in baskets, and rumours about which upstream landholders had diverted the water.

**Traces and stories:** Mud lines inside older buildings; lists of borrowed boats; household marks on reused doors. A later exposed deposit may contain objects from several settlements, so find location alone does not prove original ownership.

**GM truth:** No single act caused the whole disaster. One estate had obstructed a relief channel and worsened local flooding, but blaming it for every flooded village conceals other failures and the force of the weather itself.

### 787–775: The Agreement of the Open Sluices

Rebuilding forced the Reedbank communities, South Vey estates, and affected towns to decide which channels should remain clear and who would pay for them. Engineers could describe consequences; they could not make displaced households agree about whose land should receive floodwater.

**Surveyor Halda Penn** produced several plans, each naming the properties protected and the ground deliberately left vulnerable. **Reedbank Speaker Omer Vell** insisted those consequences be read aloud before contributions were agreed. The resulting **Open Sluices Agreement** included compensation funds, seasonal inspections, and marked limits on new obstructions.

The agreement worked where its participants maintained the channels and paid their shares. Some outlying districts declined to join. Their decision did not make them villains, but later works required negotiating how their land and water connected to the shared system.

**Traces and stories:** Boundary posts with water-level marks; inspection paths; competing engineering sketches. A present blockage can be investigated through changed land use and later agreements, not just an ancient diagram treated as timeless authority.

**GM truth:** Halda's preferred plan was not the one adopted. She helped implement the compromise because its likely protection was better than another season without coordinated work.

### 774–762: The Basilisk in the Dry Store

Flood repairs exposed an old stone storage complex near a river branch. Labourers entered to retrieve sound masonry and found several strangely lifelike figures beneath mineral deposits. A basilisk had taken shelter in the dry inner chambers, using broken drains and reed beds to move outside.

The response was led by **hunter Osric Hale**, **quarry worker Brenna Tal**, and **local practitioner Merev Senn**. They restricted entry, mapped the drains from outside, and prepared screened approaches before attempting to drive the creature into a controlled enclosure. The basilisk killed one hunter and was eventually slain after breaking through a weakened partition.

The surviving party retrieved the victims they could identify. Securing the site and seeking help for those petrified took longer than the celebrated hunt. The availability of an appropriate remedy varied by victim's condition and the expertise the communities could obtain.

**Traces and stories:** Screen fittings; an expense roll for specialist assistance; a drain plan. A stone figure recovered from later river work may be a carved statue or a petrified victim; the distinction requires investigation.

**GM truth:** The creature was an actual basilisk, not a disguised guardian or punishment for salvage. Its death is established. The storage complex has a separate human history, and any further occupants must arise from later events.

### 761–749: The Bargain at the Bedside

**Margrave Daven Halren** of South Vey spent his final illness trying to arrange a succession acceptable to his household, landholders, and towns. The Seven-Month War lay centuries behind him, but later claims about service and revenue still drew on its settlements.

His daughter **Alessa Halren** secured recognition by accepting a standing assembly of specified estate and town representatives. She became margravine; South Vey remained a distinct polity rather than being absorbed into Highmead or Reedbank. The assembly's membership excluded many people who supplied its revenue, a limitation that would matter to later history.

Daven's final public message praised unity. Private notes record argument over two mills, a disputed dowry, and the cost of his funeral. Those details reveal how power changed hands without requiring the ceremony to have been entirely false.

**Traces and stories:** Different drafts of the succession terms; funeral purchases; a hall remodelled to accommodate an assembly. An alleged original clause may actually be an unadopted proposal with signatures acknowledging receipt rather than agreement.

**GM truth:** Alessa was a capable negotiator who genuinely intended to observe the settlement. Its imperfections arose from unequal representation and competing interests, not a hidden magical loophole.

### 748–736: The Saint with Three Birthplaces

Pilgrimage accounts began placing the birth of Sister Meret, founder of the House of Returning Names, in three different towns. Each had a local tradition, an accommodating inn trade, and an object presented as evidence. The disagreement concerned a woman who had lived more than a millennium earlier.

**Travelling scholar Anwen Dorr** compared the stories and concluded that none established a certain birthplace. One town had preserved the history of an early donor; another had confused the founder with a later namesake; the third had the oldest claim but little corroboration.

Saint Meret's Rest declined to certify a winner. Its keepers invited all three towns to contribute copies of their records and support the work of identifying forgotten people. Some pilgrims welcomed that response. Others preferred a definite destination and bought certainty from less careful guides.

**Traces and stories:** Rival guidebooks; reused saint images; donor records. A contested relic can lead to a useful local history even if it does not belong to the famous founder.

**GM truth:** This chapter does not establish Meret's actual birthplace. Uncertainty is the result of lost evidence, not a clue that she was secretly three people or an immortal travelling under different names.

### 735–723: The Night Market at Ash Salt

South of Nacre Quay, at a lesser inlet called **Ash Salt**, traders began holding an evening market timed to local tides and the arrival of fishing boats. Smoked fish, lamp oil, coarse salt, repaired nets, and cheap hot food provided most of its business.

**Market keeper Nima Voss** established covered weighing tables and hired independent witnesses for bulk sales. The market attracted small sellers who found the main ports' fees difficult to afford. It also attracted smugglers, stolen goods, and officials eager to collect new revenue.

A short confrontation ended with Ash Salt paying specified dues while retaining simpler stall charges. It remained a small inlet market dependent on access and weather, not a shadow capital controlling all coastal crime.

**Traces and stories:** Oil stains in old lamp niches; tide-linked market calendars; fish-bone deposits beneath later stalls. A cargo said to have vanished at sea may have been sold in small lots through accounts that use local nicknames.

**GM truth:** Nima knowingly protected some unlicensed sellers from excessive collection, but she expelled a profitable broker after discovering that his cheap equipment came from thefts against working crews.

### 722–710: The Wedding at the Empty Orchard

Harth's Guest woke briefly during preparations for a wedding near its root ground. It recognised the feast layout and assumed an old obligation was being renewed. The couple, **Ressa Vale** and **Toman Brill**, had intended a family celebration, not a new bargain for frost protection.

The misunderstanding was resolved by **keeper Jora Nall**, who explained the changed customs and asked the Guest whether it wished to attend simply as a neighbour. It did. Witnesses recorded that it listened to the music, accepted an unfamiliar dish, and fell asleep beneath a young tree before the final dance.

For a few mornings afterward, blossoms on that tree carried frost without damage. No wider protection was promised. The wedding became famous enough that later families tried to reproduce its exact meal in hope of attracting the same guest.

**Traces and stories:** A marriage account with an unusual additional guest; competing recipes; a nursery lineage named Wedding Morning. A future festival can require clarifying what hospitality offers and what nobody has consented to owe.

**GM truth:** The brief shelter was a freely given local gift. The menu did not summon the spirit, and repeating the wedding does not compel another awakening. Its later sleep remains compatible with present-day uncertainty about when it will appear.

### 709–700: The Workroom of Mended Things

At Willowrace, flood damage and years of reduced incomes had made replacement tools expensive. **Repairer Sera Wren** organised a shared workroom where residents could borrow space and certain tools while paying skilled workers for tasks they could not safely complete themselves.

Whitewindow craftspeople offered instruction during visits. Ysra, passing through after further northern journeys, repaired a scent charm on a linen keepsake and explained why she could not restore its vanished original scent. The owner chose to record her present kitchen instead.

The workroom persisted because people found it useful, though disputes over broken tools and unpaid work never vanished. It became known as the **House of Second Use**, a name later adopted by unrelated repair houses in other districts.

**Traces and stories:** Tool registers; objects bearing several repair marks; a bench carved with apprentices' measurements. Following repairs across an object's life can identify its travels more reliably than a collector's claim that it remained untouched in one noble household.

**GM truth:** The house did not make all repairs free. Its arrangements balanced mutual help with the need for skilled workers to earn a living. Later charitable legends sometimes erase that essential part of its success.

## Third century: The Price of Old Stories — 699–600 years ago

### 699–688: The Court of Seven Roads

Highmead's **King Neral Merrow** convened representatives from seven established road districts to address escorts, duplicate collections, and recurring disputes over maintenance. The number referred to the delegations at that meeting, not every important road in the kingdom.

The resulting **Court of Seven Roads** heard a limited category of disputes through travelling sessions. It did not replace local courts or govern the whole of Caldris. Its early usefulness depended on access to testimony from carriers, hosts, and people maintaining the roads themselves.

**Road advocate Pellin Rusk** insisted that hearings sometimes take place at affected crossings rather than exclusively in comfortable town halls. The practice made sessions slower and more expensive, while exposing practical facts missing from formal maps.

**Traces and stories:** Travelling hearing calendars; roadside benches; testimony containing weather and repair details. A case preserved only as a judgment may become understandable when its field notes are located elsewhere.

**GM truth:** One district routinely sent a wealthy landholder to speak for everyone. The court's creation improved some remedies without establishing equal representation. Its later history can include reform, decline, or replacement.

### 687–675: The Glassmaker's Daughter and the False Relics

Collectors were paying high prices for anything plausibly connected to Aveline. In Istravel, **glassworker Yamina Rell** found that an ordinary marked reference blank from an old workshop collection had been advertised as Aveline's last enchanted mirror.

Comparison with Fenn Arul's copied parcel research identified the kind of object accurately. It might be the original reference piece or a workshop successor made to the same pattern; its continuous custody could not be proved. Yamina's account separated the identification of a tool from certainty about one particular object's history.

The controversy exposed several other false relics, but not every seller was knowingly dishonest. Some had inherited confident descriptions and never examined them. A collector who lost prestige eventually funded the copying of actual workshop notes, which proved more useful than the supposed mirror.

**Traces and stories:** Conflicting sale descriptions; calibration marks; copied preparation corrections. A present quest can establish whether an object works and what it was used for without guaranteeing that a famous person touched it.

**GM truth:** The reference piece has no concealed power awaiting the correct bloodline. The meaningful inheritance is transferable knowledge. Aveline's personal letter remains a separate, incompletely traced part of the dispersed parcel.

### 674–662: The Lord Beneath the Rushes

Boat crews working a neglected reed channel reported attacks by a long, low creature that could pull an unwary person from a landing. Locals called it the **Rush Lord** after seeing reeds and old netting draped across its back.

The creature was an unusually large marsh predator occupying sheltered water near a collapsed fish weir. Stories gave it tribute, subjects, and a treasury. In practice, workers had begun leaving fish scraps to distract it, teaching it to approach occupied landings for food.

**Reed cutter Bellan Ose** and **hunter Ira Thenn** organised safer access, removed the accumulated scraps, and mapped its routes. After repeated attacks continued, a guarded party trapped and killed it at a confined side channel. Reopening the neglected waterway took much longer than the hunt.

**Traces and stories:** Scarred landing posts; a trap frame in a later shed; accounts separating hunting costs from channel clearance. An impressive tooth sold as a royal relic may be authentic animal material with a wholly invented political history.

**GM truth:** The Rush Lord was neither a disguised water deity nor a surviving basilisk from the previous century. It was a distinct animal whose behaviour was worsened by human attempts at appeasement. Its death is established; later marsh dangers have their own causes.

### 661–649: The Duke Who Counted Empty Beds

In an outlying Roven district, **Duke Samel of Reth** claimed that hearth taxes undercounted people living seasonally along the pack routes. His collectors began charging for beds available to travellers as if each represented a permanent household.

The policy made shelter expensive and encouraged hosts to remove bedding before inspections. Winter carriers arrived to find rooms technically empty and practically useless. **Host Talia Penn**, supported by hauliers and several judges, challenged the interpretation through the district's own agreements.

The settlement distinguished seasonal lodging from permanent residence and established a smaller, visible contribution toward road support. Reth remained a northern jurisdiction outside Highmead's direct authority; the Court of Seven Roads could provide examples and witnesses, not dictate the outcome.

**Traces and stories:** Bed inventories; removable sleeping platforms; a satirical count of beds occupied by cats. A modern dispute may repeat the underlying problem under different rules without treating this ancient settlement as universally binding.

**GM truth:** Samel faced real defence and maintenance costs. His solution shifted them badly and created harmful incentives. Opposing the tax did not eliminate the need to support the road.

### 648–636: The Library in the Laundry Loft

A leak threatened a neglected part of the collection at Saint Meret's Rest. **Laundry keeper Erena Vask** offered dry space above a heated workroom while repairs were arranged. She and the copyists discovered that some books shelved as duplicate accounts contained household letters, route annotations, and missing-name notices unique to those copies.

The emergency led to a slow review of the collection. Paper and parchment required different care, and some damage could not be reversed. The keepers recorded losses instead of claiming complete restoration. Copies of important fragments went to Sallowmere and several regional houses.

Among the findings was a partial transcription of Aveline's parcel letter preserved in later correspondence. It apologised for dismissing a workshop assistant's criticism and credited a practical correction. The assistant's complete name was lost at a torn edge.

**Traces and stories:** Drying arrangements described in accounts; fragments with linked inventory numbers; an apology quoted without its full addressee. Identifying the assistant can restore credit to someone overshadowed by a celebrated traveller.

**GM truth:** The fragment supplies real content without recovering the entire letter or proving that every later attribution is correct. Erena was not secretly a magical scholar; her knowledge of damp, heat, cloth, and workspaces helped preserve the scholarship of others.

### 635–623: The Year the Music Stayed Home

At about 680 years old, Ysra stopped undertaking regular long journeys. She chose a modest house near Seln's musicians, where she could tend propagated plants, receive visitors, and organise notebooks accumulated across centuries.

She still made short journeys when her health and the weather allowed, and remained interested in news beyond the valley. Retirement was a change in scale, not a disappearance. Local children learned tunes that had crossed several regions in Jalen's and her hands, often preferring newer songs she had not learned yet.

**Apprentice Evin Ulan** helped compare the notebooks with growers' current knowledge. Ysra crossed out some of her own conclusions. A lifetime of travel had given her many observations, not immunity to error.

**Traces and stories:** Corrected notebooks; letters declining invitations; an instrument inventory showing extensive replacement parts. A later collection can preserve her working uncertainty instead of presenting every remark as ancient authority.

**GM truth:** Ysra did not take students in search of a chosen successor. Several people learned different parts of her work. Her eventual death is not set in this chapter, and nothing here establishes that she survives into the campaign present.

### 622–610: The Mile of Little Bridges

Repeated washouts on the approach to Goat's Ledger made carriers propose one expensive raised causeway. **Engineer Dova Tal** instead designed a sequence of short bridges and firm stretches that allowed water to pass through the low ground.

The work used stone, timber, and local knowledge of seasonal channels. Its maintenance could be divided among several households and businesses, but each small crossing still needed inspection. A neglected span could interrupt the entire route.

The finished **Mile of Little Bridges** became a familiar landmark. Travellers invented a custom of counting the bridges and making a wish at the last one. Counts differed after later alterations, leading to arguments among people certain their childhood version was correct.

**Traces and stories:** Short abutments partly buried in marsh grass; inspection tallies; songs with different bridge counts. Restoring one section can require recognising a deliberately open watercourse rather than filling every apparent gap in the road.

**GM truth:** The crossing was not sustained by an ancient spell. Its elegance lay in accommodating the landscape. A magical shortcut that ignored drainage could damage precisely what made the route useful.

### 609–600: The Festival of Unfinished Journeys

Saint Meret's Rest hosted a gathering combining unresolved-name records, route corrections, music, and accounts from people who had changed their destination. Organisers called it the **Festival of Unfinished Journeys**, intending to honour lives that did not follow the plans preserved in their first letters.

Families brought stories of return, settlement elsewhere, estrangement, reconciliation, and uncertainty. Some contributions were joyful. Others were too private for the public readings and were retained only with permission in appropriate records.

Ysra sent corrected botanical notes and a short message from Seln rather than travelling. She was about 710 years old by the end of this period. The message asked the keepers to credit Jalen for several songs filed under her name. The musicians performing them had their own arrangements and were credited too.

**Traces and stories:** A programme separating public performances from private inquiries; route books marked with new destinations; a letter refusing sole authorship. A future discovery can connect a family's apparently unfinished journey to a fulfilling life elsewhere.

**GM truth:** The festival did not close every case or reconcile every family. Some people did not want to be found. Its keepers distinguished a public historical puzzle from a living person's right to choose their relationships.

## Places to add to the world

Aunholt, Seln, Goat's Ledger, and Reth widen the northern setting beyond the familiar Roven approaches. They have their own westward and regional connections; the Chalklands are not the centre of every local map. Seven Scripts Court lies within Namar Vale in Selvar. Doorhaven and the flood works belong to the Vey basin, while Ash Salt is a lesser coastal inlet south of Nacre Quay.

Present-day forms below are working destinations for future adventures. Their later governments and institutions can change during the remaining centuries. Ancient human characters are not assumed alive, and old buildings or records survive through repair, copying, reuse, or rediscovery rather than remaining untouched.

### Aunholt — the valley with its own map

**Ancient role:** A high northern valley encountered by Ysra's expedition, already connected to western neighbours through trade and seasonal movement.

**Present-day form:** Hamlets, orchards, pasture, and irrigation channels occupy several sheltered terraces. The communal assembly roof has been rebuilt more than once, incorporating older supports where sound. Local maps record grazing turns, water rights, and winter shelters more thoroughly than distant commercial maps.

**A person to meet:** **Assembly recorder Lethan Ulan** welcomes useful information from visitors and expects their maps to acknowledge local knowledge. He is documenting a side channel before an elder who maintains it can no longer make the journey.

**Adventure:** A visiting expedition's inherited map directs it to a protected collecting ground. Resolving the conflict can lead to a shared survey and an alternative site, while an old annotation reveals why the original patron misnamed the valley.

### Seln — the village that listens before it plays

**Ancient role:** A woodland community producing instrument timber and music in partnership with the acoustics of a particular grove and its small Listener spirit.

**Present-day form:** Workshops stand among managed woodland. The original hollow trunks have died or changed; younger trees and carefully maintained structures preserve parts of the soundscape. The Listener's presence is intermittent and tied to the place, not guaranteed at every performance.

**A person to meet:** **Instrument maker Anra Rill** is testing how new timber responds to a changing windbreak. She wants listeners to hear the living grove instead of demanding a perfect repetition of a centuries-old description.

**Adventure:** Removing a diseased tree will alter the grove's tones. A repair plan can protect people, preserve useful timber, and help the community adapt its music without pretending the dead tree can remain forever.

### The Listening House — Ysra's small retirement home

**Ancient role:** A house near Seln where Ysra organised her records, taught selected skills, and continued correspondence after reducing long-distance travel.

**Present-day form:** A much-repaired house used for visiting researchers and musicians. Some notebooks belong to other collections; the local inventory records where known copies went. Its garden contains descendants of propagated plants, subject to documented losses and replacements. Ysra is not assumed to live here in the present.

**A person to meet:** **House keeper Mira Vell** can arrange access to local copies and dislikes confident claims based on a single unattributed quotation. She is searching for a missing inventory page rather than a hidden immortal resident.

**Adventure:** Comparing a damaged notebook with local nursery practice identifies a correction omitted from later editions. The result can help restore a modest charm or avert a harmful collecting expedition.

### Goat's Ledger — the town where carts stop

**Ancient role:** A transfer settlement on the southern approach to Aunholt, connecting wagon roads with a managed pack route.

**Present-day form:** Warehouses, animal yards, repair sheds, and lodging cluster around unloading grounds. Its wealth depends on reliable handling rather than ownership of every cargo. Marks on older warehouse beams record different generations of carrying partnerships.

**A person to meet:** **Loadmaster Dessa Oss** is skilled at balancing fragile cargo and impatient with clients who conceal its weight. She wants to preserve space for small carriers as a large partnership buys adjoining yards.

**Adventure:** A damaged consignment may have been packed incorrectly far away, altered at transfer, or harmed by an unavoidable route failure. Establishing the sequence can save an honest carrier from a ruinous claim.

### The Mile of Little Bridges — the road that lets water pass

**Ancient role:** A series of short crossings and firm road sections built to replace repeated washout repairs near Goat's Ledger.

**Present-day form:** The route has a different count of spans after centuries of channel movement, rebuilding, and diversion. Some ancient abutments stand beside active crossings; others lie in grass away from the modern road. Its maintenance remains local and visible.

**A person to meet:** **Crossing inspector Rovan Tal** keeps sketches of how water moves during different seasons. He wants one fashionable restoration proposal changed before it blocks a relief channel.

**Adventure:** Survey the route during a narrow weather window and identify which opening must remain clear. Historical plans and present observations each supply part of the answer.

### Seven Scripts Court — the workrooms behind the market sign

**Ancient role:** A shared interpretation and translation court within Namar Vale, preserving explanations of trade terms and later records relevant to Aveline's parcel.

**Present-day form:** Small workrooms around a courtyard, occupied by translators, copyists, and several unrelated businesses. The old name survives despite changes in its languages and organisation. Its oldest records are mostly later copies with identifiable chains of transmission.

**A person to meet:** **Translator Sera Mir** knows the local trade vocabulary and is tracing how one workshop term changed meaning. She charges for her labour while offering fair access to material held for public use.

**Adventure:** A supposed magical instruction is mistranslated because an ordinary workshop term resembles a sacred title. Comparing the preparation notes with actual glassworking can resolve the error and identify a copied fragment of Aveline's corrections.

### Doorhaven — the settlement built above the flood

**Ancient role:** An emergency camp on firm ground during the Spring of Floating Doors, later occupied by households unable or unwilling to return to their former homes.

**Present-day form:** A small riverland settlement whose oldest lanes follow the original raised camp areas. Houses contain reused materials from several places. Flood preparedness is a practical community concern, and door carvings preserve stories whose details are not always reliable.

**A person to meet:** **Boat keeper Enna Rusk** maintains a shared evacuation boat and wants new residents included in the local plan. She has found a door mark matching a family record from far upriver.

**Adventure:** Tracing the door can identify a displaced household or clarify ownership of recovered objects. The search also exposes a neglected route needed for present flood access.

### Penn's Reach — the channel with the measured banks

**Ancient role:** A section of river works associated with Halda Penn's surveys and the Open Sluices Agreement.

**Present-day form:** An inhabited waterside district with inspection paths, channels, farms, and several later sluices replacing earlier arrangements. Only part of the old scheme remains functional. Some land deliberately left open for water has acquired other uses through later decisions.

**A person to meet:** **Waterworks keeper Joren Vell** needs permission to inspect a channel through privately occupied ground. He can explain likely effects but cannot decide who should bear them on his own authority.

**Adventure:** Compare old plans with a recent obstruction and negotiate access before the wet season. Clearing it without understanding downstream occupation could simply transfer the danger.

### Ash Salt — the market that opens with the tide

**Ancient role:** A small southern inlet market serving fishing crews, modest sellers, and carriers outside the main ports' more expensive arrangements.

**Present-day form:** Working stalls, smokehouses, net sheds, and lamps arranged around a tidal landing. The market's busiest hours change with local water conditions. It is a place of honest work, occasional opportunism, and visitors who mistake informality for lawlessness.

**A person to meet:** **Witness keeper Tavi Voss** records bulk sales and can recognise sellers by marks that never appear in formal port books. She wants enough paid assistants to keep the busiest nights properly witnessed.

**Adventure:** Recover the route of a dispersed cargo through small sales. The evidence may distinguish salvage, theft, legitimate resale, and a merchant's attempt to conceal losses from partners.

### Reth Gate — the tollhouse beside the winter rooms

**Ancient role:** A northern district crossing associated with Duke Samel's disputed tax on guest beds and the settlement negotiated by hosts and carriers.

**Present-day form:** A local administrative and lodging cluster in the former Reth jurisdiction. Its current rulers and borders remain for later history to establish. The old tollhouse is partly a shelter, with marks where removable sleeping platforms once fitted.

**A person to meet:** **Host Elna Penn** is comparing lodging obligations across successive charters while trying to keep winter rooms affordable. She has little use for visitors who treat her working house as an unattended historical exhibit.

**Adventure:** A later collector invokes the wrong version of an agreement. Establishing the applicable renewal requires testimony, records, and an understanding of what support the road still needs.

## Longer stories growing from the period

**The name at the torn edge.** Aveline's apologetic letter credits an assistant whose name is incomplete. Sallowmere's fragments, Seven Scripts Court's terminology, and Istravel's workshop accounts can identify the person and the correction. The discovery restores someone to history rather than unlocking a weapon or proving a secret bloodline.

**A map of a place already named.** An expedition hires a party to follow a famous northern map. Local records expose its omissions and mistaken names. Useful cooperation can produce a better route, a fair collecting agreement, and a relationship that outlasts the expedition's publicity.

**The flood door.** A salvaged household door connects Doorhaven to an older riverside home. Following the carving can uncover migration, a family object, and a forgotten evacuation route. Present needs give the historical inquiry practical stakes.

**What the water will do.** Penn's Reach faces a disputed obstruction. Historical agreements explain how earlier people shared costs and harms, while current land use changes the decision. Surveying, testimony, negotiation, and physical repair can all contribute to the outcome.

**The counterfeit that taught something true.** An object falsely advertised as Aveline's relic is still a useful old glassworking tool. Establishing its function uncovers authentic corrections hidden beneath a fraudulent sale story. The owner may lose prestige and gain something more interesting than expected.

**The instrument and the living grove.** Restoring an old fiddle brings travellers to Seln. The community needs help adapting to diseased trees and altered wind. The Listener can contribute a modest wonder, but the answer depends on people deciding how to continue a changing musical tradition.

**The sleeping room levy.** A shelter faces a demand based on an obsolete interpretation of guest beds. Investigating renewals and current services can expose an error or abuse while preserving the contributions genuinely needed to keep the road usable.

**The winter apple.** A surviving northern graft may help growers facing a local problem. The journey requires access, careful propagation, and realistic expectations about soil and storage. It can end with a shared nursery trial rather than an instant miraculous harvest.

**The hunter's unpaid work.** An old account of the basilisk's defeat leads beyond the celebrated kill to victims' treatment, recovery, and identity. A later find can resolve one person's story or reveal what happened to a rescued survivor without undoing the original hunt.

**The invitation declined.** Ysra's late correspondence contains invitations she never accepted and notes about where she would have gone next. A future party can choose one route because it interests them or helps someone living, not because every unfinished thought became an obligation imposed from the past.

## Caldris at 600 years ago

The north is more visible in southern records and remains a collection of lived-in places with its own interests. Goods change carriers at Goat's Ledger; interpreters work in the courtyards of Namar Vale; river communities argue about channels whose maintenance everyone depends upon.

Highmead's crown remains Merrow in this chapter, while South Vey has formalised part of its internal bargaining through an assembly. Neither development settles the political map for the next six centuries. The roads still cross boundaries that maps explain more confidently than travellers experience them.

Aveline has been dead for 810 years. Her parcel's arrival is now historically recoverable, while the paths taken by its letter and working material still offer discoveries. Ysra is elderly and living near Seln, accepting that a smaller journey can still be a worthwhile one. Her eventual fate belongs to the next part of the chronology.

Magic remains rare, local, and consequential. A spirit can attend a wedding without taking responsibility for the weather. A flower can help preserve the scent of a present room without returning a lost childhood. A dangerous creature can be killed, while the work of caring for those it harmed continues afterward.

There are apple trees descended from a meeting between strangers, bridges built to leave room for water, and songs carrying the names of people who repaired them. There are letters whose most important line was an apology.

The next centuries begin with those inheritances still in ordinary hands.
