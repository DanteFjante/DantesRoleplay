# Caldris: The Years of Returning Footsteps

*100–50 years before the campaign present · Authored world lore · Includes GM secrets*

Continues [The Century of Gathering Roads](history-200-100-years-ago.md). Dates count backwards from the campaign present. Present-day people and adventure situations are setting material, not events already activated in the campaign. Explanations labelled **GM truth** are private.

The twelve chronological entries, six mapped locations, historical accounts, and separate GM story material are published in live Caldris. Map anchors illustrate placement within parent locations and do not establish travel distances. Character knowledge and campaign progress are unchanged.

Continues in [The Roads That Lead to Today: 50 years ago to the campaign present](history-50-0-years-ago.md).

## The last peace before the war

At one hundred years ago, people had good reasons to expect another ordinary decade. The previous century had ended with a proposed mobilisation cancelled and useful work funded instead. Roads remained open in many districts, households maintained agreements across political boundaries, and trade gave communities something concrete to lose by fighting.

The established Three Banner War lasted six years, from **89 to 83 years before the present**, ending in **560 AS**. Its causes concerned succession, grain passage, and mutual defence. This chapter adds local experiences and decisions within that framework. It preserves Alderwick's two changes of allegiance, the heavy losses among northern levies, and the winter cooperation already remembered in Caldris.

The three banners were wartime coalitions, not three permanent countries replacing the twelve polities. This history calls them the **Crown Banner**, favouring enforcement of a disputed dynastic settlement; the **River Banner**, organising around passage and toll guarantees; and the **Hearth Banner**, claiming collective protection against coercion. Their names expressed their own arguments. Their members disagreed internally, changed sides, and sometimes pursued interests that contradicted their announced principles.

War did not reach every village in the same way. Some saw soldiers; others saw prices rise, letters stop, or a familiar carrier arrive alone. The following peace likewise reached households at different speeds. A treaty could protect a route before anyone repaired its bridge.

## 99–96: The Spring of Full Inns

Carriers on the routes between the Vey Lowlands and the Lantern Sea reported strong business. Brinefold opened **the Green Awning Yard**, a practical gathering place where small consignments could be combined into affordable shipments. **Freight organiser Mara Kell** reserved spaces for independent carriers alongside larger contracts.

Hosts expanded kitchens, apprentices found work, and several families invested savings in carts. The yard's spring gathering included musicians from Willowrace and cooks carrying recipes learned at Saffron Court. The surviving programme contains more advertisements than speeches about politics.

Later histories sometimes describe this prosperity only as the fragile moment before disaster. Contemporary letters show people enjoying it for its own sake. Their decisions were made without knowledge of the war that would begin seven years after this period closed.

**Traces and stories:** Freight bookings, musicians' programmes, cart purchases, and correspondence between new business partners. A present quest might trace an object first shipped through the yard or recover the agreement behind a family's lost business share.

**GM truth:** Mara promised more covered space than the yard initially possessed. Neighbours supplied temporary awnings in exchange for future bookings. The improvised arrangement worked, but its undocumented first season later made several legitimate claims difficult to prove.

## 95–90: The Inheritance with Two Seals

A disputed succession claim became entangled with rights over revenues pledged to grain carriers and local defence. Highmead clerks examined copies bearing two valid seals from different stages of negotiation. Each side possessed authentic evidence; neither document alone settled all the obligations claimed in its name.

**Envoy Alis Venn** and **clerk Daro Penn** maintained a shared record room that became known as **the House of Paired Seals**. They achieved limited agreements over shipments and temporary administration while a wider settlement remained unresolved. Several households benefited from those arrangements even as political confidence deteriorated.

The failure came through a succession of decisions: payments withheld, escorts reinforced, accusations circulated, and negotiators instructed to concede less. There was no single hidden sentence that forced every later participant to choose war.

**Traces and stories:** Draft settlements, sealed copies, and instructions sent privately to delegates. A present investigation can distinguish a document's authenticity from the authority later claimed for it. Recovering a discarded compromise may reveal an option that leaders knowingly rejected.

**GM truth:** Alis privately accepted a narrower settlement than her employers would announce. Daro preserved that proposal rather than destroying it when talks failed. Its existence can complicate a family legend of uncompromising honour without proving that every participant was secretly insincere.

## 89–88: The Grain Convoys under Three Banners

Open warfare began **89 years before the present** after seizures and armed escort disputes escalated into organised campaigns. The Crown, River, and Hearth coalitions invoked different obligations to justify controlling crossings and grain stores. Alderwick initially supported the Crown Banner, presenting its commitment as enforcement of a lawful settlement.

Northern levies, including troops from Dunmarrow, bore heavy transport and escort duties. **Dwarven quartermaster Nera Coal** recorded carts lost to broken axles, spoiled provisions, sickness, and requisitions by supposedly friendly commanders. Her accounts make it difficult to separate the fighting front from the roads feeding it.

At the temporary **Low Fen Redoubt**, soldiers fortified a storehouse approach with earthworks and commandeered timber. The settlement nearest the works gained customers and lost much of its capacity to decide who could occupy its barns.

**Traces and stories:** Requisition receipts, wagon marks, levy lists, and a road map annotated with repair failures. A present journey can locate a forgotten depot or establish whether goods were stolen, purchased, or taken under a promise of later compensation.

**GM truth:** Nera concealed a small reserve to feed drivers after officers diverted official rations. It was an unauthorised act with a practical purpose. A later accusation of hoarding omits the distribution notes kept in her personal book.

## 87–86: The Siege That Consumed Its Wagons

A prolonged struggle over Low Fen failed to produce the decisive control its commanders promised. Wet ground, camp sickness, poor coordination, and damaged supply roads weakened both besiegers and defenders. Repair timber was burned for fuel; wagons were dismantled to shore up works that would soon be abandoned.

Alderwick shifted from the Crown to the River coalition during this period, citing violated passage guarantees. Some units received orders before others, leaving escorts uncertain which checkpoints would recognise their papers. Several local truces lasted only long enough for carts and wounded people to pass.

**Wheelwright Sera Dast** led a civilian repair group that served customers from more than one side. The group accepted payment and took risks; its members were neither immune to danger nor detached from the effects of their work.

**Traces and stories:** Contradictory orders, patched wagon fittings, and unfinished siege works. A disputed object might carry marks from both armies because it changed owners repeatedly. Reconstructing a local truce can explain why someone crossed a line without necessarily betraying their neighbours.

**GM truth:** One commander knowingly exaggerated the redoubt's remaining supplies to obtain reinforcements. The deception prolonged a failing operation and increased losses. The decisive comparison lies between ration issues and outgoing reports, not a conveniently preserved confession.

## 85–84: The Winter of Common Shovels

Floodwater threatened camps and nearby homes along a lower river channel. Soldiers on opposing sides informally stopped fighting to repair the same bank. **Engineer Hessa Orrel** marked urgent work, while fishers and local labourers supplied knowledge of channels that military maps had simplified.

The repaired stretch became known as **the Shared Bank**. Some officers tolerated the work, some helped, and others later claimed to have ordered what had begun without permission. Cooperation kept people alive without ending the war or erasing what participants had done elsewhere.

Alderwick changed allegiance a second time, joining the Hearth coalition while arguing for an end to the conflict. Its later public memory preferred this change to the first. Elsewhere, the winter produced both the shared shelter remembered at the Courteous Ruin and the caravan loss commemorated by the Empty Saddles. These were separate experiences within the same war, not interchangeable stories of universal goodwill.

**Traces and stories:** Mixed tool marks, repair tallies, local testimony, and orders written after the work began. A present dispute over the bank's care may involve families whose ancestors worked beside people later described only as enemies.

**GM truth:** The established account of the Empty Saddles conceals several survivors who deserted and lived under new names. The Courteous Ruin's caretaker deliberately used childhood nicknames to help officers recognise kinship. Neither secret should become common knowledge merely because this chapter places the incidents within the same winter.

## 83–82: The First Protected Grain Sailing

The war ended **83 years before the present**, in **560 AS**, through the **Lantern Sea Accord**. The settlement established protected grain routes, prisoner exchange, pilgrim rights, and regular diplomatic congresses. No coalition obtained a victory that satisfied all its members.

The first protected sailings required inspection, interpreters, repaired vessels, and people willing to believe that opposing officials would honour unfamiliar seals. **Pilot Edda Noll** carried a mixed group of passengers alongside grain, including released prisoners and household representatives searching for news.

At Brinefold, unloading continued after the ceremonial welcome ended. Damaged scales, disputed cargo shares, and exhausted workers made the practical peace less elegant than its commemorative paintings. Nevertheless, goods arrived and people stepped ashore who would not have been allowed passage a season earlier.

**Traces and stories:** Accord travel papers, inspection marks, passenger lists, and competing cargo claims. A present quest can connect a displaced person to a recorded sailing or establish how a protected route acquired its later local obligations.

**GM truth:** One checkpoint admitted passengers using a provisional form before its final authorisation arrived. The official responsible accepted a personal risk because delay would have stranded vulnerable travellers. The irregular papers are genuine evidence of passage, not automatically forged documents.

## 81–78: The Names House at Rookwater

Returning people encountered homes occupied by relatives, purchasers, tenants, or strangers who had also needed shelter. Those searching for missing family members often possessed only fragments: a nickname, a unit, a debt receipt, or the place where a letter had last been forwarded.

Nera Coal helped establish **the Names House** at Rookwater. **Copyist Mira Renn** kept separate records of confirmed deaths, witnessed departures, uncertain reports, and people found alive. The distinction frustrated officials seeking a final total but prevented guesses from hardening immediately into fact.

The house also mediated the return of tools and work animals. Families needed practical means to live, not only recognition of what they had lost. Some disputes ended in shared use or compensation rather than an impossible restoration of the household as it had been before the war.

**Traces and stories:** Search notices, forwarding records, corrected death reports, and property inventories. A present investigation may discover a survivor's later household or explain why an inheritance was divided unexpectedly. Finding someone alive does not automatically make every later arrangement fraudulent.

**GM truth:** Nera refused to circulate one located deserter's address after receiving credible threats against his new household. A restricted note explains the decision. Whether descendants should learn that history requires attention to the people affected, not just excitement at resolving a mystery.

## 77–74: The Price of Opening a Road Again

Peace reopened legal passage before it restored every bridge, cart, or roadside store. Carriers returning to smaller routes encountered missing culverts, exhausted draught animals, and settlements unable to finance repairs. Some profitable wartime detours remained more usable than the roads officials wanted celebrated as restored.

**Surveyor Ossa Pell** organised a modest circuit comparing records with actual conditions. Hosts at the Winter Latch and Lantern Hospice contributed observations and supplies. Payments were attached to completed work rather than to declarations that a district was open.

Several households relocated beside a surviving branch, leaving empty houses on an older approach. Later visitors mistook the abandonment for destruction during a battle. The surviving accounts describe a slower departure driven by access, cost, and the need to earn a living.

**Traces and stories:** Repair vouchers, competing route maps, and notices advertising houses for sale. A present expedition can distinguish an abandoned settlement from a battlefield and recover useful knowledge about why a road was given up.

**GM truth:** Ossa favoured one branch partly because her sibling's household lived there. The route was also the most repairable within the available budget. The decision deserves examination of both facts rather than automatic vindication or dismissal.

## 73–70: The Bells Returned from Storage

Several small bells requisitioned during the war survived in depots instead of being melted. Returning them required sorting objects whose clappers, brackets, and labels had become separated. **Bell founder Teren Varr** compared marks at the Narrow Bell Foundry with civic records and testimony from people who remembered their sound.

Two communities claimed the same casting. One had paid for it; the other had maintained and used it for years before requisition. The eventual agreement placed it at a shared crossing and funded an ordinary replacement for one village tower.

The first coordinated ringing after the returns became a regional celebration. Some households heard relief; others remembered warnings and departures. Several hosts provided quiet rooms for people who did not want to stand beneath the bells.

**Traces and stories:** Depot inventories, incompatible brackets, and agreements over shared ownership. A present restoration can identify a bell's travels or reveal that a supposedly ancient civic custom began with a post-war compromise.

**GM truth:** Teren could not identify every bell confidently. He marked uncertain conclusions, but a later commemorative booklet omitted those qualifications. An honest uncertainty in the workshop book is more useful than the booklet's neat list of unquestioned returns.

## 69–64: A Small Light in an Unrepaired Room

A damaged gathering room near Seln reopened with borrowed tables and one pane temporarily lent from Sunborrow House. Its stored daylight allowed fine work during short winter afternoons, but the room still needed fuel, a roof repair, and people willing to clean it.

Nivren Sael spent several weeks helping carry materials and teaching a few practical charms. He could not restore what had been lost merely because he was older than the damage. **Host Bera Kett** asked him to explain each charm's limits to the next keeper before leaving.

The pane was eventually returned, as its loan record required. The room continued using ordinary lamps. A later story claimed that Nivren had gifted the village an everlasting sun; the accounts show an exchange of borrowed things, useful labour, and a friendship maintained for several years afterward.

**Traces and stories:** A pane loan, material purchases, and instructions written for inexperienced keepers. A present quest can recover the correct mounting or identify a charm altered during a later repair. Returning a useful object may fulfil a promise while forcing a community to plan for its absence.

**GM truth:** Nivren initially offered a spell too demanding for the person expected to maintain it. Bera asked for something simpler, and he listened. The useful contribution came from adapting to the household rather than demonstrating the most impressive magic available.

## 63–58: The Open String Hall

At Willowrace, **music teacher Elna Dast** and **carpenter Samin Orr** converted an old workroom into **the Open String Hall**. Instruments with missing parts were repaired for lessons, and musicians taught several versions of tunes associated with Ysra and Orven alongside newer local compositions.

The hall welcomed adults whose schooling had been interrupted and children with no personal memory of the war. It charged fees where households could afford them and obtained support for some places. Disagreements concerned rehearsal hours, instrument custody, and whether a patron could reserve the best room indefinitely.

A performance paired an experienced player with a beginner for each piece. The result was uneven, lively, and popular enough to become an occasional tradition. The hall's founders valued music as part of a life worth rebuilding, not simply as decoration at public memorials.

**Traces and stories:** Repair labels, lesson books, and scores marked for paired performers. A present commission could retrieve an instrument, establish who owns a donated collection, or reunite parts of an arrangement carried away by a former pupil.

**GM truth:** Elna and Samin quarrelled over selling a valuable instrument to fund ordinary repairs. They eventually sold it with a documented loan-back arrangement for certain performances. Its later appearance in a private collection is not proof that it was stolen from the hall.

## 57–50: The Congress after the Harvest

Delegates returning for a regular congress under the Lantern Sea Accord encountered a generation of clerks who had learned its procedures through employment rather than wartime experience. **Interpreter Dena Vell** assembled cases showing where protected passage worked and where local charges had quietly narrowed it.

The meeting renewed useful commitments, resolved several practical disputes, and postponed others. A proposed uniform fee failed because it would have burdened poorer crossings disproportionately. Local arrangements continued, requiring records and people willing to challenge a charge that had become habitual.

At fifty years ago, the peace had lasted thirty-three years. Its achievements were substantial: journeys completed, prisoners long returned, children raised without seeing the war. Its maintenance still depended on choices that a signed accord could encourage but never make automatically.

**Traces and stories:** Interpreters' notes, postponed petitions, and travel cases that reveal how broad promises were applied. A present dispute may begin with a fee adopted as a temporary measure whose justification has disappeared. Recovering the original decision can support a new hearing without settling its outcome in advance.

**GM truth:** Dena retained a private comparison of public speeches with negotiated exceptions. It shows compromise and selective presentation, not a single conspiracy controlling every toll. The most useful discovery may be an overlooked precedent that gives a small community a fairer opportunity to be heard.

## Places to add to the world

### The Green Awning Yard — small cargoes at the water's edge

**Present-day form:** A freight assembly yard in Brinefold with repaired awnings, shared weighing space, and places rented by independent carriers. Its earliest arrangements have been replaced or renewed over time. The spring gathering survives intermittently when trade and local organisers can support it.

**A person to meet:** Freight organiser **Pera Kell** needs to establish whether a disputed booking belongs to a working carrier or an intermediary reserving space for resale.

**Adventure:** Trace a consignment, recover a booking record, or help independent carriers assemble a shipment. A box that changed hands here can lead through several households without requiring a world-threatening secret inside it.

### The House of Paired Seals — the room where agreement almost happened

**Present-day form:** A record and meeting house in Highmead containing copies of negotiations before the Three Banner War and later comparative work. Some originals are held elsewhere. Exhibits distinguish signatures, proposed terms, and provisions that were actually adopted.

**A person to meet:** Archivist **Hessa Penn** is preparing a comparison requested by rival claimants who each expect it to vindicate them.

**Adventure:** Locate a missing instruction, compare an authenticated copy with a later renewal, or identify a discarded compromise. Evidence can correct a public story without automatically deciding a present claim.

### Low Fen Redoubt — earthworks beyond the storehouse road

**Present-day form:** Overgrown wartime earthworks in the Vey Lowlands around the foundations of a former depot. Later drainage has altered parts of the site. Local households use nearby grazing while keeping known unstable pits marked; small finds are recorded inconsistently.

**A person to meet:** Surveyor **Mera Dast** wants to distinguish depot remains from later dumping before contractors level a section for access.

**Adventure:** Recover a wagon plate, locate an inventory cache, or trace goods taken under wartime authority. Flooded storage pits and unstable works provide physical danger without requiring undead soldiers to inhabit every battlefield.

### The Shared Bank — the floodwork built across a battle line

**Present-day form:** A maintained riverbank work in the Penn's Reach district, repeatedly repaired since the wartime flood. A small marker records the shared labour, while local maintenance documents identify later alterations. Its present condition depends on inspection and repair.

**A person to meet:** Water keeper **Oren Orrel** needs help assessing a weakened stretch before commemorative visitors arrive. He would rather obtain sound timber than another speech about cooperation.

**Adventure:** Compare old channels with current water, recover a repair agreement, or bring materials across a disputed access path. The memorial's history may help negotiations, but it cannot repair the bank by itself.

### The Names House — finding people after the lists were closed

**Present-day form:** A small search archive and mediation office in Rookwater. Its records distinguish confirmed outcomes from unanswered inquiries. Later household correspondence supplements the post-war material, and restricted notes have custody rules governing their use.

**A person to meet:** **Nera Coal**, the dwarven former quartermaster, remains alive in the campaign present and visits the office as her health permits. She can explain her own records and admits when she did not witness an event. Her age and longevity make her a surviving witness, not an authority on everything that happened.

**Adventure:** Trace a displaced household, correct a mistaken death report, or return a documented possession. A recovered address may affect living descendants whose interests differ from those of the person funding the search.

### The Open String Hall — music for the next pair of hands

**Present-day form:** A community teaching and performance hall in Willowrace with repaired instruments, ordinary rehearsal rooms, and a collection of marked teaching scores. Fees, supported places, and instrument loans are recorded separately. Its paired performances remain a living custom rather than a fixed reconstruction of the founders' first concert.

**A person to meet:** Teacher **Lessa Orr** needs a missing instrument returned before a public performance and wants to resolve its ownership without humiliating a former pupil.

**Adventure:** Follow an instrument's repairs and sale history, recover a loan-back agreement, or bring separated musicians together for a performance. The reward may include friendship, a useful contact, and a tune that travels with the party.

## At 50 years ago

The Three Banner War has been over for thirty-three years. Some surviving adults remember it directly; many younger people know it through households, memorials, and the practical habits it left behind. The Long Reprieve is established, but the current campaign's specific crises are not presumed already underway.

Ysra has been dead for 544 years and Orven for 229. Aurr's death remains settled. Nivren is still eligible to appear in the present, with his location and involvement unset. Nera Coal is a distinct, explicitly surviving dwarven witness whose knowledge follows her work and experience.

The existing stories of the Empty Saddles and the Courteous Ruin retain their private explanations. The Grain Fleet Refusal and other established incidents of the Long Reprieve retain their existing broad dating unless a later chapter deliberately places them more precisely.

The final fifty years can now lead into the people and pressures of the campaign's present: institutional memory fading unevenly, old promises passing through new hands, local wonders maintained or neglected, and travellers arriving at places that have had long lives before the party ever sees them.
