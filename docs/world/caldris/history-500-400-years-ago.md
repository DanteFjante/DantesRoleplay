# Caldris: The Hundred Years of Borrowed Light

*500–400 years before the campaign present · Authored world lore · Includes GM secrets*

Continues [The Century of Open Windows](history-600-500-years-ago.md). Dates count backwards from the campaign present. The opening describes the threshold at five hundred years ago; the dated events begin in the following year. Present-day adventure premises have not happened in the campaign, and historical people are not assumed still alive.

The chronology, six mapped locations, full historical accounts, and separate GM story material are published in live Caldris. Map positions illustrate places within their parent locations; they do not establish travel distances. Character knowledge and campaign progress are unchanged.

Continues in [The Roads of Salt and Rain: 400–300 years ago](history-400-300-years-ago.md).

## A peace with bills to pay

At five hundred years ago, merchants could cross much of the Chalklands without hiring an army, but knowing which coin a ferryman would accept remained a practical survival skill. Highmead's Merrow court shared authority with towns, road courts, landholders, and institutions older than the reigning household. Serevan and Selvar remained regional names on Solasca; their ports and inland markets had ambitions that did not always suit their distant customers.

The previous century had taught people to preserve corrections. This one asked who could afford to act on them. A school might explain a contract perfectly and still be unable to pay its own winter bill. An accurate tide record might save a fishing fleet while proving that a profitable quay should never have been built.

Magic remained uncommon, particular, and troublesome to inherit. The people repairing an enchanted building often knew more about its roof than its enchantment. Long-lived travellers encountered inns named after friends whose grandchildren had already died. Most journeys concerned wool, salt, meals, letters, or getting home before snow. Occasionally something old followed a traveller home.

## 499–492: The Ferry That Charged Six Pennies

At Penn's Reach, the widow **Merren Dole** advertised a crossing for six pennies. Travellers arrived carrying coins from different towns, some clipped, some debased, and some perfectly honest but lighter than her reference issue. Arguments delayed the ferry until loaded carts stood through the rain.

Merren and an evening pupil from the Twelve Windows produced a painted conversion board. Carriers welcomed predictable charges; the money changer who supplied their silver challenged the board's rates. A road hearing established a weight-based reference and a small separately stated exchange fee. Prices became easier to compare, although poorer travellers still paid disproportionately for changing tiny sums.

The expression **six pennies, whose pennies?** entered bargaining speech. Children played ferryman with potsherds, solemnly rejecting stones from the wrong side of the garden.

**Traces and stories:** Overpainted rate boards, ferry tokens, and the pupil's arithmetic exercises survive in several collections. A present dispute could hinge on whether a quoted price included exchange charges. An heirloom token might also redeem a crossing promised by an institution that still honours successor obligations.

**GM truth:** Merren initially rounded conversions in her own favour to cover losses she could not document. The pupil, **Tessa Kell**, discovered this and insisted on a published fee instead. Neither the later saintly ferryman tale nor the story of a wholly fraudulent ferry is accurate.

## 491–484: The Guest Who Returned to the Copper Kettle

The elven traveller **Nivren Sael** returned to an orchard-road inn after seventy-three years, carrying a blue cup he had borrowed from its former keeper. He expected a familiar kitchen. The keeper's great-granddaughter, **Rella Morn**, showed him the burial register and asked whether he would like supper before visiting the graves.

Nivren stayed through the pear harvest. He remembered the old keeper's voice but had forgotten the name of the woman who had cooked beside her for thirty years. Rella found that name in household accounts. In return, Nivren taught the family a small drying charm useful for damp boots, a spell he had once considered too trivial to write down.

The inn adopted the name **the Copper Kettle** after the battered vessel Nivren recognised. The blue cup entered everyday use. When it finally broke, its pieces became a mosaic beside the hearth.

**Traces and stories:** A register with an elven annotation, several versions of the boot charm, and blue pottery incorporated into later hearths. A traveller seeking Nivren can follow places that preserve something useful he taught. Recovering a missing section of the charm might matter more to a flooded village than finding his impressive battle magic.

**GM truth:** Nivren is eligible to survive into the campaign present, but his whereabouts and involvement remain unset. He was not the keeper's secret lover. They had been friends, and he had underestimated how much that friendship mattered until returning became impossible in the way he remembered.

## 483–476: The Winter of Borrowed Sunlight

In Seln, a succession of dark winters led the glassworker **Iven Sorr** and the mage **Dara Venn** to refurbish an abandoned teaching house. Dara fixed a modest enchantment into three copper-backed panes: each could release a short interval of daylight gathered during brighter weather. The light carried colour and ordinary illumination, but supplied neither meaningful warmth nor a growing season for crops.

People brought mending, accounts, and fine instrument work to **Sunborrow House**. Children watched a patch of summer brightness cross the floor while snow pressed against the shutters. The rooms still needed fuel, and the keeper charged wealthier users for reserved hours to subsidise the common tables.

An overcast autumn left too little stored light. The house shortened its sessions and bought more lamp oil. Its surviving accounts record wonder and expense on adjacent lines.

**Traces and stories:** Pane diagrams, illumination schedules, and requests for subsidised seats. A surviving pane may need exposure at a particular old gathering frame before it can shine again. Finding that frame could lead through a ruined roof garden rather than a wizard's tomb.

**GM truth:** The panes preserve illumination, not the moments in which it was gathered. They cannot show the dead. One pane contains a flaw that slowly wastes its charge; recognising the defect can prevent a patron from spending a fortune on the wrong repair.

## 475–468: The Hollow Mint Affair

Highmead's mint paid for silver it had not yet received, expecting an overdue shipment to cover its commitments. When the shipment failed, officials reduced the silver in a run of small coins. Court suppliers accepted them at their stamped value; northern wool sellers demanded more. The difference spread through markets faster than any official explanation.

**Assayer Petren Vale** and **Queen Elsent Merrow's** treasurer, **Hessa Dorn**, arranged a limited exchange funded by postponed court works and negotiated losses among creditors. Owners of large accounts recovered more reliably than servants paid in small coin. Petren preserved rejected petitions beside the official settlement.

The old assay building acquired the name **the Hollow Mint** after a popular accusation that its furnaces melted promises. Its surviving wings later housed licensed weighers and repair shops.

**Traces and stories:** Coins of the same face with different silver content, rejected wage petitions, and cancelled building contracts. A hoard can be valuable as evidence of how wages were paid even when its metal disappoints treasure hunters. Counterfeiters may exploit collectors' confusion between genuine bad coin and modern imitation.

**GM truth:** Hessa authorised the debasement to keep transport and provisioning contracts functioning, then concealed its scale. Petren exposed the assay results but accepted a settlement that left poor claimants waiting. Both accomplished something useful and left people with good reason to resent them.

## 467–460: The Breach at Brinefold

**Brinefold**, a salt-working harbour in the Vey Lowlands, expanded across an old tidal channel. During a severe storm, seawater entered behind the new embankment and trapped households between the quay and the inland road. **Pilot Oda Fen** guided shallow boats through lanes normally used by carts.

Witnesses reported a vast, pale-backed animal lying across the harbour mouth while the boats escaped. It broke part of the outer quay and withdrew before morning. Brinefold rebuilt farther inland, abandoning the lowest streets. Salt production resumed at a smaller scale, and families argued for decades over who should bear the loss of leased pans beneath the water.

Oda refused a statue until the rescue crews were named. The eventual monument lists boat owners, rowers, rope handlers, and three people identified only by their work.

**Traces and stories:** Submerged doorways, salt leases, tide records, and the names missing from the rescue monument. At exceptionally low water, an expedition can enter the old counting house before the tide closes its streets. Recovering household goods may conflict with a company's claim to everything on its drowned lease.

**GM truth:** The animal was the **Pale Breaker**, a rare coastal creature that sought a sheltered spawning channel blocked by the quay. Its actions helped the evacuation without being undertaken for human salvation. One deep chamber beneath the drowned quay may still attract its descendants during particular tides; their presence is an encounter possibility, not an activated event.

## 459–452: The Bridge of Three Versions

A new timber crossing near Willowrace connected workshops to grazing and reed beds previously reached by a seasonal ford. Three work parties raised funds with different versions of a tune associated with Ysra. At the opening, their musicians discovered that the verses no longer fitted together.

The dwarven instrument maker **Orven Dast**, then aged 163, arranged an alternating performance. He retained each version and wrote transitions instead of declaring a single authentic melody. The crossing became **Three-Reed Bridge**, after three reed pipes played at the dedication. Its maintenance chest received a share of the annual music fair's takings.

Later singers called the arrangement Ysra's last composition, although she had been dead for more than a century when the bridge opened. Orven's surviving score identifies his own work clearly.

**Traces and stories:** Three regional songbooks, bridge subscriptions, and a score that travelled with an instrument maker's family. Recovering the transition pages can restore a performance while also identifying which community originally contributed to a disputed maintenance fund.

**GM truth:** One work party wanted its rivals' verses removed because they named an unpopular labour organiser. Orven preserved them deliberately. No verse contains a magical command; its dangerous power is the possibility that someone may understand the words and ask who was excluded.

## 451–444: The Reply Kept in the Wrong Ledger

At the Quiet Archive in Leth Sarai, **copyist Samin Rel** compared an employee list with glass preparation notes previously indexed by Mara Sef. A folded reply, kept among accounts for polishing sand, supplied the missing corroboration: **Leris Amat** had received Aveline's apology.

The reply acknowledged the apology and described the practical harm caused when Leris's work went uncredited. It contained no assurance that the matter was entirely forgiven. Samin published a careful transcription with the relevant dates and workshop roles, resolving the addressee's identity while leaving some damaged phrases uncertain.

Aveline's admirers did not agree on what to do with the discovery. Some amended display labels. Others preferred a sentimental paraphrase. The archive kept both the transcription and the original folds visible in its catalogue.

**Traces and stories:** Later copies establish the reply's existence even if the original has since moved. A quest can recover the damaged wording, trace ownership, or restore credit in a current exhibition. Discovering the recipient's name is now established history rather than an endlessly postponed mystery.

**GM truth:** The reply is authentic. Its lost passage concerns payment owed to a second assistant, not a hidden romance or a spell. The assistant's identity and whether that payment was ever made remain unresolved. Leris's refusal to offer easy absolution was deliberate.

## 443–436: The Succession of Black Ribbons

Queen Elsent Merrow's death exposed incompatible promises about road revenues. One claimant had pledged reductions to carriers; another offered to preserve income owed to shelters and bridge trusts. Town delegates tied black ribbons to their account chests and refused new advances until an interim settlement existed.

After months of pressure, **Maera Merrow**, a collateral heir, accepted a negotiated crown with a published separation between household spending and specified road funds. A rival retained estates and a place at court. Several outlying communities received no delegate and did not regard the bargain as their consent.

There were arrests, a warehouse fire, and one fatal confrontation at a toll gate. Later chronicles described a bloodless succession because no field army fought a battle. The gatekeeper's family kept a different account.

**Traces and stories:** Revenue assignments, ribbon-fastened chests, rival genealogies, and the toll victim's burial entry. A later lawsuit might depend on which obligations passed to successor governments. An old claim alone does not overturn modern law, but renewals and later recognitions may keep part of it effective.

**GM truth:** The warehouse fire destroyed collateral records for loans held by supporters of both claimants. Its organiser was a grain factor protecting an insolvent partnership, not either heir. Evidence could expose a commercial dynasty whose founders profited from the political confusion.

## 435–428: The Summer the Wards Went Thin

Unusually warm weather coincided with failures in old enchantments along the Veyr approaches. A shelter's weather charm faded; a locked storehouse opened at night; a preserved wooden sluice began to soften. Tiny translucent moths gathered near the affected structures, visible most clearly against lamplight.

**Ward keeper Pell Arven** and travelling mage **Sorrel Dain** identified a swarm feeding on certain slowly decaying enchantments. At Crowstep they lured much of it into a sacrificial charm fixed to a clay vessel, then vitrified the vessel in a potter's kiln. Workers meanwhile braced the failing buildings by ordinary means.

The **glass-moth summer** changed how local keepers documented inherited magic. They recorded mundane alternatives alongside renewal instructions. Sorrel lost a treasured self-mending cloak while drawing moths away from a refuge full of travellers.

**Traces and stories:** Kiln waste, emergency timberwork, damaged charms, and lists of structures inspected after the swarm. A present expedition might need to identify a vulnerable ward before a small surviving brood reaches it. Safe containment requires learning the moths' preferences rather than bringing ever stronger enchantments into reach.

**GM truth:** The moths were real and dangerous, but they could not devour every kind of magic. They preferred one family of persistent bindings associated with particular mineral pigments. A second sealed vessel was sent to an unnamed coastal collector; locating it remains a potential quest. Its opening has not already happened.

## 427–420: The Agreement on the Violet Steps

Changes to the Penn's Reach channels prompted fishers and sluice keepers to consult old records of the Siltwife. Several cautious observers again saw a pale creature at a stone landing stained purple by mineral seepage. **Water keeper Adra Uld** tested small changes to obstructions while recording the creature's responses.

The resulting **Violet Steps Agreement** bound the human households to keep one passage clear, limit certain traps, and share maintenance. The creature accepted space and occasionally cleared silt, but never signed, spoke an oath, or appeared at a hearing. Adra insisted that the agreement describe human promises without pretending to own another being's future behaviour.

For several decades the landing became a place to settle practical water disputes. Children left smooth stones there. Keepers removed elaborate offerings when they obstructed the channel.

**Traces and stories:** Water-level marks, trap diagrams, and clauses renewed under later authorities. A current dispute could require proving where the protected passage moved after a flood. Cooperation with the creature is possible, but restoring a familiar arrangement may work better than demanding obedience to an old document.

**GM truth:** This is the same Siltwife encountered in the previous century. Its continued presence is plausible because of its long life, not because every pale river animal is one spirit. It recognises particular water conditions and repeated conduct more readily than human surnames.

## 419–412: The Letter Delivered After the Funeral

The carrier **Jori Pell** brought an elderly gardener's invitation from Istravel to a former apprentice at Windowmere. Storm delays and illness lengthened the journey. The apprentice had died eleven days before Jori arrived.

The apprentice's widower asked Jori to stay for the first harvest from a tree the two gardeners had once disputed how to graft. He sent back fruit preserves, a practical account of the successful method, and a letter of his own. Jori carried that reply on a paid return journey; kindness did not make boats or meals free.


Later retellings invented a race against death and a miraculous final meeting. The surviving exchange describes a missed chance, grief, gratitude, and someone choosing to write back even though the intended conversation could no longer happen.

**Traces and stories:** A route ledger, jars bearing reused labels, and two letters kept in separate garden collections. A present delivery can reunite their histories. Choosing what to say to a recipient who has inherited a stranger's unfinished friendship may be the heart of the quest.

**GM truth:** The first letter sat for twelve days in an intermediate counting house because its postage account was disputed. The clerk responsible later paid part of the return fare anonymously. Revealing that delay can change a family's understanding without making anyone capable of undoing the death.

## 411–400: The Second Bell of Brinefold

Brinefold's rebuilt harbour developed a subscription chest to support damaged boats and bereaved households. **Salt factor Yara Senn** supplied starting capital; crews supplied regular shares and appointed readers to examine payouts. The first bell announced departures. A second bell called a hearing when losses exceeded what the chest could cover.


The arrangement survived an early failure by reducing payments, postponing some claims, and recording debts openly. Unregistered shore workers initially received nothing. Their protests won a separate contribution and benefit scheme, smaller than the crews' but capable of expansion.

By four hundred years ago, merchants carried copies of Brinefold's terms to other ports. Some copied its safeguards; others copied only its fees. The Second Bell became a hopeful emblem whose sound meant that a community had difficult accounts to hear together.

**Traces and stories:** Contribution books, claim hearings, bell founders' marks, and competing later copies of the scheme. An adventuring group might investigate whether a lost vessel was insured, retrieve evidence of its cargo, or discover that an apparently generous fund excludes the very workers who need it.

**GM truth:** Yara's initial donation came partly from profits on leases abandoned after the old flood. She supported a useful institution while refusing to reopen every claim against her family's earlier conduct. Her descendants can inherit assets and responsibilities without inheriting personal guilt.

## Places to add to the world

### The Copper Kettle — the inn with a borrowed cup

**Present-day form:** A modest inn near Boughmeet's orchard road, rebuilt around successive kitchens. Blue pottery fragments form a small hearth mosaic, with replacement pieces documented by later keepers. A working copy of Nivren's boot-drying charm hangs beside the wet-weather bench. Most visitors come for food and shelter rather than the old story.

**A person to meet:** Keeper **Dessa Morn** inherited the business through a sale within an extended family. She is tired of visitors assuming that an old surname proves an unbroken bloodline or an obligation to give free meals.

**Adventure:** A travelling collector offers enough money to repair the roof in exchange for the mosaic. Establish which pieces came from the cup, negotiate a less destructive arrangement, or find another means to fund repairs. Nivren's possible opinion does not replace the living keeper's choices.

### Sunborrow House — daylight for a winter table

**Present-day form:** A communal workroom in Seln with two surviving enchanted panes, one unreliable, and an ordinary replacement where the third once stood. Its summer gathering frame was moved during a later rebuilding. Keepers maintain both fuel stores and an illumination rota.

**A person to meet:** Glass repairer **Maelin Sorr** understands the panes' physical mounting but needs help interpreting Dara's gathering diagrams. Her greatest worry is breaking a useful object while trying to restore its reputation.

**Adventure:** Trace the displaced gathering frame, obtain compatible copper backing, and choose between restoring one pane or stabilising both at lower capacity. A donor wants a splendid private room; the keepers want longer common-table hours.

### The Hollow Mint — an assay house full of promises

**Present-day form:** Former mint buildings in Highmead's old commercial quarter, now divided among licensed assayers, metalworkers, and small shops. The original furnaces survive only below one protected floor. The public weighing counter uses renewed standards rather than ancient coins as unquestioned authority.

**A person to meet:** Assayer **Bren Vale** can identify old alloys but dislikes being expected to judge the morality of every ancestor who handled them.

**Adventure:** Authenticate a mixed hoard before its dispersal. Its valuable clue is a batch sequence connecting wage payments to a specific contractor. Buyers, workers' descendants, and collectors all want different parts of the evidence.

### Brinefold — the harbour above its drowned streets

**Present-day form:** A small harbour and salt-working settlement in the Vey Lowlands. Its active waterfront stands inland of the drowned old quay. Certain low tides reveal walls and slippery thresholds. A rebuilt bell house keeps copies of the subscription chest's later rules.

**A person to meet:** Boatwright **Ossa Fen** maintains a chart of safe approaches and wants the old rescue monument's unnamed workers identified. Her rival operates profitable low-tide tours with a less cautious timetable.

**Adventure:** Reach the old counting house, recover records or possessions, and return before the tide cuts off the route. A nesting coastal creature can complicate the expedition without being an enemy that must be killed. Whether the recovered papers support a living claim depends on later history as well as age.

### Three-Reed Bridge — the crossing with an unfinished tune

**Present-day form:** A rebuilt crossing outside Willowrace, connecting workshops with grazing and reed beds. Stone abutments support later spans; annual music gatherings still raise a portion of maintenance costs. Three pipe-shaped posts commemorate the dedication.

**A person to meet:** Music teacher **Tovin Dast**, adopted into a mixed household, keeps family arrangements without claiming descent from every named composer. He wants a missing regional verse restored to the fair programme.

**Adventure:** Recover Orven's transitions from a dispersed workshop archive, then help communities agree on a performance. The omitted verse names workers whose contribution also appears in a disputed bridge-endowment account.

### The Violet Steps — the landing kept clear

**Present-day form:** A small stone landing beside a secondary channel at Penn's Reach. Mineral staining returns after cleaning. Its steps have been reset several times as the banks changed, and the water-level record is maintained on newer stones beside the oldest surviving marks.

**A person to meet:** Channel keeper **Leth Uld** wants sound observations of the water creature's movements. She dislikes both baited spectacles and plans to ban ordinary fishing on the strength of a picturesque legend.

**Adventure:** Reconstruct the protected passage after a later flood and negotiate practical access among fishers, a mill tenant, and the Siltwife's needs. A successful settlement can leave all parties somewhat inconvenienced while restoring a living waterway.

## At 400 years ago

Ysra has been dead for 194 years. Her music has acquired arrangements she never heard, and keeping those arrangements has become part of respecting the people who came after her. Aveline's apology has a securely identified recipient; the remaining questions concern the harm, the second assistant, and what later admirers chose to remember.

Maera's succession belongs to Highmead's regional history, not a claim that one household ruled all modern Caldris. Road obligations, coin standards, port agreements, and local charters will survive, change, or fail under later governments. Their influence requires a chain of use and renewal rather than the automatic authority of age.

Brinefold's old streets remain underwater. Some panes still hold borrowed daylight. A vessel containing dangerous moths has an incomplete custody trail. Nivren may yet return to a familiar road and discover that the people keeping it alive have changed again.

For the next century, the strongest open threads are the copying and abuse of mutual-aid contracts, the fate of inherited enchantments, the uncredited second assistant, and the growing distance between public histories and the people whose work made them possible. Their outcomes remain unwritten.
