# Caldris: The Century of Open Windows

*600–500 years before the campaign present · Authored world lore · Includes GM secrets*

Continues [The Hills Beyond the Map](history-900-600-years-ago.md). Dates count backwards from the campaign present; the events begin after the preceding chapter's closing year. These histories belong to the world's past. Present-day people and adventure situations below are setting material, not records of player actions.

Its core chronology, mapped locations, historical accounts, and separately scoped GM story material are now published in live Caldris. Quest premises remain unplayed, and no character knowledge or campaign actions have been invented.

Continues in [The Hundred Years of Borrowed Light: 500–400 years ago](history-500-400-years-ago.md).

## A century within reach

The world of this century was old enough to argue about its own ruins and young enough to build new houses from them. Highmead still had a Merrow court. South Vey's assembly had become an institution people expected to consult, though many who paid its revenues could not speak there. Carriers crossed the northern valleys through agreements whose practical meaning depended on the next host opening the door.

Ysra was elderly in Seln. Aveline's life survived in copies, repairs, and a newly understood parcel whose arrival had once been mistaken for disappearance. Their histories were becoming things other people could use well or badly.

This century brings the older regional names into clearer relation with the modern atlas. The Chalklands lie within Alderwick on Eredane. The northern Roven country, Aunholt, and Seln occupy neighbouring uplands along the Veyr and Lornesse approaches. Serevan and Selvar are historic coastal and inland districts of Solasca, connected across the Lantern Sea through the route called Morrow Strait. These local names do not replace the continents or the later kingdoms shown on the world map.

## 599–592: The Last Lesson in Seln

Ysra's students expected a final revelation when she asked them to gather her notebooks. Instead, she asked each person to identify one conclusion they no longer believed. A nursery keeper described a failed planting; an instrument maker admitted that a revered timber mark had been copied; Ysra corrected a journey date she had repeated confidently for decades.

She died in **594 years before the campaign present**, aged **716**, in the house near Seln where she had spent her last years. Friends carried out the instructions she had actually left, including the return of borrowed tools. Her repaired fiddle went to a local teaching household for continued use.

**Traces and stories:** Several independent copies of her last corrections; a returned-tool receipt; a tune identified by its successive arrangers. A future search can recover a useful observation without requiring Ysra to be alive or her grave to contain an undiscovered weapon.

**GM truth:** Her death was natural and final in this history. A later story that she walked into the singing grove confuses her burial procession with departure. The Listener is a separate place-spirit and contains none of her memories.

## 591–584: The House with Twelve Windows

At the edge of Highmead's older market quarter, **Sister Dena Cor** and **glazier Orrin Vask** opened a school above a communal dining room. Its twelve narrow windows used mismatched donated glass. Children, apprentices, and adults working irregular hours attended different sessions.

The school taught letters, measures, practical accounts, and the reading of ordinary contracts. Its founders argued over whether charging small fees would exclude people or keep the rooms open. They adopted a mixed arrangement of payments, sponsored places, and paid work, revising it after the first winter's accounts.

**Traces and stories:** Slates bearing several learners' hands; windows with panes replaced across centuries; a meal list more complete than the surviving roll of pupils. A neglected account can identify an anonymous donor or restore credit to a teacher omitted from a patron's monument.

**GM truth:** Orrin could barely read when the school opened. He became one of its earliest adult pupils and never stopped being embarrassed by the ceremonial inscription crediting his wisdom.

## 583–576: The Silver Eels of Penn's Reach

A river channel cleared under a renewed sluice agreement produced an unusually abundant eel run. Households celebrated until disputes over traps and passage began damaging the channel itself. **Water keeper Nessa Uld** persuaded participants to leave a shared opening and stagger their catches.

A pale river creature appeared beneath the upstream willow on three recorded nights. It loosened small obstructions but withdrew when people tried to enclose it for display. Witnesses called it the **Siltwife**, although its shape, sex, and preferred name were never reliably established.

**Traces and stories:** Trap-spacing marks; a festival dish eaten from shared platters; descriptions of the willow pool. An apparently vanished local wonder may have moved after a later channel change rather than died or withdrawn in anger.

**GM truth:** The creature helped a limited stretch of water suit its own needs. It promised no perpetual harvest. The useful agreement among the fishers remained necessary after it stopped appearing.

## 575–568: The Highmead Marriage Refused

**Lady Alin Merrow** refused a marriage intended to strengthen the crown's hold over a northern road district. Negotiations continued without the wedding and produced a narrower agreement on escorts and maintenance. Her refusal became a romance in which a mysterious lover supposedly defeated a king.

Alin instead founded a household business arranging cloth shipments. Her correspondence shows affection for friends, commercial errors, and a determination to choose her own future. The abandoned marriage's other party, **Lord Teren Oss**, married elsewhere and remained one of her occasional business partners.

**Traces and stories:** A cancelled wedding order repurposed as a shipping ledger; cloth seals; two incompatible love songs. A collector seeking a lost lover's name might discover a more useful history of an independent household.

**GM truth:** No hidden lover caused the refusal. Alin disliked the proposed life and said so. Neither party needed to become a villain for the marriage not to happen.

## 567–560: The Winter at Crowstep

An early freeze trapped a carrier group at **Crowstep Shelter**, on a lesser northern branch. The building had beds and a sound roof but insufficient fodder. **Host Bela Reth** arranged a careful descent of the animals in small groups while travellers divided repairs, cooking, and watches.

A white-furred predator followed the final group. **Drover Ansel Marr** and two companions drove it away with coordinated noise and guarded passage, losing one pack animal before reaching safer ground. The incident became a grand monster hunt in a later fair play.

**Traces and stories:** Feed tallies; a surviving list of shared chores; a route note marking a safer winter descent. Restocking the shelter offers a reason for a present expedition to compare the old route with changed terrain.

**GM truth:** The predator was an ordinary dangerous animal, not an ancient winter deity. The host's advance planning saved more lives than the brief confrontation.

## 559–552: The Menders' Procession

Repair houses and independent craftspeople gathered at Willowrace after a dispute over the price of public work. They displayed tools, repaired objects, and accounts showing time and materials. The procession ended with demonstrations at the House of Second Use.

**Carpenter Sela Thenn** argued that useful labour should be paid even when the finished repair was nearly invisible. Several patrons agreed to clearer contracts. Others hired cheaper workers, some competent and some not. The gathering established a regional custom of signing significant public repairs.

**Traces and stories:** Hidden repair signatures; objects bearing both the original maker and later menders; a song naming tools in working order. Identifying a repairer's mark can explain a relic's unexpected materials or travels.

**GM truth:** One celebrated signature belongs to an apprentice who completed the work after the named master fell ill. The master deliberately asked for the apprentice to receive credit.

## 551–544: The Quiet Archive of Serevan

At Leth Sarai, warehouse conversions threatened boxes of copied shipping correspondence. **Clerk Mara Sef** rented a small dry room behind an oil seller and began the **Quiet Archive**, charging for commercial searches while copying historically important fragments for other collections.

Among the papers were records of a workshop assistant whose name resembled the torn addressee in Aveline's apologetic letter. Mara noted the possibility and the remaining discrepancy instead of announcing a discovery she could not prove.

**Traces and stories:** Index cards made from reused cargo labels; annotated name comparisons; references to material subsequently moved elsewhere. Comparing handwriting, dates, and workshop roles can advance the search for the assistant's identity.

**GM truth:** The strongest candidate was **Leris Amat**, an assistant involved in preparing glass surfaces. This chapter establishes a credible candidate, not a complete proof or the recovery of the full letter. A later adventure can find the missing corroboration.

## 543–536: The Chalk Stag at Rusk Hollow

A collapsed storage chamber revealed a small chalk carving of a stag above a blocked passage. Rumour connected it to Branna's long-dead white hart. Several visitors paid to see the site, and one entered the unstable passage without permission.

**Stoneworker Henn Vell** led the rescue with people who knew the storehouses. They found drainage work, household tallies, and an older burial niche, not the hart's returning spirit. The rescued visitor helped pay for safe closure and later documentation.

**Traces and stories:** A chalk carving with later protective marks; rescue payments; a plan separating successive building phases. A future opening can reveal the niche's occupants or more of the storage complex without repeating the same rescue.

**GM truth:** The stag was an old household emblem. Its resemblance to a famous monster is coincidental. The older niche remains a separate historical question.

## 535–528: The Empty Seat of the Road Court

An outbreak of ordinary illness prevented several representatives from attending a travelling session of Highmead's Court of Seven Roads. A collector urged the remaining members to decide a disputed levy immediately. **Judge Orra Penn** postponed the case and ordered temporary arrangements that kept passage open.

The delay inconvenienced carriers and cost money, but allowed affected hosts and workers to supply testimony. A later procedural rule required explicit notice when a hearing proceeded without a represented district. The empty seat became its emblem.

**Traces and stories:** Adjourned calendars; notices carried by named messengers; a chair painted on courthouse signs. A supposedly final ancient ruling may actually be a temporary order issued while testimony was still outstanding.

**GM truth:** Orra was not certain which claim would ultimately prevail. Her decision protected a fairer hearing rather than disguising a predetermined result.

## 527–520: The Gardens of the Farther Window

Boughmeet and an Istravel gardening household exchanged propagation records through carriers already moving fine glass and wool. Their experiments produced a few useful adaptations and many failures. At **Windowmere Garden**, a sheltered plot near Boughmeet, plants were labelled with the person who supplied the method rather than merely the patron who paid for the wall.

**Gardener Tavi Brin** preserved failed trials because another grower might otherwise repeat them. Visitors preferred the successful flowers, and Tavi planted the less impressive trial beds beside the entrance so they could not be entirely ignored.

**Traces and stories:** Labels recording failures; seed orders; wall repairs made to alter shelter. A present garden problem can be solved by recognising that a celebrated method worked under different conditions.

**GM truth:** One attributed foreign innovation was independently developed in both places. Comparison reveals shared practical reasoning, not necessarily theft or a secret common teacher.

## 519–512: The Lamp That Waited Through Repairs

Lastlight Pier needed another major repair. The neighbours tending Little Lastlight temporarily moved the inner lamp bracket to a covered landing nearby, explaining where fishers would approach. The spirit lit reluctantly at first, then settled into the temporary routine.

When the pier reopened, some residents wanted to leave the light where it now served more walkers. The final arrangement moved it back while installing an ordinary lamp at the temporary site. Maintenance required an additional household rota, a less romantic but essential part of the story.

**Traces and stories:** Two lamp foundations; supply rosters; a fisher's account of the temporary landing. These records provide a practical precedent for later relocation without claiming that a spirit can be moved anywhere against its will.

**GM truth:** Little Lastlight's hesitation came from unfamiliar surroundings. It imposed no curse on the repaired pier and demanded no costly sacrifice.

## 511–500: The Feast of Names Added Later

Saint Meret's Rest hosted musicians, growers, repairers, and families contributing corrections to local histories. Organiser **Edda Linn** displayed old accounts alongside amendments, keeping the earlier text visible where possible. Acknowledging a mistake became part of the ceremony rather than something quietly concealed before it.

Songs credited Jalen, Ysra, and later arrangers. Workshop notes named assistants. Darnel Field received two additional identifications, while many blank spaces remained. The gathering ended with ordinary meals supplied by several communities, each named in the accounts.

**Traces and stories:** Paired original and corrected inscriptions; a programme of shared authorship; unresolved-name lists. Future discoveries can add something meaningful without having to overturn everything previous generations knew.

**GM truth:** The feast did not settle all disputes. One family declined participation because the proposed account exposed private harm. The keepers retained a restricted record rather than force public reconciliation.

## Places to add to the world

### The Twelve Windows — a school above a dining room

**Present-day form:** A rebuilt school and communal dining room in Highmead's Dunmere quarter. Successive windows preserve the tradition of mixed donated glass. Its early slates survive mainly as reused fragments and copied exercises.

**A person to meet:** Teacher **Bera Cor** offers evening lessons and is organising repairs without pricing out adult learners.

**Adventure:** A wall repair exposes accounts connecting an anonymous patron to a forgotten teacher. The evidence may help restore a neglected educational endowment through its later, still-recognised terms.

### Crowstep Shelter — the smaller northern refuge

**Present-day form:** A maintained refuge on a local northern branch near the Roven approaches. It serves fewer carriers than Hearthscar and depends on seasonal deliveries. The winter descent has changed after later rockfalls.

**A person to meet:** Keeper **Nera Reth** needs a party to verify an alternate delivery route before the first snow.

**Adventure:** Locate a missing supply wagon, assess the safer descent, and bring back a realistic account of what animals and carts can use it.

### The Quiet Archive — the records behind the oil shop

**Present-day form:** A small archive and copying business in Leth Sarai, occupying repaired rooms above an ordinary shop. Its collections have moved within the district several times; custody lists explain the surviving material.

**A person to meet:** Copyist **Hadi Amat** is comparing workshop records and refuses to infer ancestry from a shared surname alone.

**Adventure:** Corroborate whether Leris Amat received Aveline's apology by connecting preparation notes, a later letter, and an employee list held elsewhere.

### Windowmere Garden — the plot of useful failures

**Present-day form:** A small teaching garden within the Boughmeet nursery district. The walls have been rebuilt and the original plants replaced or propagated. Successful and failed trials receive equally legible labels.

**A person to meet:** Gardener **Sella Brin** is testing a northern graft and wants help retrieving a neglected record before accepting a donor's expensive remedy.

**Adventure:** Find the account of an earlier failed trial, then obtain a modest material or propagation method that addresses the actual growing conditions.

### The Chalk Stag Chamber — the household emblem underground

**Present-day form:** A restricted archaeological chamber beneath the Rusk Hollow storehouse complex. A safe upper approach permits examination of the carving; deeper passages need a separate assessment after each significant collapse.

**A person to meet:** Surveyor **Oren Vell** wants to record the older burial niche without disturbing unstable walls.

**Adventure:** Identify the niche's household through tally marks and later records. The reward can be a restored name and a safer site, with any deeper mystery arising from evidence actually found.

## At 500 years ago

Ysra has been dead for ninety-four years. Her work continues through several learners and collections, with no single chosen heir. Aveline's letter has a plausible addressee whose identity still needs corroboration. Both lives remain complete even while discoveries about them continue.

The Crown of Highmead and neighbouring institutions have not yet become every modern polity shown on the atlas. Their remaining changes belong to the next five centuries. Ancient political names can survive as regional names without implying that the same rulers still govern.

This century leaves schools, repaired shelters, corrected records, and a few modest wonders. Many of its best stories begin with someone noticing that an ordinary object has been carefully kept—and asking who did the keeping.
