# Caldris: The Roads of Salt and Rain

*400–300 years before the campaign present · Authored world lore · Includes GM secrets*

Continues [The Hundred Years of Borrowed Light](history-500-400-years-ago.md). Dates count backwards from the campaign present. Present-day people and adventure premises are setting material, not records of player actions. Historical accounts and the private explanations labelled **GM truth** are kept separate.

The twelve chronological entries, six mapped locations, full historical accounts, and separate GM story material are published in live Caldris. Map anchors illustrate placement within each parent location and do not establish travel distances. No character knowledge or campaign progress is advanced by this history.

Continues in [The Century of Distant Hearths: 300–200 years ago](history-300-200-years-ago.md).

## When a promise could travel farther than its maker

At four hundred years ago, a Brinefold boat owner could buy provisions with a claim on a voyage that had not yet returned. A gardener in Boughmeet might receive glass made on Solasca without ever meeting anyone who had crossed the Lantern Sea. These arrangements saved journeys and created new opportunities to misunderstand, cheat, or help strangers.

The peace was uneven. A prosperous port could sit a week's journey from a valley where every winter still threatened isolation. Highmead's crown, the Court of Seven Roads, local assemblies, and independent towns all maintained obligations with different boundaries. Authority accumulated through bargains, marriages, custom, and force; no single kingdom commanded the whole network.

This century gives Caldris roads opened and abandoned, unfamiliar food becoming familiar, craftspeople outliving their reputations, and genuine wonders whose survival depends on ordinary care. Its dangers often begin when someone tries to make a useful thing serve more people than it can safely support.

## 399–392: The Dry Exchange of Brinefold

Brinefold's subscription chest began accepting certain claims as security for supplies. **Warehouse keeper Nera Oss** established the **Dry Exchange**, a covered hall where clerks compared voyage declarations, boat shares, and payment promises. Buyers could obtain salt or timber before their own cargo arrived, provided someone would accept the risk.

Two vessels then returned late together. The exchange suspended several payments, and a boat owner discovered that the same expected cargo had supported advances from three lenders. Negotiations kept the storehouses operating, but some creditors accepted grain and repair work instead of silver. Smaller claimants sold their rights cheaply to households able to wait.

The settlement introduced a public register of pledged cargo. Market songs mocked the hall as the place where a fish could be sold three times before it was caught.

**Traces and stories:** Pledge books, cargo seals, and receipts for payments made in work. A family may possess a genuine claim whose value depends on a later renewal. Recovering the missing register can reveal duplicate pledges without making every debtor a criminal.

**GM truth:** The boat owner initially believed the advances covered separate shares. A broker concealed how much had already been pledged and purchased distressed claims through a relative. Their partnership left a trail in warehouse rent payments rather than in the polished exchange accounts.

## 391–384: The Night of the Unlocked Doors

A sealed vessel from the glass-moth summer appeared in a Leth Sarai collection. Its owner mistook an old warning for a maker's boast and commissioned the glaze to be opened for examination. Moths escaped into a district containing several workshops protected by the mineral-based bindings they preferred.

Doors opened, a lifting charm failed, and falling glass injured workers. **Potter Seli Varr** and ward repairer **Taman Rel** adapted the earlier containment method. They gathered the swarm around disposable bindings and fired the vessels in a kiln whose ordinary brickwork required no enchantment. Neighbours carried water, braced damaged structures, and guarded shops while their magical locks were useless.

The surviving kiln became **Mothwatch Kiln**, supported partly by fees for examining inherited enchanted objects. The collection's proprietor paid compensation after a public hearing, then sold most of the remaining collection.

**Traces and stories:** Kiln ledgers, injury settlements, and a catalogue listing where the other objects went. A present commission can trace a dangerous acquisition before its new owner repeats the mistake. A locked room may be safely opened by understanding its ward, without releasing live moths to do the work.

**GM truth:** This was the missing coastal vessel from the previous century. Its custody mystery is resolved here. Most of its brood was destroyed; a small surviving sealed sample remained for study. Whether that sample survives into the present must be established by later custody evidence, not assumed because danger would be convenient.

## 383–376: The Stair the Giant Would Not Finish

Carriers exploring a Veyr upland route encountered **Aurr**, a solitary stone giant, repairing part of an ancient stair above a ravine. Aurr accepted food, iron tools, and assistance moving smaller stones. **Road surveyor Dema Pell** proposed completing the passage for pack trains, which could avoid a longer route closed by winter slips.

Aurr stopped work where the proposed stair crossed a sheltered shelf. Attempts to buy permission failed. Eventually Dema understood that the shelf held the graves of two members of Aurr's household. The survey was redrawn, but the alternative required a costly timber gallery. The sponsors withdrew before it was completed.

Local people continued using the lower steps. The **Unfinished Stair** became a warning in merchants' stories about stubborn giants; several valley families remembered the surveyor who had refused to force a road through a burial place.

**Traces and stories:** Oversized tool marks, abandoned gallery sockets, and two versions of Dema's plan. A present expedition can assess a less invasive route or recover equipment left beyond the unfinished span. The graves deserve consideration even if nobody is available to enforce it.

**GM truth:** Aurr understood more of the negotiations than the carriers realised. The refusal was deliberate, not a failure to grasp money. This chapter does not establish whether Aurr survives into the present. The graves contain personal belongings and worked stone, not a giant's hidden treasury.

## 375–368: The Kitchens of Saffron Court

At Namar Vale, an association of established traders tried to exclude travelling cooks from the principal market. The cooks depended on caravan arrivals and could not afford permanent stalls. **Cook Hadi Nemm** and **lodging keeper Vessa Tal** rented a disused dye yard and opened **Saffron Court**, with shared ovens and stalls paid for by the day.

Its name promised luxury; most meals used onions, pulses, coarse grain, and inexpensive local herbs. Cooks introduced travellers to unfamiliar dishes and learned what would keep during a journey. Translators found work explaining both prices and ingredients. The court became a useful place to hire guides or compare news carried by caravans.

Within a generation, successful stallholders tried to restrict newcomers themselves. A revised lease reserved several daily places, enforced through later renewals rather than through the founders' reputation alone.

**Traces and stories:** Oven bookings, annotated recipes, lease renewals, and words borrowed between trading languages. A lost recipe can identify a migration route or lead to a household keeping an older version. A disputed stall allocation offers an adventure in negotiation, evidence, and making sure someone can still earn supper.

**GM truth:** Hadi used the prestigious name to attract customers and openly admitted that most dishes contained no saffron. The real deception came later, when a lease-holder described reserved newcomer stalls as private property in a loan agreement.

## 367–360: The War of the Shorter Road

A newly improved branch diverted traffic from a toll crossing between the Chalklands and the Vey Lowlands. The crossing's lord claimed that an earlier grant entitled his household to payments from all traffic in the district. Carriers argued that the grant applied to the maintained crossing alone.

Escorts seized carts, toll guards dismantled part of the branch, and retaliatory raids burned fodder stores. Later chronicles called the conflict a war, although its main weapons were detention, hunger, threats, and the destruction of small livelihoods. **Mediator Orra Senn** secured a settlement that reduced the old toll and assigned maintenance duties to both routes.

The shorter road remained open, but a drainage ditch cut during its construction damaged downstream grazing. Compensation for that harm continued after the political dispute was supposedly settled.

**Traces and stories:** Confiscation lists, damaged boundary stones, fodder debts, and testimony from households absent from the formal treaty. An old road claim may matter today through later recognised renewals. A buried culvert can be more useful evidence than a noble family's heroic history.

**GM truth:** Neither route could handle all seasonal traffic safely. Several negotiators knew this while publicly insisting that their opponents' road was unnecessary. The settlement worked because carriers quietly used both. A later restoration that ignores seasonal capacity could revive the original danger.

## 359–352: The Name Beside the Second Hand

Records brought from an Istravel workshop identified **Nen Tal** as the second assistant mentioned in Leris Amat's reply to Aveline. Nen prepared abrasive mixtures and helped develop a repeatable finishing method. A later household account showed that part of the disputed payment eventually arrived, years after Nen had left that employment.

**Archivist Kessa Amat** published the matched records alongside examples of Nen's later pottery work. A craftspeople's teaching house in Istravel adopted the name **the House of Two Hands**, commissioning a sign that showed a maker and an assistant working together. Its founders endowed paid teaching places, hoping recognition would become useful to living workers.

The discovery completed the central chain of names surrounding Aveline's apology. Arguments continued over how exhibitions should describe the work and whether every later copy needed a lengthy account of the dispute.

**Traces and stories:** Workshop recipes, payment fragments, pottery signatures, and teaching endowments. Future quests can authenticate an object, follow Nen's independent career, or protect a neglected teaching place. They need not repeatedly withhold the assistant's identity after the evidence has settled it.

**GM truth:** Nen accepted the late payment without treating it as full settlement. The account includes money intended for someone else's supplies, making the total easy to overstate. A descendant's later claim that Aveline secretly funded Nen's entire workshop is unsupported and became popular because it made reconciliation simpler.

## 351–344: The First Rain at Rainbell Orchard

At Boughmeet, grafts taken from an old pear tree produced fruit with a curious property: the dried seed cases rang faintly when struck by the first drops of rain. Most cuttings lost the effect. **Nursery keeper Mara Elven**, a human despite her surname, preserved it in a sheltered plot later called **Rainbell Orchard**.

The orchard attracted musicians, growers, and visitors who expected predictions of storms. Mara demonstrated that the cases sounded only after water touched them. Their tones varied, and an entire dry harvest could fail to ring. A local maker nevertheless built a modest rain instrument, played under an open gutter during festivals.

The nursery sold ordinary fruit and propagation stock alongside its unreliable wonder. Its accounts show that practical pear varieties paid for keeping the enchanted line alive.

**Traces and stories:** Grafting records, fragile seed instruments, and lists of failed cuttings. A present search might recover compatible rootstock from an unremarkable household garden. Restoring the festival instrument can require patient growing and cooperation rather than a spell cast over dead wood.

**GM truth:** The inherited effect is real and limited. Its original source remains unknown; no ancient deity's return is required to explain its survival. Taking every viable cutting from one tree could destroy the very lineage a collector hopes to preserve.

## 343–336: The Pension Chest with Two Locks

After a period of intermittent escort fighting, Highmead's regional court promised payments to wounded guards and households that had lost wage earners. **Clerk Eren Vask** found that household allowances and court patronage were being paid from the same poorly distinguished fund.

Representatives of claimants negotiated a chest requiring two custodians and a public payment day. The reform made it harder to divert money quietly but did not create enough revenue to meet every promise. Widows serving as household heads received payments more readily than unmarried dependants, and several families petitioned for recognition over successive years.

The chest's two locks became a familiar emblem of shared custody. Later authorities copied the arrangement, sometimes without copying the requirement that claimants could inspect the accounts.

**Traces and stories:** Paired keys, claimant rolls, and a series of petitions with increasingly worn folds. A missing key can be an inconvenience rather than a magical barrier; the real mystery may concern who was entitled to hold it. A forgotten allowance could still matter if its terms were renewed by successor institutions.

**GM truth:** Eren's sister was among the excluded unmarried dependants. That personal interest helped him notice the problem and also influenced which petitions he pursued first. His reform deserves neither dismissal as selfishness nor a legend of perfect impartiality.

## 335–328: Orven's Last Public Performance

At Three-Reed Bridge, the elderly dwarven arranger **Orven Dast** played one of his own transitions incorrectly and stopped the ensemble with an irritated laugh. He had begun losing dexterity and could no longer manage passages he had written decades earlier. A younger musician suggested replacing the concert with speeches honouring him.

Orven chose to arrange simpler parts and teach them. The fair heard a new version suited to the people actually playing that year. He retired from public performance afterward, remaining involved in instrument repairs and arguments about tempo. His death belongs to later history, rather than being implied by his departure from the stage.

One teaching copy contains the instruction: leave enough silence for the other player to breathe. It became a saying among bridge musicians who seldom knew its first context.

**Traces and stories:** Simplified scores, altered instruments, and recordings in ordinary written notation. Recovering an early arrangement might help a musician adapt a treasured work to a changed body. A competition demanding the supposedly original version could be challenged using Orven's own revisions.

**GM truth:** The younger musician meant the proposed speeches kindly. Orven initially mistook the suggestion for an attempt to remove him. Their eventual correspondence records an apology on each side; neither needed to lose the other's friendship for the misunderstanding to matter.

## 327–320: The Winter of False Harbour Seals

Merchants in several Lantern Sea ports presented payment orders bearing convincing Brinefold exchange seals. Some were legitimate promises copied onto the wrong forms; others concerned ships that had never sailed. Refusals spread until honest carriers struggled to obtain supplies.

**Ship's accountant Daro Venn** traced the fraud through repeated cargo descriptions and a seal-maker's purchase of an unusual wax mixture. Port clerks agreed to exchange dated counterfoils by trusted carriers. The procedure slowed transactions but restored confidence without requiring every merchant to carry a chest of silver.

Arrests did not recover all the money. Several villages had already supplied goods in good faith, and the final settlement distributed losses among creditors, suppliers, and a limited guarantee fund.

**Traces and stories:** Counterfoil bundles, wax recipes, and fictitious ship names repeated in later family stories. A supposed ancestral voyage may originate in a fraudulent order. Recovering genuine counterparts can rescue an honest claim from a batch of forged documents.

**GM truth:** The forgers included a dismissed exchange clerk and a respected intermediary who continued helping investigators. Their relationship can be established through paid storage, not merely through a villain's confession. A few surviving false orders were deliberately kept as training examples and should not be mistaken for active claims.

## 319–312: Nivren Learns the New Road

Nivren Sael returned to the Copper Kettle and found a hearth mosaic where he remembered a blue cup. The orchard approach had shifted after changes to a stream. He became lost following a path that was now a boundary hedge and was guided back by **apprentice carrier Pella Morn**, who assumed he was simply unfamiliar with the district.

Nivren bought a current route book. He spent part of the winter teaching Pella the boot-drying charm, then travelled with her to a neighbouring market as her paying passenger. His older memories proved useful at one abandoned crossing and misleading at another.

Their later correspondence survives in fragments. Pella described journeys with people Nivren never met. He replied to what she actually wrote rather than repeatedly telling her how the roads had been when he was young.

**Traces and stories:** A corrected route book, lesson notes, and letters naming now-vanished stopping places. An expedition can compare old and newer routes while deciding which memories remain useful. Nivren's possible present-day appearance should respect the lives he missed as well as those he remembers.

**GM truth:** Nivren still carries guilt over returning too late to the inn in the earlier century. Pella does not cure that grief. She gives him a new friendship with its own beginning, disagreements, and eventual ending. His survival and whereabouts in the campaign present remain available setting choices.

## 311–300: The Convoy That Waited for Rain

A convoy leaving the Serevan coast faced pressure to sail before an approaching seasonal change. A recently repaired harbour charm promised clear guidance between submerged shoals, and investors treated that promise as a substitute for local pilots. **Pilot Sera Noll** refused the departure, arguing that the charm's reference marks had shifted with the rebuilt quay.

The captains delayed while crews compared soundings and inspected the old marks at low water. Rain then revealed a runoff channel that the new plans had mistaken for stable ground. The convoy departed later by a safer approach, missing a profitable fair but avoiding the route that would have endangered its deepest vessels.

The dispute established a regional practice of paying pilots for refused departures when their reasons survived review. Its opponents called it wages for staying ashore. Sailors called it the right to come home late.

**Traces and stories:** Sounding books, refused-departure payments, and two incompatible harbour plans. A present contract can place a party between a deadline and a pilot's defensible caution. Surveying the old approach may recover useful knowledge without proving the abandoned route safe for every vessel.

**GM truth:** The charm was working according to its original placement. Its failure was the mismatch between that placement and the altered harbour, not secret sabotage. One captain understood Sera's concern but concealed support until other investors accepted the delay. His private letter can complicate a later reputation for reckless courage.

## Places to add to the world

### The Dry Exchange — promises beneath a warehouse roof

**Present-day form:** A covered trading hall in Brinefold with raised document rooms and a public counter for inspecting registered cargo pledges. Its rules have been revised under successive authorities. Carved fish on the older beam ends commemorate the early dispute over promises made against the same catch.

**A person to meet:** Registry keeper **Jessa Oss** wants to separate valid inherited claims from commemorative papers and fraudulent copies. She is competent at accounts and poor at refusing people who have travelled a long way for disappointing news.

**Adventure:** Recover a missing counterfoil from a dispersed collection before a shipowner loses access to supplies. An apparently worthless rent receipt may identify who profited from buying distressed claims.

### Mothwatch Kiln — the workshop that distrusts enchanted locks

**Present-day form:** A pottery workshop and small examination room in Leth Sarai. Its older kiln is maintained with ordinary masonry, and dangerous objects are handled in rooms with mechanical closures. The public collection includes harmless fired vessels and copies of historical warnings.

**A person to meet:** Potter **Venn Seli** can reproduce the sacrificial binding medium but refuses commissions that omit the object's custody history.

**Adventure:** Trace an inherited sealed vessel before anyone attempts to open it. Establish whether it contains a dead specimen, a harmless imitation, or a surviving brood; its appearance alone is insufficient. Containment and restitution can matter more than ownership.

### The Unfinished Stair — a road that stops before the graves

**Present-day form:** A partly maintained ascent in the Veyr uplands, ending at abandoned gallery sockets above a ravine. The lower steps serve shepherds and surveyors. A sheltered burial shelf lies beyond the intended road alignment, and later walkers have marked a respectful route around its approach.

**A person to meet:** Surveyor **Hessa Pell** is assessing a small seasonal footpath, not promising a caravan highway. She needs observations of rock, snow, and water before accepting a sponsor's timetable.

**Adventure:** Reach the old survey stations, retrieve a lost instrument, and determine what route the terrain can support. The graves and any living giant household have interests of their own. A useful answer may be a limited path and a clear explanation of its limits.

### Saffron Court — the market of shared ovens

**Present-day form:** A busy courtyard market in Namar Vale with shared ovens, washing places, and a mixture of permanent and daily stalls. Its name remains grander than most of its meals. The reserved newcomer places survive through later lease renewals that vendors must occasionally defend.

**A person to meet:** Oven keeper **Miri Nemm** knows caravan departures by the food people order. She wants accurate news of an absent supplier and dislikes visitors treating every foreign recipe as a secret remedy.

**Adventure:** Follow a missing spice or grain consignment, negotiate substitute supplies, or recover a lease renewal before a lender closes the daily stalls. Shared meals can introduce future companions, rivals, and guides naturally.

### The House of Two Hands — a school for work done together

**Present-day form:** A craftspeople's teaching house in Istravel, with pottery benches, finishing tables, and copies of Nen Tal's documented work. Original objects rotate between collections; labels distinguish originals, teaching copies, and later reconstructions. Some teaching places are supported by renewed endowments.

**A person to meet:** Instructor **Kera Tal** is preparing an exhibition of assistants' work. She has no proven kinship with Nen and objects when donors advertise her as a rediscovered descendant.

**Adventure:** Authenticate a workshop object or find the later renewal that protects a paid teaching place. A collector may offer valuable evidence on condition that the exhibition repeat a flattering but unsupported family story.

### Rainbell Orchard — pears beneath a listening gutter

**Present-day form:** A small nursery plot within Boughmeet's growing district. Ordinary fruit trees support the maintenance of a few carefully propagated rainbell pears. A sheltered festival instrument sounds only when rainwater reaches its dried seed cases; replacement parts are recorded by harvest year.

**A person to meet:** Grower **Samin Elven** wants compatible rootstock and reliable labour for the next grafting season. He likes the orchard's visitors but refuses to sell cuttings that would endanger its remaining healthy trees.

**Adventure:** Find a surviving graft in a neglected garden, establish who owns it, and bring back propagation material in good condition. A musician's urgent commission and the orchard's slower biological needs need not have the same timetable.

## At 300 years ago

Ysra has been dead for 294 years. Her work remains present through musicians who adapt it, people who repair instruments, and disagreements that would have been unfamiliar to her. Orven has retired from performance but is not declared dead by this chapter. Nivren's friendships have expanded beyond the households he first set out to revisit.

Aveline, Leris, and Nen now have a documented shared history. Future discoveries may clarify objects, payments, and later uses of their reputations, but their central identities are settled. The glass-moth vessel's arrival on Solasca is also established; any surviving specimen requires a later chain of custody.

Brinefold trades in promises that reach well beyond its harbour. Namar Vale feeds strangers in a courtyard founded by people once excluded from its market. A stair still stops before a grave, and a nursery spends ordinary fruit money preserving something beautiful and impractical.

The next century inherits expanding trade, obligations that are easy to copy and hard to honour, and old magic whose surroundings keep changing. The open questions concern who maintains those arrangements, who gets left outside them, and what happens when an overlooked place becomes important again.
