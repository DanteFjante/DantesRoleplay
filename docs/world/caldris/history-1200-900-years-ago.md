# Caldris: The Tides of Promise

*1,200–900 years before the campaign present · Working setting lore · Includes GM secrets*

Continues [The Long Summer and the Returning Roads](history-1600-1200-years-ago.md). Dates count backwards from the campaign present. The opening describes conditions at 1,200 years ago; dated events begin in the following year.

Continues in [The Hills Beyond the Map: 900–600 years ago](history-900-600-years-ago.md).

This chapter adds freely revisable lore, locations, and people for the setting before play begins, as authorised by the user. Its core chronology, mapped locations, historical accounts, and separately scoped GM story material are now published in live Caldris. Present-day situations are available adventure premises, not events already occurring. Historical accounts can become discoverable lore; **GM truth** paragraphs contain information to keep separate when preparing player-facing material.

## At the turning of the tide

By this time, a traveller could walk through Highmead wearing a Serevan coat, carrying a Whitewindow knife, and complaining that nothing good came from elsewhere.

The roads connected people whose rulers rarely agreed. Veymouth's free port, the Reedbank Association, and South Vey's margravate occupied the former river kingdom. Highmead retained a crown whose authority rested on repeated bargains with towns and estates. In the north, the Roven Marches kept the principal passes open through cooperation that was more reliable in summer than in winter.

There were still poor villages within a day's journey of rich markets. A merchant's profit could depend on a storm beyond the horizon. A carefully tended orchard could survive an absent spirit, but an absent millwright might leave a district hungry. Wonders existed amid that work: a vessel preserving a few words, glass answering particular stars, an old house that knew when somebody had come to visit.

Aveline Sere had been dead for 210 years. Some long-lived people still remembered meeting her, but her public image was already becoming tidier than her life. Her scattered papers were valuable, difficult to identify, and mixed with later commentary.

Over the next three centuries, trade would reach new markets, a short war would leave long consequences, and communities would learn what could be repaired after peace. The focus widens along the established roads, northern passes, and Morrow Strait. It does not make these events a single age experienced identically across all Caldris.

## First century: The Far Markets — 1,200–1,100 years ago

### 1,199–1,188: The Fair Beneath the Sailcloth

An autumn storm damaged Alderwake's covered market shortly before its largest fair. Harbour merchants sent used sailcloth inland in exchange for future stall rents. For six weeks the market traded beneath patched blue, brown, and cream canvas, its roof rising and falling with every gust.

**Fairkeeper Hessa Varn** arranged common kitchens while carpenters worked above occupied stalls. Visiting Serevan merchants found customers for goods they had expected to sell nearer the coast. Inland makers discovered that foreign buyers wanted ordinary woollen blankets and reliable tool handles more than exotic curiosities.

The fair became an annual meeting between inland and maritime traders. It remained seasonal; the greater traffic did not turn Alderwake overnight into a wealthy metropolis. Families rented out rooms, argued over prices, and learned enough unfamiliar words to praise or insult breakfast.

**Traces and stories:** Sailmakers' marks preserved in accounts; unusual tent-post sockets beneath later paving; a fair dish called Storm Supper. A scrap of canvas in a shrine can identify a ship whose recorded voyages never included the port that supposedly donated it.

**GM truth:** One celebrated donation was a purchase quietly financed by local stallholders. Harbour patrons received most of the public credit because they could afford commemorative banners.

### 1,187–1,175: The Lantern at the End of the Pier

At Leth Sarai, an elderly lamplighter named **Faris Nemm** maintained a small light on a disused pier because his brother's boat had once returned there in a fog. Ships no longer unloaded at that landing, but fishers and people walking the mudbanks used it to find their way home.

After Faris died, the light appeared on several evenings when nobody admitted tending it. A small spirit had settled in the lamp housing, accustomed to the repeated act of welcome. It could coax a remaining ember and shield a flame from a modest wind. It could not create oil, withstand any storm, or guide ships through unknown channels.

Neighbours agreed to leave supplies. The spirit acquired the name **Little Lastlight**, and the pier became a place where travellers said goodbye without attracting the bustle of the main harbour.

**Traces and stories:** Oil accounts kept beside memorial names; successive lamp housings preserving an older inner bracket; farewell ribbons on later railings. A present light that keeps failing may need repairs and a renewed arrangement rather than a ritual of banishment.

**GM truth:** Faris's ghost did not become the spirit. Lastlight imitates some of his habits without possessing his memories. It cannot answer questions about his missing brother, whose eventual fate remains a separate human story.

### 1,174–1,162: The Road of Bitter Leaves

Caravans from Serevan's eastern neighbour, the **Principality of Selvar**, began attending the markets at Istravel in greater numbers. They brought a dried leaf brewed into a bitter, fragrant drink, alongside dyestuffs, woven mats, and pottery well suited to long transport.

The drink's popularity spread first among affluent households and people working late in counting rooms. Most Chalklands villages still preferred familiar herbs or ordinary ale. An expensive imported taste did not immediately become a universal custom.

**Rima Dast**, a Selvari carrier who owned three mules with two relatives, kept a route book describing wells, guesthouses, and the difference between advertised shelter and a dry place to sleep. She made good money for several years, then lost much of it when buyers favoured a fashionable rival district's leaves.

**Traces and stories:** Small brewing pots; leaf-storage jars with dated seals; Rima's sharply worded guesthouse notes. A merchant asks travellers to verify whether a premium cargo came from the named terraces or was relabelled along the route.

**GM truth:** The unfashionable leaves were not poisonous or fraudulent. Changing taste ruined some honest growers. Counterfeit prestige and actual quality must be investigated separately.

### 1,161–1,149: The Queen of the Second Table

The final Sorne sovereign of Highmead died after a reign marked by disputed levies and a failed succession arrangement. The most credible claimant, **Queen Maressa of House Merrow**, secured recognition through renewed estate agreements and the support of several market councils.

Maressa held formal audiences at the great table of her predecessors. She also kept a smaller table near the household kitchens where petitioners with urgent practical complaints could be heard. Its usefulness depended on her time and judgement, not an automatic right of access available to everyone.

She confirmed selected common rights, paid down part of the crown's arrears, and accepted that some collectors appointed by her own supporters should be removed. House Sorne survived as several lesser families. Highmead's crown continued under a new household, rather than requiring one dynasty to govern unchanged through every century.

**Traces and stories:** Kitchen-side petition rolls; furniture accounts for an ordinary trestle table; renewed grazing stones. A supposed secret royal chamber may be the service room where petitioners once waited with borrowed bowls of soup.

**GM truth:** Maressa sometimes favoured a compelling personal story over a less eloquent but stronger claim. Later correspondence records corrections. Access to a sympathetic ruler helped people and remained an uneven form of justice.

### 1,148–1,136: The Island of the Wrong Stars

Three vessels approaching the small island of **Veyr's Lantern**, north of the usual Morrow Strait crossing, reported star reflections on water covered by cloud. A wreck followed when a captain trusted the reflected pattern more than his pilot's warning.

The island held an abandoned observing court fitted with dark glass tiles. Earlier damage had tilted part of it toward a tidal pool. On particular nights, a surviving enchantment projected an old recorded arrangement of stars across the water. It had once been an instructional device; in its altered setting, it became a navigational hazard.

**Pilot Soren Avet** and **glassworker Dima Rell** led an expedition to screen the reflecting surface and recover inscriptions. They left much of the buried court undisturbed because their hired boat could not remain through the next weather change.

**Traces and stories:** Glass in shoreline rubble; a temporary screen described in expense records; warning marks in later pilot books. Renewed exposure after erosion can justify a return expedition with practical preparations and a clear danger to address.

**GM truth:** The stars are a recorded pattern, not a prophecy or a doorway into the sky. The court's builders may connect to the wider glassworking tradition, but this chapter does not establish who built it or why the island was abandoned.

### 1,135–1,123: The Meeting of Three Widows

At a reed-market landing, three women whose households had lost boats formed a shared reserve to support replacement craft and bereaved families. **Talla Oren**, **Bess Hal**, and **Nim Sareth** brought different resources: a workshop lease, trusted accounts, and connections among the boat crews.

Their **Reed Chest** grew through small regular contributions. It distinguished losses eligible for replacement help from ordinary maintenance, and investigated suspicious claims through people familiar with the boats. Several members disliked paying during years in which they needed nothing.

The arrangement supported families that would otherwise lose their livelihoods after a single accident. It also failed some applicants, especially newcomers unable to produce the required contributions or witnesses. A fund with limited money could not repair every tragedy.

**Traces and stories:** Boat marks paired with household names; contribution tallies; an old chest repaired with timbers from a wreck. An ancient claim can reveal a family's history, but modern entitlement depends on later continuity and terms, not possession of a weathered token alone.

**GM truth:** One founder concealed a payment to a family outside the rules, then repaid it gradually from her own share. The institution's survival owed something to both firm limits and personal generosity that the public accounts did not explain.

### 1,122–1,110: The Companions of the Late Blossom

An elven herb collector named **Ysra Leth**, born 1,310 years before the campaign present, began travelling with **Jalen Fen**, a human repairer of musical instruments. Ysra sought plants described in older nursery records. Jalen needed paying work and wanted to hear regional songs before their older singers died.

They spent four seasons moving between Boughmeet, Hazelstairs' lower approaches, and the fairs. Their disagreements concerned food, money, and whether a promising detour was worth missing a festival booking. Jalen taught Ysra to ask how long someone could afford to wait.

At **Bluecup Hollow**, they found a tiny blue flower whose opening petals were used in a modest charm to retain a scent in linen. The plant grew only in a particular damp limestone pocket. Overharvesting threatened it more immediately than any monster.

**Traces and stories:** A joint notebook alternating plant sketches and tune fragments; repaired instruments marked with a leaf; a scent cloth in a sealed keepsake box. A missing plant name can be reconstructed from where the companions performed rather than from botanical description alone.

**GM truth:** Ysra and Jalen were friends and working companions. Later love ballads invent a romance. Their affection was real without needing the relationship those songs supplied.

### 1,109–1,100: The Night of a Hundred Names

Saint Meret's Rest hosted a gathering of families seeking relatives missing after journeys, wars, marriages, and migration. The sanctuary's keepers compared regional editions of the Book of Roads Left Open with household testimony and burial notices.

Some people found answers. Some discovered that a relative had chosen another life. Many went home with nothing certain. **Clerk Adelin Ross** kept a separate list of unresolved cases rather than allowing the closing celebration to imply that all had been solved.

Ysra attended with Jalen, who sought news of an uncle lost along the northern roads. They learned that he had survived his presumed accident and died years later at a distant hospice. The answer brought grief and relief, along with a letter explaining why he had never returned.

**Traces and stories:** A register separating confirmed identifications from possibilities; small personal effects; an annual practice of reading unresolved names. A new annotation in a later copy can offer the next lead in a case that several generations failed to settle.

**GM truth:** The gathering did not use a perfect magical means of identifying the dead. Its strength came from combining incomplete records and accepting when they did not justify certainty.

## Second century: The Unquiet Borders — 1,099–1,000 years ago

### 1,099–1,088: The Orchard Behind the Wall

At Harth, the Guest remained asleep while orchard land became more valuable. A wealthy household enclosed an old access path to create a private garden. The path was used by nursery workers, pilgrims, and residents taking the short way to a well.

**Keeper Sena Brill** organised a careful account of who used it and under what arrangements. The resulting settlement preserved a narrow public passage and changed the route around the family's private rooms. It satisfied nobody completely and worked well enough to endure through several later renewals.

The enclosure also exposed a buried bowl and the roots of an older tree. Excitement over a supposed divine judgement brought more visitors than the path could comfortably carry. Sena spent much of the following summer asking people not to stand on the fragile ground they had come to revere.

**Traces and stories:** A narrow lane turning sharply around a wall; copied access agreements; a modest offering bowl displayed elsewhere for protection. A later blocked passage can be investigated through use and renewal rather than an assumption that an ancient spirit owns every nearby field.

**GM truth:** The Guest did not wake or command the outcome. The settlement was a human achievement, and the bowl's discovery was a consequence of digging rather than evidence of approval from the sleeper.

### 1,087–1,075: The Silver That Would Not Travel

Highmead's mint reduced the silver content of a new coin issue while retaining familiar names and designs. Officials expected the coins to circulate locally at the old rate. Foreign counters weighed them and offered less. Sellers at Alderwake began quoting two prices, producing arguments that spread faster than reliable explanations.

**Assayer Pel Marrin** published comparison tables and offered witnessed tests. He became unpopular with the crown's collectors and with opportunists misrepresenting older coins as equally debased. A small deficit in royal accounts became a much wider crisis of confidence.

The settlement accepted the new coins for specified crown dues and stopped insisting that distant markets value them identically. It also replaced one mint official whose concealed private dealings had worsened an openly adopted policy.

**Traces and stories:** Clipped sample coins in an assayer's board; two-price account books; a market rhyme about a king's thin face. An apparently counterfeit hoard may contain legitimate issues that circulated under different values.

**GM truth:** The policy and the official's fraud were distinct. Not every loss was stolen, and proving a coin genuine does not establish that it ever held the value its owner claims.

### 1,074–1,062: The Bridge of Untied Ribbons

Ysra returned from a lengthy coastal journey to find Jalen elderly and using a cane. His daughter had taken over most repair work. He still played well on good evenings and had kept a box of unfinished instruments for tasks he hoped to resume.

They made a final short journey together to **Ribbon Bridge**, a small crossing where travellers tied cloth strips for people expected home. Jalen removed a ribbon he had left for his uncle decades before. He gave Ysra a repaired travelling fiddle and asked her to return his unfinished work to those who could complete it.

He died at home during the following years. Ysra visited his family before setting out again. She did not inherit their grief or authority over their household simply because she remembered a version of him they had never known.

**Traces and stories:** Repair notes dividing work among apprentices; a bridge song with a verse about taking a ribbon down; a fiddle with a replaced neck. A restoration commission can reveal several owners' lives rather than one pristine heroic object.

**GM truth:** Jalen left a few tasks unfinished because he had lost interest, not because every item concealed a final wish. His daughter chose which were worth completing. Ysra learned to respect that choice.

### 1,061–1,049: The Seven-Month War

A dispute over armed escorts and toll collection escalated between Highmead's crown and South Vey's margravate. Both claimed that a stretch of road called the **Linden Corridor** was necessary to protect their traders. Both had supporters within its villages. The Reedbank Association tried to maintain passage while refusing exclusive access to either army.

The principal fighting lasted seven months, although requisitions and local violence extended beyond them. Soldiers burned stores, damaged mills, and seized animals. Two significant battles decided control of crossings; neither produced the sweeping conquest later commemorated in royal art.

At **Darnel Field**, **Captain Edris Voss** of Highmead accepted a withdrawal agreement proposed by **Lady Saela Halren** after both sides ran short of provisions. A negotiated peace left the corridor under local administration with limits on permanent garrisons.

**Traces and stories:** Requisition tallies; a battlefield beneath later orchards; contradictory muster rolls. Recovering a soldier's effects may identify someone counted as a deserter who was buried under the wrong unit's name.

**GM truth:** There was no hidden mastermind requiring the war to occur. Political ambition, fearful assumptions, and profitable opportunities were enough. Several leaders understood the costs and continued anyway.

### 1,048–1,036: The Mill That Ground for Both Banks

Peace left damaged infrastructure and families unable to buy new animals. At **Willowrace**, a mill on a river branch serving the former war corridor, **millkeeper Osa Trent** reopened the surviving mechanism with parts salvaged from two ruined mills on opposite sides of the conflict.

Her users included widows, former soldiers, displaced tenants, and landholders who had supported the fighting. She published a temporary reduced charge and accepted labour where households could not pay in grain. The work strained her family's reserves and depended on contributions she was sometimes too proud to request.

The rebuilt mill became a meeting place where practical cooperation preceded forgiveness. Memorial names were added to its yard wall without sorting them by allegiance. Some households refused to attend the dedication but still brought grain the following week.

**Traces and stories:** Mismatched mill fittings; a wall bearing names in different hands; repair accounts connecting formerly hostile villages. A modern restoration may uncover why a particular family's name was added later and in smaller letters.

**GM truth:** Osa's teenage son had served in an abusive requisition party. She did not conceal his service, and some neighbours continued blaming him. Her useful work did not automatically resolve that injury.

### 1,035–1,023: The Hound of Empty Houses

People resettling the war-damaged corridor reported a huge dark hound appearing at abandoned thresholds. It entered houses where an invitation had been spoken aloud, copied familiar voices from within, and attacked people who followed those voices away from company.

The creature had travelled with an old carved lintel salvaged into a roadside refuge. **Ward-maker Halen Sore**, **drover Bren Uld**, and Ysra identified a recurring invitation phrase in the accounts of survivors. They worked with local hosts to change how empty buildings were opened and trapped the creature within the lintel's threshold before destroying its bodily manifestation and breaking the binding object.

The hunt lasted several weeks. Its success allowed shelters to reopen, but did not instantly make frightened households comfortable sleeping alone.

**Traces and stories:** Door-opening customs; a burned lintel fragment documented in a later collection; a survivor's route sketch. A new haunting must be investigated on its own evidence rather than assumed to be the same hound returned.

**GM truth:** The hound was a predatory bound spirit exploiting a narrow permission. It was not the collective anger of war victims, and its destruction did not free every bereaved family from grief. No surviving intact binding is established.

### 1,022–1,010: The Children of the Guest Rooms

Displacement had left children living with distant relatives, foster households, and sanctuaries. Some knew their origins clearly; others possessed contradictory names and dates. In **Celandine House**, near the old corridor, **Matron Verra Kell** began keeping records of placements, continued family contact, and children old enough to express where they wished to remain.

The practice spread unevenly through Open Door Houses. It helped some families reconnect and exposed cases in which useful labour had been described as charitable care. Local authorities disagreed about their responsibility to people from outside their jurisdiction.

An annual gathering combined record review with ordinary games, meals, and repair of donated clothes. Later commemorations often omitted the practical work and showed only a benevolent patron distributing gifts.

**Traces and stories:** Placement books with corrections; paired household tokens; a courtyard measuring post. A family history inquiry can uncover consent, coercion, or a chosen home without reducing every foster relationship to either rescue or wrongdoing.

**GM truth:** Verra protected some private information because publishing it would expose families to retaliation. Missing details are not automatically evidence of falsehood; some were deliberately kept in a separate, restricted record.

### 1,009–1,000: The Winter of the Pale Roofs

Several hard winters culminated in snow damage along the northern approaches. The Roven Marches closed one high route and concentrated effort on a lower alternative through Tallow Ford. Hazelstairs remained a seasonal resource; abandoned terraces could not simply be returned to profitable farming by decree.

**Roadwarden Nella Dorn** organised shared repair crews and public tallies of available fodder. Whitewindow sent fittings, tools, and workers, while lower valleys supplied food. The arrangement helped but did not reach every isolated household before its stores failed.

At **Hearthscar Shelter**, a half-collapsed old hall became a refuge large enough to accommodate stranded carriers and their animals separately. Its occupants argued about smoke, space, and whose turn it was to fetch water. Those arguments were remembered fondly by people who survived to tell them.

**Traces and stories:** Snow-load braces incorporated into later roofs; route closures dated in courier books; shared tool marks. A modern winter problem can be approached through known lower routes and realistic limits rather than treating every old pass as permanently accessible.

**GM truth:** Nella ordered one unsafe bridge abandoned despite an isolated family's request to keep using it. The family survived by another route. That fortunate outcome does not make the decision painless or prove that every similar choice will succeed.

## Third century: The Work of Peace — 999–900 years ago

### 999–988: The Stonecutters' Spring

Several former repair crews stayed together after the northern emergency and accepted work across jurisdictions. Their **Company of the Common Plumb** began as a small association sharing specialist tools, injury support, and responsibility for inspecting one another's work.

Its best-known early project was a replacement arch near Tallow Ford. **Mason Idda Coal** insisted that the opening ceremony wait until the mortar and temporary supports were ready. A patron complained that the best guests would have departed. The bridge opened later, with fewer banners and no collapse.

The company trained local apprentices and recorded unusual foundations for whoever maintained them next. Its reputation spread farther than its actual workforce. Unaffiliated builders soon copied its mark, some because they admired it and others because it helped win contracts.

**Traces and stories:** A plumb-shaped mason's mark; foundation notes; tool-sharing accounts. Distinguishing a genuine company inspection from an imitation can matter when deciding whether an old bridge is safe to reopen.

**GM truth:** The company's early work included repairs and occasional mistakes. Its mark indicates a particular history of responsibility, not an enchantment guaranteeing eternal structural soundness.

### 987–975: The Dowry of Maps

**Lady Orienne Merrow**, a cousin of Highmead's sovereign, married **Councillor Vasil Dast**, a member of a prominent Selvari merchant household. Their marriage connected court and commercial interests without creating a union of kingdoms.

Orienne brought copied route books, estate surveys, and an interest in gardens. Vasil brought access to carriers and an alarming habit of discussing prices during ceremonial meals. Together they funded lodging repairs along a route between Istravel and the Selvari town of **Namar Vale**.

Their most useful gift was permission for participating hosts to keep corrected working copies of the maps. Courtiers admired illuminated originals; carriers valued pages on which someone had marked a well gone brackish or a bridge replaced downstream.

**Traces and stories:** Different editions of a wedding atlas; lodging accounts; paired garden designs adapted to different climates. A beautiful map may omit the corrections needed for a journey, while an unattractive copy provides the useful route.

**GM truth:** Some financed houses served the couple's commercial partners preferentially. The work improved travel and advanced private interests at the same time. Later users can benefit without accepting the patrons' entire public legend.

### 974–962: The Empty Chair at Whitewindow

Whitewindow's guest hall received an elaborate chair from a neighbouring ruler who expected it to become a seat of honour for his representative. The hold accepted the craftsmanship and declined the implied political arrangement. For several years the chair stood unused beside a wall while negotiations continued.

Guestmaster **Dorra Vel** eventually placed it in a quiet room for visitors waiting on difficult news. The donor's household protested, then discovered that the new use had become locally respected. People brought cushions, blankets, and small repaired toys for those waiting there.

The episode helped clarify a guest agreement: accepting gifts or shelter did not itself establish obedience, religious allegiance, or an inherited service obligation. The principle had precedents in the Open Door Houses, but Whitewindow expressed it through its own law and customs.

**Traces and stories:** A chair repaired repeatedly; competing ceremonial descriptions; a guest-law commentary. An inherited gift with an alleged hidden obligation can prompt a search for what was actually offered and accepted.

**GM truth:** The chair was never cursed. Its political meaning was an attempted expectation, not magical compulsion. Its quiet later use was a deliberate act of hospitality rather than a disguised insult.

### 961–949: The Scent of a House That Was Gone

Ysra returned to Bluecup Hollow and found the damp limestone pocket partly buried beneath road spoil. The flower survived in a few sheltered cracks. She worked with local households to remove unstable debris and propagate a small number of plants without stripping the wild site.

A prepared linen square among her belongings still held a faint scent recorded during her first journeys with Jalen: resin, warm wood, and the herbs hanging in his workshop. She gave it to his great-granddaughter **Tera Fen**, who had inherited the family tools but knew the room only from stories.

The gift prompted Tera to copy repair notes that were beginning to deteriorate. A small sensory wonder led to ordinary preservation work whose usefulness outlasted the charm.

**Traces and stories:** Nursery notes naming the restored pocket; a tools inventory linked to older notebooks; accounts of a cloth whose scent later faded. Reconstructing the charm requires a living source to record and cannot recover every lost smell on command.

**GM truth:** The cloth did not preserve Jalen's soul or reveal scenes of his life. Tera's response belonged to her own imagination and family knowledge. The wild flower needs a specific habitat; planting it everywhere is not a solution.

### 948–936: The Pilgrimage of the Unlit Lamps

A severe storm damaged shrines and guesthouses along a coastal road. During the rebuilding, several religious communities organised a shared walk carrying unlit lamps, collecting oil and repair materials for places unable to provide shelter.

At **Morrowstep Sanctuary**, **Sister Arel Venn** proposed that the walk end with a meal open to people who had contributed labour as well as money. Some participants treated it as worship; others regarded it as solidarity with hosts and travellers. The organisers agreed on conduct without settling every belief.

The pilgrimage became an occasional event in difficult years. It did not remain a single uninterrupted annual tradition. Different later communities adopted its image for purposes ranging from relief to political protest.

**Traces and stories:** Lamps with names scratched beneath the base; repair lists; a walking song whose verses change by district. Recovering the original use of a disputed emblem can complicate a modern claim to exclusive ownership of it.

**GM truth:** One sanctuary's account of a lamp lighting itself is unsupported by the surviving witnesses, while Little Lastlight's modest activity elsewhere is real. This world contains both genuine wonders and sincere reports that evidence does not confirm.

### 935–923: The Cellar Beneath the Seventh Hearth

Repairs at the Seventh Hearth revealed a sealed shallow cellar beneath a later kitchen floor. It contained fragments of Iven's pottery, household accounts from several periods, and a wooden box whose contents had been moved there long after his lifetime.

The box held **a copy of an inventory associated with Aveline's undelivered parcel**. It listed a folded letter, a small wrapped object, and pages identified only by a workshop mark. The record placed the parcel with a coastal carrier after her death but did not establish where it finally went.

**Carpenter Oren Vask** and **copyist Sella Rinn** recorded the find. They returned the house's ordinary utensils to storage rather than stripping it into a museum. Copies of the inventory travelled to Sallowmere and Saint Meret's Rest, each acquiring later annotations.

**Traces and stories:** A documented cellar discovery; two related inventory copies; a carrier's mark that changed over time. A future search gains a concrete next step without requiring the parcel or its contents to be found in this chapter.

**GM truth:** The cellar box was not the parcel. The plain travel cup Aveline left at the house remained there, subject to later repair and custody. The inventory does not confirm that the wrapped object was magical or that any recorded voice existed inside it.

### 922–910: The Rain at Darnel Field

Unusually heavy rain exposed graves along the edge of Darnel Field, more than a century after the Seven-Month War. Orchard workers found personal objects with the remains. Some families wanted a grand military memorial; others wanted individual names recovered before any ceremony was planned.

**Schoolteacher Mara Pellin** compared muster rolls, local burial notices, and Celandine House's placement records. The work identified several people who had disappeared from family accounts under inaccurate names. Many remained unknown.

The resulting memorial left spaces for later identifications and recorded occupations where military allegiance was uncertain. An annual orchard meal helped fund its care. The place remained a working landscape as well as a burial ground.

**Traces and stories:** A memorial with intentionally blank panels; copied identity comparisons; fruit trees planted around protected graves. A new discovery can fill one blank without revealing an entire lost regiment or overturning the whole war's history.

**GM truth:** One apparent officer's burial belonged to a civilian carter wearing borrowed clothing when he died. Careful identification matters because impressive equipment can produce a persuasive but wrong story.

### 909–900: The Fair of Three Roads

At the close of the period, Alderwake hosted a fair bringing together carriers from the coast, the northern passes, and the orchard country. Participants renewed selected arrangements for lodging, public measures, disputed claims, and escort contributions. They did not resurrect the first Lantern League as if its failures had never happened.

The agreements recognised different local practices while specifying what visiting traders could expect at participating places. **Fairwarden Delia Ross** insisted on publicly readable summaries as well as formal copies. Tavern versions were immediately shorter, funnier, and less accurate.

Ysra attended at roughly 410 years of age, carrying Jalen's repaired fiddle. She then accepted a place with a northern botanical expedition beyond the familiar Roven routes. Her later life remains for subsequent centuries; this chapter does not establish her survival into the campaign present.

**Traces and stories:** Three-road marks on seals; stall records naming distant hometowns; an expedition supply list. A present party might follow a regional edition of the fair agreements to identify an overlooked stopping place or trace Ysra's next journey.

**GM truth:** The fair's legacy depends on later renewals, adaptations, and failures. Its most durable immediate achievement was bringing people into direct contact who had previously understood one another through prices and rumours.

## Places to add to the world

These destinations extend the existing setting without fixing the final continent map. Selvar lies east of Serevan on the farther shore. The Linden Corridor connects upland Highmead interests with South Vey's road network, with river branches and crossings influenced by Reedbank communities. It is not a replacement name for the Lantern Road, which still ends at Bramblebridge in the campaign present.

Present-day forms below are working adventure locations. Rebuilding, copied records, changing institutions, and custody explain what remains. None assumes that an ancient human figure is alive now or that a settlement has remained unchanged for nine centuries.

### Lastlight Pier — the little landing beyond the cargo sheds

**Ancient role:** A disused Leth Sarai pier maintained by Faris Nemm, later home to the small lamp spirit Little Lastlight.

**Present-day form:** A modest landing rebuilt along the old line, used by local fishers and people walking the shore. The lamp has successive outer housings around an older internal bracket. Supplies and maintenance have passed between households; the spirit's continued residence is a working present-day premise, not evidence that its wooden shelter survived untouched.

**A person to meet:** **Lamp keeper Sabi Nemm** shares the founder's surname but relies on neighbourhood trust rather than a proven hereditary office. She wants the light kept useful and dislikes visitors demanding a miracle before donating oil.

**Adventure:** The pier needs moving away from eroding ground. Lastlight recognises the old approach and worries that people will fail to find the new landing. Preparing a visible transition and teaching it the changed route can be part of the solution.

### Namar Vale — the town of shaded courtyards

**Ancient role:** A Selvari market town connected to Istravel by the lodging route improved through the Dowry of Maps.

**Present-day form:** A settlement of shaded yards, carriers' lodging, potteries, and leaf warehouses near irrigated cultivation. Fine imported goods pass through, but most residents earn a living from ordinary growing, carrying, repairs, and food. Old survey lines are preserved unevenly beneath later subdivisions.

**A person to meet:** **Carrier Laleh Dast** has inherited a useful set of route corrections through her business, not the entire authority of the old marriage settlement. She wants a fair price for sharing work that keeps people safe.

**Adventure:** A restored ceremonial atlas sends a caravan toward a failed well. Laleh's plain working copy suggests alternatives, but one crosses land whose current occupants need to be consulted.

### Veyr's Lantern — the island with a dark observing court

**Ancient role:** A small island north of the usual Morrow Strait crossing, where a damaged glass court became a hazard by projecting an old star pattern onto tidal water.

**Present-day form:** A rough landing, seabird cliffs, and partially buried foundations reached only in suitable conditions. Erosion has changed access and may uncover screened glass. No continuously inhabited lighthouse settlement is established by its name.

**A person to meet:** **Pilot Enna Avet** has compared later warnings with current soundings and wants a survey before another salvage crew treats the site as a safe anchorage.

**Adventure:** Reach the island, make the reflecting surface safe, and document exposed inscriptions before the next weather window closes. Further investigation of the observing court can follow once the immediate maritime danger is addressed.

### Bluecup Hollow — the pocket garden beneath the limestone

**Ancient role:** A damp limestone hollow where Ysra and Jalen found a flower used in a scent-preserving charm, later restored after road spoil threatened it.

**Present-day form:** A small protected patch amid working land near the routes between the orchard country and the northern foothills. Propagated plants grow in a nearby nursery, but the wild pocket retains conditions difficult to reproduce elsewhere. The ancient companions' exact collecting spot is only approximately known.

**A person to meet:** **Nursery keeper Benna Varn** maintains access agreements and permits careful study. She is saving for a drainage repair that preserves the wet pocket rather than drying it out.

**Adventure:** A wealthy buyer offers to fund repairs in return for all future flowers. An alternative agreement could meet the nursery's needs while protecting local access and a small supply for practitioners.

### Ribbon Bridge — the crossing for taking leave

**Ancient role:** A small bridge on a local route near Saint Meret's Rest, where people marked expected returns with cloth and Jalen finally removed his uncle's ribbon.

**Present-day form:** Several replacement bridges have occupied the crossing. Ribbons are now tied to a separate frame so they do not obstruct inspections. Households remove them after a return, a confirmed death, or a decision to stop waiting; the meanings differ and are not publicly policed.

**A person to meet:** **Bridge tender Arlo Fen** keeps fallen ribbons dry until someone claims them. He knows many local stories but will not share private explanations simply because a stranger is curious.

**Adventure:** A preserved ribbon contains a stitched mark matching an unresolved sanctuary case. Following the connection can help a family choose how to remember someone without promising a reunion.

### Willowrace — the mill of mismatched stones

**Ancient role:** A branch-river mill rebuilt from equipment salvaged across the Seven-Month War's opposing sides.

**Present-day form:** A working mill occupying a repeatedly repaired site in the former Linden Corridor. Its oldest documented fittings are now displayed safely apart from the operating machinery. A memorial wall stands along the yard's quiet edge, while wagons continue arriving with grain.

**A person to meet:** **Millkeeper Dena Trent** wants to restore the memorial and keep the business viable. She dislikes the assumption that charging for work dishonours Osa's generosity.

**Adventure:** A damaged supply channel exposes timbers marked with a former owner's name. Claims over historical salvage complicate repairs urgently needed by several present households.

### Celandine House — the courtyard with the measuring post

**Ancient role:** A guesthouse and care institution that recorded family placements and children's circumstances after wartime displacement.

**Present-day form:** A modest local institution with rebuilt accommodation, gardens, and a small archive. It has changed governance and function more than once. Some early records survive as copied extracts in other collections, with private details separated from public registers.

**A person to meet:** **Archivist Teva Kell** helps families investigate their history and explains what the evidence does not establish. She must also decide which recent records can be shared appropriately.

**Adventure:** Two branches of a family bring incompatible names for the same ancestor. Comparing public and legitimately accessible restricted material may reveal a chosen new household rather than fraud or a hidden noble title.

### Hearthscar Shelter — the hall below the snow line

**Ancient role:** An old hall repaired into an emergency refuge during the Winter of the Pale Roofs.

**Present-day form:** A maintained northern shelter on the lower alternative to a difficult pass. Human and animal accommodation are separate. Stores depend on deliveries and honest use. Older braces and wall scars remain visible among later work.

**A person to meet:** **Shelter keeper Runa Dorn** asks departing visitors to report depleted supplies at the next settlement. She has little patience for people who call that mundane request less important than a grand charitable gesture.

**Adventure:** Missing replenishment threatens a group sheltering after an early storm. The cause may involve a broken wagon, a misunderstood closure notice, or competing emergencies along the route.

### Morrowstep Sanctuary — the house above the storm road

**Ancient role:** A coastal religious house that helped organise the Pilgrimage of the Unlit Lamps and repair neighbouring shelters.

**Present-day form:** A small sanctuary above a coastal road serving the western approaches to Morrow Strait. Its guest rooms and workshop are used more often than its ceremonial hall. Different communities preserve different explanations of the unlit-lamp emblem.

**A person to meet:** **Sister Melren Sore** arranges practical relief and hopes to host a shared repair gathering without allowing patrons to turn it into a contest of public piety.

**Adventure:** An old store of lamps is offered for sale as sacred treasure. Identifying the objects can support their return, lawful purchase, or continued use in a new relief effort rather than assuming every old religious object belongs in one place forever.

### Darnel Field — the orchard with unfinished names

**Ancient role:** A battlefield of the Seven-Month War, later the focus of identification work and a memorial leaving space for unresolved names.

**Present-day form:** A working orchard beside a protected burial area. The memorial has been repaired and partially recopied; original fragments are stored locally. Later ownership changes explain why some graves lie beyond the oldest recorded boundary.

**A person to meet:** **Teacher Ilva Ross** works with the orchard's occupants to document finds and update a local history used by children. She wants corrections to remain visible instead of replacing one confident legend with another.

**Adventure:** Storm damage exposes an object matching a sanctuary notice. Establishing whose it was requires comparison of work, family, and military records. A single recovered name can be the reward.

## Longer stories growing from the period

**The parcel's next shore.** Compare the Seventh Hearth inventory with the Sallowmere and sanctuary copies. Identify when the coastal carrier used the recorded mark, then follow a route toward Istravel. The investigation can reveal a change of ship, storage, or intended recipient. The parcel remains a bounded mystery, not proof that Aveline engineered a quest centuries after her death.

**Three maps and a missing well.** A ceremonial wedding atlas, a carrier's corrected copy, and a regional Book of Roads Left Open disagree. Travellers must establish which details remain useful. The journey connects people who each know part of the route and can end with a practical new edition shared under fair terms.

**A border after the war.** The Linden Corridor offers ruins, divided local memories, resettled families, and shared infrastructure. An immediate need such as repairing Willowrace or locating a safe crossing gives a reason to investigate the history. No current inhabitant automatically inherits an ancestor's guilt.

**The names beneath the fruit trees.** Begin with one object exposed at Darnel Field. Follow a muster error through Celandine House and Saint Meret's Rest to a family account. The discovery may reunite objects with a community or allow a corrected memorial, without requiring an enormous treasure reward.

**The light that needs moving.** Lastlight's pier is threatened by erosion. Surveying a new landing, arranging maintenance, and helping a small spirit understand the change provide a gentle local adventure. A storm can add urgency, but the story need not threaten an entire coast.

**The wrong sky.** Renewed exposure at Veyr's Lantern endangers navigation. A party must work with pilots, respect weather windows, and neutralise a specific effect before exploring deeper. The old observing court offers further mysteries after the practical problem is solved.

**The flower and the ordinary room.** Reconstruct a scent charm through Ysra and Jalen's notes while protecting Bluecup Hollow. A patron may want to preserve a present home before moving, rather than recover an impossible perfect past. The spell's modest limits make the choice of what to record more meaningful.

**Who keeps the shelter?** Hearthscar's stores run low after an unexpected closure. Communities disagree about contributions because each remembers providing more than it received. Records can clarify the past; an actual delivery and a workable future arrangement keep people safe now.

## Caldris at 900 years ago

The roads have survived because people keep changing them. Some old alignments are local tracks; some new bridges incorporate the stone of failed projects. The two shores exchange working knowledge as well as fashionable goods. Maps are better in places and outdated in others before their ink has faded.

Peace has repaired much of the Linden Corridor without erasing its losses. Highmead's crown endures under a different household. South Vey and the Reedbank communities retain their own interests. Northern travel relies on shelters, cooperation, and an honest acknowledgement of winter.

Aveline has been dead for 510 years. Her legacy belongs to copies, objects, places, and people choosing what to preserve. Ysra, whose life began long after Aveline's death, is setting out on a journey whose ending is not yet written. Long lives connect generations; they do not make every loss reversible.

There is a small lamp beside the sea that still needs somebody to bring oil. There is a bridge where taking a ribbon down can mean that a person came home, or that those waiting have finally learned what happened. There are fair tables where strangers begin with prices and occasionally end with an invitation to supper.

Those are the promises the next centuries inherit.
