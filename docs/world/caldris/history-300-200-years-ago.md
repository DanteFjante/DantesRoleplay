# Caldris: The Century of Distant Hearths

*300–200 years before the campaign present · Authored world lore · Includes GM secrets*

Continues [The Roads of Salt and Rain](history-400-300-years-ago.md). Dates count backwards from the campaign present. Present-day people and adventure premises are available setting material, not events already occurring in the campaign. Private explanations are labelled **GM truth**.

The twelve chronological entries, six mapped locations, historical accounts, and separate GM story material are published in live Caldris. Map anchors illustrate placement within parent locations and do not establish travel distances. Character knowledge and campaign progress are unchanged.

Continues in [The Century of Gathering Roads: 200–100 years ago](history-200-100-years-ago.md).

## The cost of being connected

Three hundred years ago, distant harvests influenced the price of a meal in Highmead. A port dispute on Solasca could leave a northern shelter short of lamp oil. Merchant houses offered speed and security, but villages along the quieter roads increasingly depended on decisions made somewhere they would never visit.

Local powers still mattered. Crowns could close gates more easily than they could make merchants bring goods through them. Towns borrowed against future tolls; religious houses offered shelter while arguing about the cost; carriers maintained friendships that outlasted the companies employing them. Highmead's regional political history remained one strand among many across Caldris.

Old magic entered a different kind of inheritance. People increasingly possessed enchanted things without knowing their first purpose. Some objects remained useful, some failed quietly, and a few became dangerous when moved. The best repairer might be an ordinary craftsperson patient enough to ask what had changed around an old spell.

This century follows people learning to live with those connections. Its history contains shortages, rescues, ambitious businesses, departures that become permanent, and meals shared by travellers who never learn how much they altered one another's lives.

## 299–292: The Wool That Arrived Too Late

A wet season damaged stored fleeces in the northern valleys just as Highmead cloth merchants had promised large deliveries to coastal buyers. **Wool factor Teren Bale** bought substitute stock farther away, paying advances that several smaller workshops could not match. Cloth prices rose while growers with spoiled wool struggled to buy winter grain.

**Spinner Edda Rusk** organised workshop purchases of mixed-quality fleece and accepted slower, less profitable work. Their coarse blankets sold to carriers and shelters that could not afford fine cloth. Several workshops survived, although their workers earned less and some households left the trade.

The following year brought abundant wool and falling prices. Growers who had expanded on borrowed money discovered that a good harvest could also bring ruin. Later songs remembered the wet winter and often omitted the difficult summer that followed.

**Traces and stories:** Advance contracts, samples of mixed cloth, and abandoned fulling sites. An inherited debt may originate in an apparently prosperous year. A commission to identify an old blanket can connect a traveller to the small workshops that made survival possible without becoming rich.

**GM truth:** Teren anticipated the shortage accurately and still nearly failed because a major buyer delayed payment. He later claimed to have financed Edda's purchases; he had refused the request. Her actual guarantors were three carrier households whose names survive on a damaged joint bond.

## 291–284: The Winter Latch

Heavy snow trapped travellers near the lower approach to the Unfinished Stair. **Aurr**, still living in the Veyr uplands during this period, opened a stone shelter used for storing tools and fodder. The giant could move a fallen lintel that the travellers could not, but could not supply enough food for everyone.

**Carrier Ossa Renn** led two journeys down a sheltered descent, bringing provisions and returning with help. Aurr marked safe footholds at a scale human travellers could use. After the thaw, valley households built a smaller refuge nearby, called **the Winter Latch**, and arranged seasonal stores.

The stair above remained unfinished. Successful cooperation did not persuade Aurr to surrender the burial shelf that had stopped the earlier road. The refuge supported a modest route, not the unrestricted passage its most enthusiastic sponsors advertised.

**Traces and stories:** Giant-cut footholds, supply rosters, and a lintel carrying both human and oversized tool marks. A present supply journey can rediscover which descent is safe after later changes. The refuge's existence does not prove that the upper stair ever became a caravan road.

**GM truth:** Aurr had already stored food for an expected visit from another giant household and shared part of it. The rescue cost the giant something. Records do not establish Aurr's eventual death or present whereabouts; later encounters should not assume an immortal guardian waiting at the same door.

## 283–276: The Last Measure in Willowrace

The dwarven musician and instrument maker **Orven Dast** died peacefully in **279 years before the campaign present**, after a life extending across more than three centuries. His final workshop instructions concerned unfinished repairs, deposits to return, and which instruments had been lent rather than sold.

His former pupil **Ilda Kett** completed a plain travelling fiddle he had left unfinished. The workbench became known as **the Last Measure Workshop**, although Orven had repeatedly objected to the idea that his last work must be his finest. Ilda preserved both his celebrated arrangements and the easier teaching versions he made after retiring from public performance.

The first memorial gathering at Three-Reed Bridge included several awkward young players. Their teachers insisted that a tribute to someone who had spent decades teaching should make room for people still learning.

**Traces and stories:** Deposit lists, unfinished components, and teaching copies with corrections in several hands. Returning an instrument to the proper successor household can be a meaningful quest. Establishing the history of a repair may matter more than proving that every part came from Orven's bench.

**GM truth:** Orven's death is natural and final in this history. The travelling fiddle contains no hidden soul or message from beyond death. Ilda quietly changed its neck dimensions to suit the intended player, fulfilling the practical purpose of the commission rather than preserving every unfinished choice.

## 275–268: The Opening of Ashglass House

In Istravel, **glassmaker Sena Dorr** and mage **Arvel Senn** founded **Ashglass House**, a workshop specialising in the repair and repurposing of small enchanted glass objects. They studied records of the glass-moth failures and avoided several older binding pigments where reliable alternatives existed.

Their most useful product was an ordinary-looking vessel that released a little stored warmth gradually. It could keep a drink pleasant during a short journey; it could not heat a house or make winter travel safe by itself. Producing consistent vessels proved harder than making a spectacular demonstration piece.

The founders sold conventional glass to support experiments. Apprentices learned to label unreliable batches, and some failed objects were dismantled rather than sold with optimistic explanations. A costly dispute followed when a patron demanded the original enchantment remain untouched during a repair that required replacing its cracked support.

**Traces and stories:** Batch marks, repair assessments, and objects with both old and replacement components. A present commission can reveal that a famous relic has survived through several thoughtful alterations. Finding an original recipe may help, but preserving the original material is not always the safest repair.

**GM truth:** Arvel once concealed a failed batch to protect the workshop's reputation. Sena discovered it before sale and made the disposal part of the accounts. Their partnership survived the dispute, but the later tale that they never disagreed was invented for an anniversary celebration.

## 267–260: The Birds in Cinder Meadow

A dry-season fire crossed part of Boughmeet's nursery country. Growers saved cuttings and tools, but several plots burned. During the first rains, witnesses saw slender birds with ember-bright throats searching the ash. Their footprints cooled into faint glassy impressions in exposed sand.

**Nursery keeper Pera Elven** fenced a small nesting patch rather than allowing collectors to chase the birds through newly planted ground. Most departed within weeks. One pair remained until its young could fly, and the damaged plot acquired the name **Cinder Meadow**.

The nursery recovered through ordinary planting and exchanged stock. The birds became a symbol of renewal, although their appearance had neither prevented the fire nor restored the lost trees. Families whose livelihoods had burned did not all welcome cheerful songs about the disaster.

**Traces and stories:** Preserved cuttings, fire boundaries, glassy footprints, and several incompatible bird illustrations. An expedition might identify a later nesting site, protect it from collectors, or recover a surviving nursery variety thought lost in the fire.

**GM truth:** The birds were rare magical animals known locally as **ember herons**, attracted to warm mineral soils after fire. They do not resurrect people, and burning another meadow would not reliably summon them. A few properly documented impressions could teach a careful observer more than a costly supposed feather bought without provenance.

## 259–252: Salt Without Sails

A dispute over harbour charges on the Serevan coast led several merchant houses to withhold sailings. Officials expected a short concession struggle. Instead, the interruption lasted through an important preserving season, raising salt prices in some inland markets while salt workers near idle quays struggled to sell their stock.

**Caravan organiser Dema Noll** arranged smaller shipments through secondary landings and inland roads. The journeys were costly and exposed carriers to theft. Some officials tolerated them; others seized goods to demonstrate that the embargo still meant something.

The eventual settlement reduced selected charges and changed how harbour repairs were funded. The temporary routes did not all disappear. A handful of overlooked villages acquired inns, workshops, and customers, while the older route lost part of its dependable trade.

**Traces and stories:** Emergency freight agreements, concealed landing places, and warehouses built for a prosperity that did not always last. A forgotten route can support a present journey, but its old existence does not guarantee that bridges, permissions, or water levels remain suitable.

**GM truth:** Dema carried both necessary household supplies and valuable luxury goods under the same arrangements. The profits from the latter helped finance the former. Later admirers removed the luxury cargo from the story, making her work look less commercially complicated than it was.

## 251–244: The Evening Market of Brinefold

Brinefold set aside a quay-side yard for the sale of used equipment, small return cargoes, and household goods. Its **Evening Market** opened when workers finished their daily labour. A carpenter could sell spare fittings, a sailor trade an unfamiliar cooking pot, and a widow dispose of business stock without renting a permanent warehouse stall.

**Market keeper Jori Fen** required disputed goods to remain available for inspection instead of vanishing into immediate resale. The rule inconvenienced honest sellers as well as thieves. Regular traders developed a habit of asking an object's journey before asking its price.

The market became a place where foreign fashions arrived piecemeal. A damaged warming cup from Solasca might sit beside an ordinary northern blanket, both carrying more history than their sellers knew.

**Traces and stories:** Small sale tickets, repair labels, and objects bearing successive owners' marks. A modest purchase can begin a journey to identify a maker or return something borrowed generations earlier. An apparently sinister object may simply have been misunderstood when its instructions were lost.

**GM truth:** Jori sometimes waived inspection charges for sellers in immediate need, recording the shortfall as his own debt. One surviving account can look like theft until the corresponding household receipts are compared. Compassion did not spare him from having to explain the money.

## 243–236: The Letters That Wintered Abroad

Bad weather and disrupted sailings stranded a collection of private correspondence in Leth Sarai. **Host and copyist Mira Vell** rented a dry room where carriers could deposit letters awaiting a reliable route. Senders paid small fees; local households sponsored deliveries for people without ready money.

The arrangement grew into **the House of Salt Letters**, named for the journeys its contents had yet to cross. Mira kept letters sealed unless a sender had explicitly commissioned a copy. When a recipient died, she sought instructions from the sender or a recognised household representative rather than treating grief as permission to publish private words.

Some letters finally arrived after their urgent business had passed. Others established new friendships between people who wrote replies explaining why the intended recipient could no longer answer.

**Traces and stories:** Carrier receipts, returned envelopes, and forwarding lists. A present delivery can trace a household through changes of name and residence. Recovering a packet is only the beginning; determining who should receive it can be the more difficult task.

**GM truth:** One celebrated cache of unsent love letters was left deliberately unposted by its author. A clerk preserved it under a storage agreement, not through negligence. Its survival does not establish consent for strangers to read or circulate it.

## 235–228: The Hearing Beneath the Old Banner

An ageing road grant was invoked to collect payments from settlements now served by several different routes. Highmead's regional court heard claims from landholders, carriers, maintenance houses, and towns whose practical obligations had diverged over generations.

**Judge Tessa Dorn** refused to treat the earliest document as the complete answer. The court compared later renewals, actual repair work, and agreements made by successor authorities. The resulting settlement preserved some payments, ended others, and required fresh negotiation where the evidence was contradictory.

Petitioners lodged in the city for months. Bakers, hosts, and money changers benefited from the gathering, while families at home absorbed the cost of absent workers. The hearing became a political landmark and a collection of private disappointments.

**Traces and stories:** Annotated renewals, lodging accounts, and unfinished petitions. A quest might recover evidence of a bridge repair that altered later obligations. Discovering an ancient charter can matter without automatically placing every living household under its oldest wording.

**GM truth:** Tessa's compromise knowingly left one small settlement in a weak position because its crucial witnesses could not be found before the hearing closed. Their testimony remains a plausible archival discovery. Whether it changes a present claim depends on what happened during the intervening centuries.

## 227–220: The Day Little Lastlight Went Dark

Little Lastlight failed to appear at the repaired pier in Leth Sarai. The household tending the lamp had changed, and the replacement keeper faithfully performed a ceremony learned from a popular printed account. Fishers installed ordinary lamps while neighbours investigated.

**Lamp keeper Hadi Renn** compared older maintenance notes with accounts from people who had known previous keepers. The bracket had been turned toward a handsome new approach that the local fishing boats did not use. After several evenings of patient work at the working landing, the light returned.

The episode weakened a fashionable story that the spirit's aid depended on an elaborate ancient rite. It also reminded the district that accommodating a local wonder required noticing the place where it actually lived.

**Traces and stories:** Conflicting ceremony sheets, altered brackets, and a temporary lighting rota. A present failure might arise from changed surroundings, interrupted care, or a different cause altogether. Investigation should establish which, rather than replaying this episode as a universal solution.

**GM truth:** Little Lastlight recognised repeated practical activity at the landing more readily than ceremonial language. Its return was a response, not a spell anyone can reproduce by copying Hadi's gestures. The ordinary lamps continued in use because reliance on one spirit left real gaps.

## 219–212: Nivren's Unremarkable Journey

Nivren Sael joined a small carrier group crossing from the Boughmeet country toward Willowrace. He carried no urgent royal message and sought no lost fortress. A damaged bow case needed repair, and he wanted to hear how the bridge musicians were arranging Orven's work after his death.

**Carrier Lessa Morn** charged the usual fare and asked him to share the cooking. Along the way, Nivren taught a child the first part of the boot-drying charm and learned a local bean dish from a driver who disliked travelling meals that tasted identical every night.

At Willowrace, he found that one of Orven's simplified teaching versions had become more widely played than the difficult arrangement he remembered. He bought a copy. The journey survives because Lessa kept a detailed household travel book, not because contemporaries thought it especially important.

**Traces and stories:** A bow-case repair, a travel book, and a score with a food stain beside a difficult passage. These objects can establish a long-lived traveller's route without revealing every year of his life. An ordinary stopping place may hold the most personal surviving account.

**GM truth:** Nivren initially found the changed arrangement disappointing. Hearing a beginner complete it altered his response. This was a small change of mind, not a final cure for grief or a permanent lesson he could never forget again. His present whereabouts remain unset.

## 211–200: The Tables Kept for Strangers

Hosts along several northern and coastal routes adopted a modest agreement to keep a few places available for delayed carriers during difficult weather. **Host Sera Kell** coordinated the first surviving set of reciprocal promises, accepting that some houses would provide more shelter than they received in return.

The arrangement required communication, stored food, and occasional payments between households. It broke down in places where sponsors treated the promise as free labour from innkeepers. Elsewhere it endured through revised contributions and limits clearly explained to travellers.

At two hundred years ago, a marked table could mean a chance to wait out rain among strangers with different destinations. The custom carried stories, recipes, warnings, and songs as effectively as the freight travelling beside them.

**Traces and stories:** Host marks, food purchases, reciprocal accounts, and complaints from excluded travellers. A present journey might restore contact with a house that has stopped answering, supply a refuge before bad weather, or uncover why one community withdrew from the arrangement.

**GM truth:** The agreement never covered every road or guaranteed a place to everyone. Several successful houses later advertised a grander founding promise than the records support. Repairing the custom can require new commitments from living people rather than merely exposing a forgotten obligation.

## Places to add to the world

### The Winter Latch — the refuge beneath the giant's road

**Present-day form:** A small maintained refuge near the lower approach to the Unfinished Stair in the Veyr uplands. Its walls incorporate stones placed during the old rescue. It serves a limited seasonal route and keeps an ordinary store of fuel, fodder, and food when local deliveries succeed.

**A person to meet:** Keeper **Nessa Renn** needs reliable observations of the sheltered descent after recent erosion. She is tired of maps that turn a difficult footpath into an inviting broad line.

**Adventure:** Deliver stores, locate a missing route marker, and assess whether pack animals can pass safely. The giant's former shelter may contain evidence of later visitors, but Aurr's presence must be established through current signs rather than assumed.

### The Last Measure Workshop — repairs after the master's death

**Present-day form:** An instrument workshop in Willowrace whose later owners preserve copies of Orven's deposit lists and teaching arrangements. The benches and tools have changed repeatedly. A few documented components survive in protected storage, while working instruments continue to be altered for living players.

**A person to meet:** Repairer **Bela Kett** wants to complete a disputed restoration without pretending that a replacement part is ancient. A patron prefers a more flattering certificate.

**Adventure:** Trace the ownership of a fiddle listed as borrowed, authenticate an old component, or recover a teaching arrangement held in another collection. The most satisfying outcome may be an instrument played again rather than sealed away.

### Ashglass House — a workshop for useful enchantments

**Present-day form:** A glass workshop in Istravel with a small repair room and a collection of clearly labelled failed experiments. Most of its business is ordinary glass. Its modest enchanted vessels require careful handling, compatible materials, and occasional renewal by someone who understands their particular binding.

**A person to meet:** Glassmaker **Mera Dorr** has a sound replacement for a cracked support but needs help persuading an owner that repair will change the object. She also wants a missing batch book recovered before accepting several inherited pieces.

**Adventure:** Obtain suitable material, trace an unreliable vessel's maker, or compare old mounting diagrams with the object now in hand. A powerful customer may want a dramatic restoration where a modest safe repair would serve better.

### Cinder Meadow — the nursery plot with glassy footprints

**Present-day form:** A recovering meadow and nursery margin in Boughmeet's growing country. Later fires and planting changes have altered the ground. A few protected impressions are documented as surviving evidence of the ember herons; visitors can also see casts that are clearly labelled as copies.

**A person to meet:** Nursery keeper **Tavi Elven** wants to establish whether reports of unusual birds concern ember herons or ordinary birds seen in poor light. He needs help protecting new grafts from enthusiastic visitors.

**Adventure:** Follow credible sightings, recover a threatened nursery variety, or investigate a trader selling supposed magical feathers. Finding the real birds need not lead to capture, and their nesting needs may conflict with a landowner's plans.

### The Evening Market — the quay of second journeys

**Present-day form:** A modest market yard in Brinefold where sailors, craftspeople, and households sell used equipment and small consignments after the working day. Inspection rules and fees reflect later amendments. Repaired awnings cover stalls carrying practical goods alongside occasional objects with unusual histories.

**A person to meet:** Market keeper **Oren Fen** needs a disputed object kept available while its ownership is investigated. He is also trying to stop a wealthy buyer reserving all the affordable stalls through intermediaries.

**Adventure:** Buy, identify, repair, or return an unfamiliar object. Follow its marks through a shipping agent, a workshop, and an older household account. An apparently trivial purchase can provide the reason for a journey with several possible endings.

### The House of Salt Letters — correspondence between crossings

**Present-day form:** A copying and forwarding house in Leth Sarai, with dry storage for letters awaiting reliable carriers. Its procedures distinguish delivery, copying, and private storage. Some historical packets remain sealed because later custodians have found no legitimate recipient or permission to open them.

**A person to meet:** Letter keeper **Dena Vell** wants a delivery completed to a household whose descendants have moved inland. She can pay a modest fee and provide careful forwarding evidence, but not a guaranteed welcome.

**Adventure:** Trace a recipient, reunite separated letters, or recover a carrier's missing packet. Decide who may properly receive the material before treating a private archive as a source of entertaining secrets. The correspondence can introduce a distant friendship, an old dispute, or a present obligation.

## At 200 years ago

Ysra has been dead for 394 years, and Orven for seventy-nine. Their work continues through people whose lives neither could have imagined. Nivren remains a possible living witness, but his memories are personal and incomplete; they do not replace the records and experiences of everyone else.

Aveline, Leris, and Nen retain their established identities and history. Aurr's eventual fate remains unwritten. The ember herons are rare creatures with particular needs, and Little Lastlight remains a local spirit rather than a guarantee of safe passage everywhere.

Trade has created useful alternatives and uneven dependencies. New routes have helped some places prosper and left others behind. Ordinary households continue maintaining promises that more powerful institutions advertise as their own achievements.

The next two centuries can bring these arrangements toward the campaign's present: changes of government, the later wars already remembered in the world, new ownership of old objects, and the practical work of keeping a welcome available when someone finally reaches the door.
