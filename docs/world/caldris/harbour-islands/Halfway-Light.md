# The Halfway Light

A lighthouse island in the Lantern Sea, maintained jointly by four states that agree it belongs to none of them. The Halfway Light guides ships through Morrow Strait and shelters people brought ashore by its rescue crews. Four keeper households share watches, stores and a common ledger; no state may close the landing to another's mariners. A supply cutter brings lamp oil, provisions and replacement hands when weather permits. Stone paths connect the beacon, keeper court, fog bell and lifeboat ramp. Unlike the busy port of Tidecross, this small station exists to keep the passage visible and bring people home.

## The Halfway Beacon

A tall stone tower rises above the northern rocks. Its lamp is watched through the night, and a sheltered workroom holds spare wicks, polished reflectors and the weather book. Keeper Edda Vale checks the light before arguing over anything else. Each watch signs the same ledger regardless of which state pays its wages. The worn steps are steep, the gallery catches the full wind, and the view makes approaching trouble visible long before the landing hears about it.

## Four Keepers' Court

Four modest keeper houses face a common house inside a low stone enclosure. Here the households eat, divide watches and settle maintenance arguments at a table with four equal places. No national banner flies above the others. Clerk Tomas Renn records every delivery and repair in duplicate so a delayed payment cannot become a claim of ownership. In bad weather the common room fills with wet coats, mending work and news carried by rescued crews.

## The Common Landing

A timber jetty occupies the sheltered eastern cove. The supply cutter unloads oil, flour, mail and relief keepers here; visiting boats must leave room for rescue work. Boatkeeper Neri Sann reads the swell before allowing anyone onto the wet steps. There is no permanent customs post or passenger market. A scratched board lists supplies still needed from each of the four contributors, beside letters waiting for the next safe departure.

## The Rescue Shed

A broad shed and steep southern slip shelter rescue boats, oars, cork belts, blankets and hauling ropes. Coxswain Jessa Orr drills whoever is fit to pull, including visiting sailors. Crews are chosen by skill rather than flag. After a rescue, the boats are cleaned and the missing names entered before anyone discusses salvage. The ramp can be dangerous even when the eastern landing looks calm.

## The Fog Bell

A heavy bell hangs on a western clifftop platform. When mist hides the beacon, its measured strokes warn vessels away from the rocks. Keepers take paired watches so one can ring while the other listens for replies. The exposed stair has a rope handline renewed every season. Locals say the bell belongs to whoever cannot see the shore; its iron has been repaired by smiths from all four contributing states.

## The Open Door Refuge

A small refuge above the southeastern rocks offers dry bunks, hot broth and a place to recover before travelling on. Caretaker Orla Penn keeps spare clothes sorted by size, not origin, and refuses payment from people brought in by the lifeboat. A memorial garden beside the house remembers mariners whose names were learned too late. Messages to families wait in a tin box for a trusted ship; several former guests now send blankets every winter.

## The Rain Court

A covered rain cistern and carefully tended beds supply the keeper households with water, greens and herbs. Ration marks are visible to everyone during a dry spell. Garden keeper Fen Arel trades seedlings with supply crews and tolerates no washing of oily hands near the water. The little court is also where island children learn knots and mariners recovering at the refuge sit out of the wind.

## The Lamp Store

A detached stone store holds sealed lamp-oil vessels, rope, spare fittings and repair tools away from the tower and living rooms. Two keepers count the reserve together when a delivery arrives. Its public tally matters: every state must see that the light can continue through a missed supply run. A cracked lid, damp wick or short shipment is ordinary work with serious consequences, and discrepancies are written down before blame travels ashore.
