# Caldris: The Century of Hearths and Measures

*2,000–1,900 years before the campaign present · Proposed world lore · Includes GM secrets*

Continues in [The Roads Beyond the Hearth: 1,900–1,600 years ago](history-1900-1600-years-ago.md).

This chapter develops one connected region of Caldris, centred on the country now called the Chalklands and its trading neighbours. It does not imply that the entire world shared one age or culture. Dates are relative to the campaign present; no new calendar is established. The modern place names orient the reader and need not have existed at the time.

The catalog provides a starting point: Caldris's grounded, gentle character, rare magic, the Chalklands, Highmead, Bramblebridge, the Lantern Road, and ancient storehouses in the Button Hills. The user has authorised adding and revising lore, places, and people freely before play begins. This is therefore a working setting chapter, open to further revision, rather than a request for approval of each addition. Its core chronology, mapped locations, historical accounts, and separately scoped GM story material are now published in live Caldris. Present-day situations below are adventure material, not events already activated in a campaign.

Live map reconciliation: the ancient Merecross ferry ruins appear as **Old Merecross**, distinct from the living city already in the atlas. Very early dates use era ordering coordinates within the game’s bounded date range; the displayed years-ago labels retain these authored dates. Map anchors illustrate a place within its parent scope and do not establish travel distances.

## The feeling of the century

Later generations remembered this as an age of good roads. Those who lived through it remembered wet feet.

Most people belonged to a valley before they belonged to a kingdom. A lord's reach often ended where his grain wagons could no longer travel. Coastal towns bought timber, wool, and patience from the interior; inland households depended on salt whose price was decided by weather they would never see. News arrived with drovers, pilgrims, and travelling repairers. A silver coin might cross six jurisdictions before returning to the hand that clipped it.

Magic existed, but knowing that a spell was possible did not put a magician in the next village. One household possessed a bowl that kept broth warm. A hill sanctuary could preserve a seed against frost. Neither could feed a valley. The people who worked wonders still needed food, shelter, materials, and somebody willing to mend their boots.

Long-lived travellers watched friendships become family traditions. Small spirits belonged to particular springs, orchards, and thresholds; some welcomed company, some wanted solitude, and some were dangerous. Their bargains were local and imperfectly understood. Human institutions learned to preserve promises across generations, but also learned how profitable an old promise could become when only one party remembered its wording.

## 2,000–1,993 years ago: The Winter of Borrowed Hearths

Three poor harvests culminated in a winter that closed the upland tracks. Smoke rose from fewer chimneys as families gathered in the houses with sound roofs. The chalk-cut storage chambers of the Button Hills became communal granaries, guarded by shepherds and opened before witnesses.

Maelin Rusk, a widow responsible for weighing grain, refused to use the measures brought by the estate collectors: their baskets had grown larger since the last harvest. She measured distributions in an ordinary household cooking pot. Her decision saved several hamlets from surrendering seed grain as rent and made her a criminal in the surviving estate accounts.

The hill communities adopted the custom of leaving a small portion of supper beside the hearth for an absent traveller. Over time, hospitality replaced hunger in the explanation.

**Historical traces:** Soot-blackened communal kitchens; scratched family marks around granary doors; a winter song in which the same woman is called both thief and grandmother.

**Quest seeds:** A newly opened storehouse contains grain tallies that undermine a family's celebrated claim to have fed the district. Two villages want Maelin's pot returned to them before their winter festivals.

**GM truth:** Maelin also diverted grain from a prosperous hamlet that had contributed fairly. Its children suffered. Her descendants preserved her courage and concealed the cost; the other village preserved the injury and forgot the wider famine.

## 1,992–1,985 years ago: The Road of Seven Suppers

Recovery depended on moving seed and livestock between valleys. Seven households agreed that a traveller carrying a recognised wooden tally could receive supper and a place beside the fire, then repay the hospitality through work or goods at another participating house.

Derren Vale, a muleteer with an unreliable singing voice, carried the first settlement accounts. The elven traveller Aveline Sere accompanied him for four seasons while collecting small household spells. She remembered the journey as a brief, pleasant interruption. Derren built the rest of his life around it.

These stopping places established a practical route across the hills. Parts of it are proposed predecessors of the present Lantern Road, although floods, enclosures, and changing markets would later move its course.

**Historical traces:** Seven shallow notches cut beside old doors; road songs whose verses describe meals; inns that offer a bowl of broth before discussing payment.

**Quest seeds:** A tally opens a hidden cupboard in an apparently much younger inn. An old song gives directions to a lost stopping house, but one verse describes a river that has changed course.

**GM truth:** The seventh host was a threshold spirit inhabiting an abandoned shepherd's dwelling. It received no share of the settlements because Derren could not persuade the others that an empty house had expenses. Its first grievance was quite reasonable.

## 1,984–1,977 years ago: The Orchard Compact

An orchard district survived a succession of late frosts unusually well. Its farmers credited a spirit called the Guest Beneath the Boughs, who appeared as a mud-stained old person with blossom caught in their hair.

The oldest compact required an unharvested strip of orchard, protection for nesting birds, and a shared meal when the first fruit ripened. The Guest sheltered blossoms against a few nights of frost; the farmers maintained the habitat on which the orchards depended. The compact prospered because both sides worked.

A visiting religious house recorded the agreement and offered witnesses for later renewals. Some clergy regarded the Guest as a neighbour; others called it an affliction. The disagreement never divided neatly between kind villagers and cruel priests: several tenants welcomed the clergy because orchard owners were charging them for access to supposedly sacred fruit.

**Historical traces:** A crooked row left unpicked; bowls buried beneath boundary trees; feast days held after the first fruit rather than on a fixed date.

**Quest seeds:** A landowner clears the unharvested strip and finds the orchard failing. Restoring it would remove the only building plots affordable to local families. A traveller asks to attend the feast under an old name that nobody recognises.

**GM truth:** The Guest can shelter only a limited area. Enlarging the orchards without renegotiation eventually made apparent favouritism inevitable. Its silence arose from shame as much as pride.

## 1,976–1,969 years ago: The War of False Weights

Trouble began when three neighbouring market authorities certified incompatible measures under the same name. Traders bought wool by one stone and sold it by another; tenants owed rents whose meaning changed at the estate boundary.

A confiscated caravan led to reprisals. For two years, armed households seized scales, burned toll shelters, and detained merchants. Later ballads supplied great battles that contemporary burial records do not support. Most suffering came from missed planting, stolen animals, and villages cut off from salt.

Sella Venn, a travelling assayer, proposed publicly accessible reference weights and the right to witness a weighing. Merchant houses opposed the expense until a fraudulent shipment embarrassed their own patrons.

**Historical traces:** Stone weights sunk into market steps; a gesture of touching both sides of a balance before bargaining; miniature scales left on graves.

**Quest seeds:** An ancient market weight is found to be hollow. A dispute over whether it was altered could overturn generations of rent calculations—or expose a recent forgery.

**GM truth:** Sella knowingly certified one imperfect standard because insisting on complete restitution would have restarted the fighting. Her private notebook records who paid for that compromise.

## 1,968–1,961 years ago: The Small Wonders Journey

Aveline returned to find Derren grey-haired, prosperous, and annoyed that she had missed his wedding by twenty-seven years. They travelled together once more, more slowly, collecting useful enchantments from households along the recovering road.

Their collection included a charm that revealed a hidden crack in a cup, a verse that coaxed a stubborn cooking flame, and a spell that kept the smell of rain in a folded cloth. Some worked only with particular materials. Some supposed spells proved to be excellent practical advice. Derren insisted those deserved recording too.

They lodged copies with three teaching houses. This modest collection later acquired an extravagant reputation as the Book of Gentle Arts. Its most valuable achievement was persuading a few practitioners to teach people outside their own families.

**Historical traces:** Contradictory copies with recipes between incantations; repairs bearing a tiny cup mark; cloth that smells of a vanished garden.

**Quest seeds:** Recover a page valued as a weapon by a collector, although its actual purpose is to preserve the voice of someone saying goodnight. Reconstruct a spell whose missing ingredient is an obsolete name for an ordinary plant.

**GM truth:** Derren's marginal notes contain the only account of their friendship written for Aveline herself. Most later copies omit them as irrelevant bookkeeping.

## 1,960–1,953 years ago: The Salt Petition

Storms and the loss of several coastal cargoes drove up the price of inland salt. A trading association secured exclusive access to the usable winter route, arguing that dependable escorts required dependable revenue. For a time it delivered exactly what it promised.

Then the association began charging villages for salt they had not ordered. The contracts made participation a condition of using the road. Preserving meat became a matter of debt, and poor households slaughtered animals early rather than feed them through winter.

Brewers, shepherds, and a coastal widow named Ossa Pell pooled small subscriptions to finance one independent convoy. Their Salt Petition survives in the proposed history as an early example of ordinary households funding a commercial venture together.

**Historical traces:** Subscription marks on small lead tokens; salt cupboards with two locks; songs about a widow who bought a road one spoonful at a time.

**Quest seeds:** Find a convoy lost with the subscribers' original register. A surviving token may support a claim to a charitable endowment, but demonstrating it could bankrupt the institution currently feeding the poor.

**GM truth:** Ossa used a smuggling route maintained by the same association she opposed. Its local agents had their own grievances and quietly helped her. The victory was made possible by divided loyalties.

## 1,952–1,945 years ago: The Bell Above the Water

A destructive flood shifted the crossing near the site of modern Bramblebridge. Several households moved uphill; their abandoned wells remained beneath the silt.

A ferryman called Hadrin Moss paid a bellfounder to cast a warning bell, while a rare local enchantress arranged for its clapper to respond to water rising inside a sealed shaft. It warned of certain upstream floods, not every danger, and it needed ordinary maintenance. Keeping the shaft clear proved harder to fund than commissioning the wonder.

The first bell keeper was Hadrin's daughter Enna, who refused the accompanying marriage offer and negotiated a wage instead. Later stories remembered only that the bell rang for a maiden.

**Historical traces:** A buried stair; a foundation built for a much smaller crossing; bell-shaped marks beside flood levels.

**Quest seeds:** A bell rings beneath dry ground. Excavating its shaft would disturb occupied buildings, but leaving it alone may conceal a diverted underground stream.

**GM truth:** The system was deliberately disabled once so a powerful household could deny receiving warning before a flood destroyed a rival's stores. Its maintenance record preserves a suspicious gap. This bell has no assumed connection to any modern campaign bell mystery.

## 1,944–1,937 years ago: The Eighty-Day Crown

An upland ruler died without an accepted successor. The town councils appointed a wool merchant, Tavin Orr, to coordinate supplies until the dispute was settled. He wore the treasury's ceremonial crown at one public hearing because nobody could find the ordinary chain of office.

Later legend made him a reluctant king. Surviving orders suggest a capable, impatient administrator who wanted the roads reopened before shearing season. His government lasted eighty days. It paid soldiers partly in cloth and obtained peace by confirming several villages' rights to their shared pasture.

The eventual dynasty retained his emergency taxes and described his pasture grants as provisional.

**Historical traces:** Cloth seals resembling coins; a comic play about a crowned accountant; pasture stones with their inscriptions turned inward.

**Quest seeds:** A grazing dispute sends travellers searching for the original grants. A claimant produces a convincing copy whose witnesses include someone supposedly dead before it was signed.

**GM truth:** The grant is authentic. One witness continued using her deceased husband's commercial identity because the council would not recognise her independent business. Proving the truth could also expose unrelated family property claims.

## 1,936–1,929 years ago: The House of Returning Names

At a hill sanctuary, Sister Meret began recording travellers who died far from home. With their names she kept small descriptions: a missing tooth, a favourite song, the smell of a trade, the name of someone they hoped to see again.

Merchants carried copies of these notices. A room intended for burial records became a meeting place for lost relatives and returning labourers. Offerings supported messengers, funerals, and the occasional fraud investigation.

The sanctuary's fame grew after an elven visitor identified a century-old burial from a badly remembered joke. Long lives gave the records unusual value, but memory remained fallible: witnesses could be certain and wrong.

**Historical traces:** Name boards reused in later buildings; anonymous graves paired with occupational symbols; a custom of giving one's home village before requesting a bed.

**Quest seeds:** Deliver an ancient personal effect to a long-lived recipient, if that person survives. Identify a nameless grave before roadworks move it. Resolve two families' incompatible claims to the same ancestor.

**GM truth:** Meret sheltered fugitives by recording some of them as dead. One much-celebrated reunion was a carefully arranged escape. Her records are valuable testimony, never infallible proof.

## 1,928–1,920 years ago: The Empty Storehouses

Success brought new grain stores to the Button Hills. Several chambers were cut into older workings whose builders the inhabitants no longer knew. In one cluster, grain spoiled despite dry floors and sound doors. Rats abandoned the site. Labourers reported hearing someone moving sacks after sunset.

An expensive investigation blamed an ancient malediction. The owners commissioned protective inscriptions, collected compensation, and quietly moved their good grain elsewhere. The abandoned chambers gave later shepherds shelter and later storytellers a kingdom beneath the hills.

**Historical traces:** Protective marks carved over tally scratches; blocked ventilation channels; bronze clothing fasteners washed out of spoil heaps—one proposed source of the hills' later association with buttons.

**Quest seeds:** A sinkhole exposes a chamber that someone has recently cleaned. Investigate nocturnal footsteps while keeping curious children away from unstable workings.

**GM truth:** Blocked ventilation caused much of the spoilage, but the footsteps belonged to a small household spirit carrying out instructions from dead custodians. It moved grain out of the damp, unintentionally mixing seed stocks and ownership marks. There is both an engineering problem and a lonely neighbour to deal with.

## 1,919–1,910 years ago: The Last Supper at the Seventh House

Derren died before Aveline's next return. His granddaughter presented her with a packet of letters. The earliest asked when she would visit; the later ones told her what she had missed. The final letter contained directions to a pear tree he thought she would enjoy.

Aveline followed the old supper road and found its seventh house standing behind a wall of brambles. Its threshold spirit still expected the original account to be settled. It had continued laying places for people whose names no longer appeared in any village register.

The surviving song says that Aveline bought everyone's supper. It does not explain what she paid.

**Historical traces:** A pear tree repeatedly grafted onto younger stock; an empty chair at certain roadside feasts; a song that loses a companion in each verse.

**Quest seeds:** Reassemble Derren's letters from household collections. An inn unexpectedly sets places for the party's dead acquaintances; determine whether the invitation is kindness, misunderstanding, or somebody exploiting an old custom.

**GM truth:** Aveline paid with years of service: she agreed to bring the house one living guest each autumn for the remainder of this century. The guests were free to leave. She learned to arrange her travels around an annual promise. Whether she or the spirit survives into the present is deliberately unresolved.

## 1,909–1,900 years ago: The Lantern Settlement

The century ended with agreements between road wardens, market councils, sanctuary keepers, and landed households. They established shared contributions for bridges, public inspection of market measures, winter hospitality funds, and recognised places where travellers could lodge complaints.

Lamps became the visible sign of a participating stopping house. The settlement offers an origin for traditions later associated with the Lantern Road, without requiring today's road or institutions to have existed unchanged for two millennia.

It was an imperfect achievement. Outlying hamlets paid for repairs they seldom benefited from. Several ferry families lost their livelihood. Households without recognised standing had no direct voice. Nevertheless, more people could carry a winter's savings to the next market and reasonably expect to return with them.

On the last evening of the negotiations, someone stole the fine silver lamp commissioned for the signing. The delegates completed their work beside a kitchen lantern. Later paintings consistently show the silver one.

**Historical traces:** Lamp niches beside gates; complaint stones in old market squares; a kitchen lantern described in inventories as an object of state.

**Quest seeds:** A road fund's founding document contains an overlooked obligation to a neglected hamlet. Recovering the missing silver lamp reveals it was broken up to compensate displaced ferrymen.

**GM truth:** The settlement's steward organised that theft with the consent of two delegates. Their unauthorised compensation helped keep the peace but established a secret that could be mistaken for simple corruption.

## Places to put on the map

These locations establish a connected inland-to-coast corridor without fixing distances, continent boundaries, or the final world map. Travelling east from the chalk uplands leads through the Highmead basin and the Bramblebridge crossing, then downriver toward the coast. A southern branch passes the orchard country. The Seven Suppers route is an ancient predecessor with missing and diverted stretches; the modern Lantern Road still ends at Bramblebridge. Downriver travel uses separately named roads and waterways.

### Rusk Hollow — the village beneath the sheep pasture

**During this century:** A cluster of turf-roofed homes around Maelin's communal kitchen in the Button Hills. Its granary doors opened into the hillside, where the temperature stayed steady and children drew sheep in the chalk. Its prosperity depended on wool, stored grain, and the neighbouring hamlets whose contributions kept the stores full.

**Present-day form:** The original homes lie beneath pasture. A younger village occupies the hollow's sheltered edge, and shepherds still uncover hearthstones after heavy rain. The old kitchen can be entered through a collapsed smoke vent. Later building phases explain why a cellar may contain objects separated by centuries.

**A person to meet:** Bessa Thorn, a tenant shepherd and enthusiastic local historian, wants the storehouses made safe before treasure seekers destroy their records. She has a persuasive theory about Maelin and dislikes evidence that complicates it.

**A story here:** A farmer claims an unearthed granary while neighbouring families insist it was held in common. The strongest evidence is not treasure but a sequence of marks showing who repaired the roof during hungry years.

### Dunmere Steps — the market that became a foundation

**During this century:** A steep market settlement in the basin below the hill on which Highmead would later grow. Porters carried goods between three terraces, each controlled by a different authority. Sella's public reference weights were installed on the middle terrace, making it a deliberately neutral place to trade.

**Present-day form:** Dunmere survives as a name for an old lower quarter of Highmead. Most ancient structures have been replaced, but a market stair incorporates original weighing stones. Bakers leave flowers on one step each year without agreeing whose grave it commemorates.

**A person to meet:** Corren Bale, a municipal measurer, is scrupulously honest and painfully aware that his own office's fees are driving small sellers into debt.

**A story here:** A counterfeit weight is discovered during a public festival. Corren needs help proving when the substitution occurred before merchants take their business elsewhere. The culprit may be manipulating the date of a real historical fraud to conceal a smaller current one.

### Harth Orchard — the trees with an empty row

**During this century:** The heart of the Orchard Compact, south of the main hill route. Pears, apples, beehives, and grazing beneath the trees support a patchwork of tenant holdings. The Guest's table stands under a bent pear tree; a religious guesthouse faces it across a muddy lane.

**Present-day form:** The orchard boundaries have changed many times, but growers keep renewing the unpicked strip. The founding tree survives only through successive grafts. The guesthouse is now a small hospice whose garden contains the oldest known graft.

**A person to meet:** Sister Annet keeps the hospice accounts and quietly sets a second bowl out every evening. She has never seen the Guest and is unsure whether her habit is faith, courtesy, or something she learned from her mother.

**A story here:** A rare tree disease threatens both the hospice's income and the living continuity of the compact. A cure requires knowledge preserved by an unfriendly commercial nursery. Negotiating its help may accomplish more than seeking a powerful spell.

### Merecross — the crossing before Bramblebridge

**During this century:** Hadrin and Enna's ferry settlement stands beside a shallow, shifting river channel near the future site of Bramblebridge. Basket-makers, boat repairers, and seasonal fishers live along raised walkways. The warning bell hangs above a stone maintenance shaft on firmer ground.

**Present-day form:** Merecross is an archaeological layer beneath meadow and river silt near Bramblebridge, not a second thriving town. Some descendants of its inhabitants moved to the later bridge settlement. Drought exposes old ferry posts; exceptional floods reveal foundations and then conceal them again.

**A person to meet:** Jory Reed repairs punts and collects fragments of old boats. His family calls itself the last ferry family, a claim several neighbours vigorously contest.

**A story here:** A newly exposed walkway leads toward the bell shaft. Surveying it could reveal a forgotten flood channel before new construction blocks it. Jory hopes for vindication, while the builders have already borrowed against the work.

### The Seventh Hearth — a house that remembers its guests

**During this century:** A shepherd's house on an inconvenient bend of the supper road. Its threshold spirit can tend the fire, shift small objects within the dwelling, and recognise an invitation freely accepted. It cannot produce food, compel guests to stay, or command the countryside. Its hospitality depends on travellers bringing provisions and helping with repairs.

**Present-day form:** Repeated repairs have replaced nearly every timber, while the original hearthstone and threshold remain. The road bypassed it long ago. Brambles obscure the approach; inside, ordinary dust lies beside a carefully swept place at the table. Whether its spirit has remained awake continuously is left for later history to establish.

**A person to meet:** Pell, an itinerant roofer, discovered the house during a storm and returns when work brings him nearby. He considers its resident a demanding elderly landlord and has never thought to ask how old it is.

**A story here:** The threshold stone must be moved before a bank collapse destroys the house. The spirit fears that a new doorway will make it a different person. Finding precedents in old repairs offers a solution grounded in its own history.

### Saint Meret's Rest — the archive of ordinary lives

**During this century:** Initially called the House of Returning Names, the sanctuary occupies a ridge above the upland road. The designation Saint Meret's Rest belongs to later generations, not to its founder's lifetime. A kitchen, burial ground, and room for notices become a modest institution supported by merchants and grieving households.

**Present-day form:** A working hospice and archive built and rebuilt around the burial ground. Almost all early records survive as later copies; water damage, transcription mistakes, and well-intended corrections are normal. A few original wooden name boards were preserved in dry interior walls and rediscovered during repairs.

**A person to meet:** Archivist Edris Fen has a magnificent memory for records and a terrible memory for visitors' faces. He wants to make family histories accessible, but knows that some entries were written to protect people from being found.

**A story here:** An apparently harmless genealogy request could reveal the present descendants of someone whose concealment became a sanctuary obligation. The archive needs a decision about disclosure, not simply a recovered book.

### Veymouth — where inland winter had a price

**During this century:** A tidal port farther downriver from Merecross, connected to offshore fisheries and coastal salt traders. Ossa Pell buys cargo space here for the Salt Petition. Warehouses stand above a lower shore of rope sheds, smokehouses, and flood-prone lodging. The town's councils quarrel over whose money maintains the harbour mouth.

**Present-day form:** A smaller working port beside the silted remains of a former harbour. Commercial importance has shifted elsewhere more than once. Inland families remember Ossa as a champion, while local tradition celebrates the otherwise obscure boat crews who brought the cargoes through.

**A person to meet:** Mara Pell, a harbour bookkeeper who shares Ossa's surname but makes no claim of descent, is organising repairs to a communal landing stage. A wealthy patron will fund it if the town adopts his flattering version of its history.

**A story here:** Low tide exposes a cargo bell bearing marks from the independent convoy. Establishing its provenance leads through salvage rights, a family's guarded letters, and an obsolete shoreline map. The useful reward is a surviving route through the mudflats.

### Wetherfold Common — the pasture nobody quite owns

**During this century:** Upland grazing shared by several hamlets under Tavin Orr's emergency settlement. Low walls shelter lambing pens; a seasonal fair trades fleeces and breeding animals. Herding rights are recorded by household marks rather than precise acreage.

**Present-day form:** A smaller common surrounded by successive enclosures. Later grants renew parts of the original arrangement, giving the ancient settlement practical relevance without making it automatically binding. A boundary stone has been reused as the base of a roadside shrine.

**A person to meet:** Halwen Dorr, a young surveyor, wants to map rights accurately. Her employer expects a cleaner and more profitable answer than the evidence supports.

**A story here:** Moving the shrine to restore a boundary offends its current worshippers. Leaving it in place conceals a mark vital to smallholders' grazing rights. A negotiated survey, witnessed by both communities, may resolve the dispute without either side surrendering its history.

## Using the century in new stories

The events form several connected strands: famine created shared stores and hospitality; safer travel exposed incompatible laws and measures; greater trade produced monopolies and new forms of cooperation; institutions preserved memories while selectively editing them. No single conspiracy explains the century.

For a quiet adventure, recover a recipe or carry an undelivered letter. For a local mystery, combine physical evidence, a family account, and a document that each tell a different part of the truth. For a longer campaign, follow disputes over roads, pasture, and the obligations hidden inside inherited wealth.

After nineteen centuries, most traces should be fragments: reused masonry, copied texts, altered festivals, grafted trees, excavated objects, or customs whose explanations have changed. A complete original document needs a credible preservation history. An ancient charter can influence a modern dispute through later renewals, local custom, or political pressure; age alone does not make it binding. Surviving witnesses need an established lifespan or a specific explanation.

Keep the GM truths separate from player-facing accounts. Discovering an old wrong should create a present choice rather than automatically identify a living villain. A village may depend on an unfair arrangement. An earnest historian may damage a reconciliation. A spirit may deserve restitution without being entitled to everything it demands.

Above all, let the past contain people whose lives mattered beyond their usefulness to an adventure. Derren wanted his friend to see a pear tree. Maelin wanted enough seed left for spring. Enna wanted to be paid for work everyone praised. Their small wishes are what make the surviving wonders worth finding.
