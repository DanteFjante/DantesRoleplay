# Caldris: The Long Summer and the Returning Roads

*1,600–1,200 years before the campaign present · Working setting lore · Includes GM secrets*

Continues [The Roads Beyond the Hearth](history-1900-1600-years-ago.md). Dates count backwards from the campaign present. The opening establishes conditions at 1,600 years ago; the dated periods begin with the following year.

Continues in [The Tides of Promise: 1,200–900 years ago](history-1200-900-years-ago.md).

This is freely revisable setting material for the world before play begins, as authorised by the user. Its core chronology, mapped locations, historical accounts, and separately scoped GM story material are now published in live Caldris. Present-day adventure situations are available premises, not events already underway. Historical accounts and **GM truth** paragraphs should be kept separate when preparing player-facing discoveries.

## The world at the threshold

There were people in Highmead who had never seen the sea but wore cloth dyed on its farther shore. In Serevan, a child could learn the name of a Chalklands market from a shipping label without knowing whether it was a city, a family, or a kind of sheep.

The road had made distant lives useful to one another. Understanding travelled more slowly.

The Crown of Highmead stood above competing councils and estates. Low Vey remained divided between branches of an old river dynasty. Whitewindow depended on external grain and sold workmanship that outlasted the governments ordering it. A narrower successor to the first Lantern League carried money promises and letters between selected counters, its confidence tempered by remembered failure.

Harth's orchard spirit slept. Sallowmere's scholars copied small, difficult wonders. Aveline Sere had departed east with a caravan, following a fragmentary address connected to Samir Orel's blackglass. At the Seventh Hearth, a place was set when someone was expected and put away when they were not.

Four centuries would give all these arrangements time to change. New towns would prosper beside old ruins; kingdoms would fracture without taking everyday life entirely with them; one long friendship with a landscape would finally end. These events concern a widening network around the Chalklands, the Vey river, the northern passes, and Serevan. They do not describe every culture in Caldris as sharing a single historical age.

## First century: The Gardens Across the Water — 1,600–1,500 years ago

### 1,599–1,584: The Address Beneath the Blue Paint

Aveline reached **Leth Sarai**, a Serevan port whose old harbour houses had narrow courtyards filled with washing lines, potted herbs, and arguments audible from the street. The address she carried belonged to a glassworker's shop converted into a communal bakery three generations earlier. Its name survived beneath layers of blue limewash.

The baker **Hadi Sef** helped her trace the former occupants through marriage records and kiln-rental accounts. He accompanied her inland during the bakery's annual repair closure, partly out of curiosity and partly because he had wanted to see the hills since childhood. Aveline assumed he could extend his journey whenever necessary. Hadi pointed out that his sisters were already covering his work.

They reached **Istravel**, a terraced town above an inland basin, with three days remaining before Hadi needed to turn home. A surviving workshop catalogue described dark navigation glass produced there centuries before Samir's lifetime.

**Traces and stories:** A bakery wall preserving the old sign; a route list with bread prices beside distances; workshop emblems on fragments sold as jewellery. A copied address can lead present travellers into a lived-in neighbourhood whose residents have their own reasons to help or refuse entry.

**GM truth:** Hadi was neither a secret prince nor a disguised spirit. His practical help made the discovery possible. Aveline later paid for the bakery's lost labour instead of giving him a valuable object he could not readily use.

### 1,583–1,567: The Blackglass Garden

Istravel's glassworkers had once used a **Night Garden**, an open courtyard with dark pools, observation marks, and shallow channels for cooling prepared glass. Their navigation disks combined an unusual mineral, painstaking alignment under particular stars, and a small enchantment maintained during manufacture.

Aveline and the current workshop keeper, **Rashan Emet**, recovered enough instructions to identify Samir's disk as one of a limited family of instruments. Its makers had lived and worked in ordinary houses. Their workshop was exceptional; it was not evidence that every household in the ancient town possessed effortless magic.

The sea had taken an older workshop quarter during a distant coastal subsidence, explaining the story of a drowned city without establishing a lost world empire. Reproducing the disks proved costly. Two usable examples took years, and trained pilots still needed to interpret them.

**Traces and stories:** Glazed basin fragments; star marks beneath later paving; a furnace account recording many failures. A commission to repair a disk requires expertise from several trades and gives an expedition a concrete reason to visit the old mineral workings.

**GM truth:** The recovered instructions deliberately used an obsolete trade name for the mineral to protect the makers' livelihood. It is identifiable from workshop waste. The missing ingredient is difficult to source, not the blood of a vanished people.

### 1,566–1,550: The Apricot Marriage

**Princess Samin of Serevan** married **Lord Edric Aster**, a younger relative of Highmead's reigning household. Their agreement concerned harbour access and guaranteed purchases of upland wool. Its public celebrations promised friendship between peoples whose negotiators had spent most of the winter arguing over freight losses.

Samin arrived with attendants, cooks, a physician, two gardeners, and trunks of ordinary belongings. Some returned home within a year. Others settled, married locally, or founded businesses. The exchange made a lasting impression through courtyard gardening, preserved fruit, lending customs within migrant households, and new words for familiar kitchen tools.

An apricot grown against a sheltered Highmead wall became a symbol of the marriage. It bore little fruit in cold years. Samin funded a gardeners' lodging house after noticing that the story of its miraculous flourishing was obscuring the labour required to keep it alive.

**Traces and stories:** Wall gardens; recipes using cheaper local substitutes; household letters written in two languages. Restoring a migrant family's burial inscriptions can reveal a different account of the celebrated arrival.

**GM truth:** Samin and Edric developed genuine affection while disagreeing about policy. Neither secretly controlled the other. Their correspondence is valuable because it records the negotiation of a shared life, including homesickness that public portraits omitted.

### 1,549–1,533: The Ford of Sleeping Carts

A safer northern approach opened through **Rovenfell**, an upland kingdom of steep valleys and scattered fortified halls. Its rulers controlled several passes but depended on village associations to keep them usable. The new route connected Whitewindow's western trade to grain country beyond the Chalklands.

At **Tallow Ford**, floodwater stranded an autumn caravan for twenty-six days. Merchants hired local households to stable animals and bought food on credit. One dispute over fodder nearly turned violent before **Essel Dorn**, a Rovenfell road judge, made both sides account for what the animals actually needed.

The settlement left a permanent seasonal caravan yard. Local people called it the Ford of Sleeping Carts, though the merchants remembered very little sleep. The road diversified winter supplies and reduced the power of anyone controlling a single southern crossing.

**Traces and stories:** Raised tether stones; measurements scratched on stable walls; a song about a mule eating a king's tax. A washed-out bridge sends modern travellers looking for the older ford, whose bed has shifted since the surviving map was drawn.

**GM truth:** Essel's fair settlement relied on the village accepting delayed payment. Later accounts praise her judgement while overlooking the households that assumed the risk. Some never received everything owed.

### 1,532–1,516: The Quiet Fire at Sallowmere

A chimney fire spread into Sallowmere's copying rooms during a windy night. Residents saved people first, then whichever books they could reach. **Pera Nall**, an apprentice preparing ink, carried out a basket of account books because she had been told they were nearest the door and easiest to lift.

Several celebrated spell collections burned. The accounts survived, along with lists of borrowers, repair instructions, and letters used as bookmarks. Those humble records later made it possible to locate copies scattered across dozens of households.

Rebuilding produced a smaller library with detached cooking rooms and cupboards assigned to different kinds of material. The teachers also began placing copies with distant friends. This preserved knowledge but loosened their control over how it was taught and attributed.

**Traces and stories:** Heat-cracked shelf tiles; borrower lists; a charred book cover enclosing an unrelated later text. Following a loan record can turn a supposed lost masterpiece into a set of recipes still used by a family unaware of its scholarly reputation.

**GM truth:** The fire was an accident. Pera did not foresee the accounts' future importance. She followed sensible instructions under frightening conditions and deserved recognition without being made a prophet.

### 1,515–1,500: The Year of the Empty Throne

The last reigning Aster sovereign died after a long illness, leaving disputed claimants and an exhausted treasury. Highmead's councils funded essential guards and bridge repairs while neighbouring estates withheld contributions. The interregnum lasted several years despite its name.

The eventual sovereign, **Queen Ilyen Sorne**, came from an upland household whose acceptance depended on written limits and renewed estate agreements. The Crown of Highmead continued, but House Aster's rule ended. Its relatives remained landholders, merchants, religious patrons, and sometimes disappointed conspirators.

Ilyen's first progress through the countryside was modest. At Wetherfold she sat beneath a patched awning to hear grazing disputes. Later paintings supplied a pavilion large enough to hide the common she had come to inspect.

**Traces and stories:** Cancelled court appointments; crown furniture sold into private houses; a renewed commons roll. An apparent royal heirloom may have entered a family through a public sale rather than theft.

**GM truth:** Ilyen accepted a restriction she disliked: emergency levies needed witnesses from the affected districts. She obeyed it during her reign. Later rulers' attempts to weaken it belong to their own choices, not a secret defect in her original promise.

## Second century: The Long Summer — 1,499–1,400 years ago

### 1,499–1,484: The Meadows That Moved Uphill

A run of relatively favourable growing seasons encouraged settlements above the older fields. People later called this part of the period the Long Summer, though it contained ordinary winters, failed crops, and years remembered locally for rain.

The new village of **Hazelstairs** grew along terraced slopes below a northern pass. Its households included younger children of established farmers, displaced tenants, two Whitewindow families, and Rovenfell herders. Their cooperation was practical and often contentious. Terrace walls, water turns, and grazing limits mattered more than proclamations of shared destiny.

**Danna Vey**, a millwright, built a small mill using a channel supplied by several springs. She reserved a maintenance share of every milling payment. The arrangement worked while users could see the repairs their grain paid for.

**Traces and stories:** Abandoned upper terraces; a millstone reused as a doorstep; water turns preserved in a counting game. An old channel could restore a failing village supply but also drain a wet meadow now important to downstream households.

**GM truth:** The settlers knew the high fields were marginal. Later landlords converted a cautious experiment into a claim that the slopes had always supported higher rents.

### 1,483–1,467: The River Without a King

Low Vey's last widely acknowledged sovereign died without the money or alliances needed to enforce a succession. Three generations of local arrangements had already shifted power toward towns, landholders, and crossing districts. The kingdom's dissolution was spread across negotiations, small armed confrontations, and years in which different officials collected competing dues.

Veymouth acquired a free-port charter from its own coalition of harbour interests. Reedminster's surrounding communities formed the **Reedbank Association**, a federation responsible for flood channels, ferries, and mutual defence. The southern estates accepted the **Margravate of South Vey**, ruled by House Halren in exchange for recognisable limits on service.

Members of House Keld survived in all three territories. Some served new institutions; some emigrated. A kingdom ended without its population disappearing.

**Traces and stories:** Royal stones recut with local emblems; coins circulating long after the sovereign died; three incompatible official accounts of the last court. A burial discovery can give an unglamorous answer to the fate of a supposedly vanished prince.

**GM truth:** One Keld claimant became a harbour accountant under his own name. His descendants later found exile and secret royalty more marketable than competent employment.

### 1,466–1,450: The Honey Road

Beekeepers from the Chalklands and Serevan began exchanging wax, hive-making methods, and suitable flowering plants through a chain of inland fairs. Fine wax commanded good prices among scribes, metalworkers, households, and religious communities; adulteration became a recurring problem.

The route's busiest meeting place, **Amberstile**, grew around a pasture gate and a sheltered wagon ground. **Marel Thenn**, a travelling wax tester, persuaded several buyers to compare samples in public instead of trusting prestigious seals. She became famous enough that fraudulent sellers began using her name.

People called the route the Honey Road, though much of its value lay in wax and returning cargoes of needles, cloth, and salt. Its inns were remembered for sweet cakes partly because travellers arriving with honey could settle small expenses in kind.

**Traces and stories:** Wax cakes stamped with household marks; account entries for payment in candle ends; an old fair song naming flowers that no longer grow nearby. A shipment of genuine but unfamiliar wax is accused of being false, and resolving the dispute requires knowledge rather than exposing a villain.

**GM truth:** Marel occasionally rejected good material because her method was imperfect. Her later notes explain the exceptions. A simplified textbook version creates opportunities for modern mistakes and deliberate abuse.

### 1,449–1,433: The Choir Below Greyfold

Quarry workers near Rovenfell opened a sealed chamber beneath **Greyfold Ridge**. From within it came singing in voices resembling people the listeners loved. Three workers entered and did not return. The chamber's occupant learned further names by listening to the search parties outside.

The predator became known as the **Hollow Cantor**. It was an old, intelligent thing that borrowed remembered voices and used the underground passages as a hunting ground. Its first language remains unknown. It was capable of speech and bargains, but promises of reunion were deliberate lures.

**Serit Vale**, a Rovenfell scout unrelated to any established Vale lineage, led the final approach with written signals, waxed ear coverings, and rehearsed routes. Aveline and a local ward-maker weakened the voice's supernatural pull. The company killed the creature and sealed two unstable lower passages.

**Traces and stories:** A slate route board; memorial pebbles bearing occupations rather than heroic titles; a passage marked with a crossed mouth. A recent imitation of the song might be an opportunist exploiting the legend or an acoustic remnant worth investigating.

**GM truth:** The Cantor died. The lower passages conceal older architecture and the remains of earlier victims, not an automatically recurring version of the same monster. Its apparent ability to summon the dead was deception, not resurrection.

### 1,432–1,416: The Supper of Unremembered Faces

Aveline returned to the Chalklands after many journeys between the two shores and the northern roads. She found herself greeted as an ancestor's friend by people who had never met her. Some expected intimacy she did not feel; others wanted explanations for promises she had never made.

At the Seventh Hearth she hosted a supper for people preserving fragments of the old road's history. **Lysa Marr**, a potter who had purchased rather than inherited Iven's former kiln site, brought cups. A sanctuary copyist brought versions of Derren's letters. A shepherd arrived with a song whose final verse Aveline had never heard.

She corrected some stories, admitted uncertainty about others, and asked to keep the new verse. The supper established no magical order. Its influence came from the copies and ordinary friendships carried away by the guests.

**Traces and stories:** A guest list containing several crossed-out assumptions of kinship; a song with Aveline's notes; cups bearing different workshop marks. A commission to reconstruct the supper can become an affectionate journey through scattered family traditions.

**GM truth:** One of Derren's letters at the table was a later imitation composed by a descendant who wanted to say something on his behalf. Aveline recognised the change in voice and kept it, labelled with its actual author.

### 1,415–1,400: Aveline's Last Winter

Aveline died at Sallowmere **1,410 years before the campaign present**, aged **688**. This places her birth 2,098 years before the present and her first journeys with Derren a little over a century into her life. She died after a period of declining health, attended by friends and people paid to provide the practical care she needed.

Her final months included irritation, unfinished correspondence, several good afternoons, and a dispute about where her papers should go. She divided her working collection between Sallowmere, the House of Returning Names, and a correspondent in Istravel. Her plain travel cup remained at the Seventh Hearth, where she had left it two centuries earlier.

She asked for a modest burial above the lake and for Derren's practical notes to remain with the spells. Her companions planted a pear graft from the lineage associated with his tree. Neither the graft nor the grave was given an enchantment of eternal preservation.

**Traces and stories:** Three bequest lists; a gravestone preserved through later resetting; a final letter asking whether a parcel arrived safely. Recovering part of the bequest can reconnect collections without discovering a secret claim to her soul.

**GM truth:** Her death was real. No hidden bargain, replacement body, or waiting resurrection is established. Her contribution to present-day stories can be complete without her becoming an immortal source of answers. One intended recipient never received their parcel; its contents remain an open adventure seed.

## Third century: The Years of Unfastened Gates — 1,399–1,300 years ago

### 1,399–1,384: The Mapmaker's Daughter

**Tarin Hesh**, a surveyor from Leth Sarai, travelled inland with her father **Omren**, whose eyesight was failing. He could remember a coastline he could no longer see clearly; she could measure a channel but did not know all its seasonal warnings. They argued through two years of work and produced a practical atlas of selected ferry routes, coast approaches, and market roads.

Their atlas marked uncertainty openly. It distinguished a measured distance from a traveller's estimate and a seasonal ford from a permanent crossing. Buyers sometimes preferred more confident rival maps.

After Omren died, Tarin continued inland, adding the road to Whitewindow and marginal notes from local guides. Her later work reached Rovenfell. It offers evidence of travel and contact, not a definitive map of every land in Caldris.

**Traces and stories:** Maps with different symbols for certainty; sketches of small ferries omitted from royal surveys; guide payments in the margins. Reconciling two editions can reveal a deliberately removed safe route without making every cartographic discrepancy a conspiracy.

**GM truth:** Omren's memory was valuable and fallible. Tarin's decision to correct one of his proudest drawings cost her a patron who preferred the celebrated older version.

### 1,383–1,367: The Open Gate Rebellion

Rovenfell's ruler attempted to collect several years of arrears after failed military ventures beyond the northern valleys. Gate officials began seizing pack animals, including those carrying seed and winter supplies. Village associations blocked collections and opened an unlicensed pass to divert trade.

The conflict became the **Open Gate Rebellion**. Its leaders included mule drivers, a disinherited noblewoman named **Lady Brenna Oss**, and two valley judges who disagreed about nearly everything except the seizures. Fighting at **Juniper Gate** killed people on both sides and burned the records of several households' obligations.

A settlement replaced the failing royal administration in the affected valleys with the **Roven Marches**, a compact of local jurisdictions maintaining a shared road guard. Rovenfell's outer districts took different paths. The old kingdom did not become a single, harmonious republic overnight.

**Traces and stories:** Cut chains displayed above gates; conflicting casualty lists; a pass shrine built from burnt tollhouse stone. A recovered account can establish that a village had already paid a levy later remembered as rebellion against all taxation.

**GM truth:** Brenna used the coalition partly to recover her family's influence. Several other leaders knew this and worked with her anyway. The settlement's practical gains do not depend on every participant having pure motives.

### 1,366–1,350: The White Salt Marsh

A severe coastal storm changed the channels near Nacre Quay and drove salt water across low fields. Some farms recovered; others became marsh. The community of **Bellwade** moved inland over several seasons, carrying door lintels, household shrines, seed, and whatever could be loaded on borrowed carts.

The old settlement's small warning bell was moved with them. Only later songs placed it ringing beneath the water. Fishers used the submerged lanes as seasonal fishing grounds, while former landholders disagreed over whether old field boundaries meant anything in a tidal creek.

Nacre's harbour gained one deeper channel and lost another. Merchants celebrated an advantage born from other people's displacement. Relief contributions eventually helped rebuild Bellwade around a common drying ground.

**Traces and stories:** Orchard stumps in tidal mud; reused lintels bearing inland street names; a bell with repair marks from its overland journey. A low-water survey can recover household objects before erosion destroys them.

**GM truth:** Bellwade's actual bell is not submerged and is not related to Merecross's ancient magical warning bell. Sounds heard across the marsh have several possible ordinary causes; later authors should establish any new supernatural source rather than assume one from the song.

### 1,349–1,333: The Sleeper Opens One Eye

After almost three centuries of sleep, the Guest Beneath the Boughs woke briefly at Harth. It recognised the root ground and failed to recognise most of the people tending it. It asked for Tessa Brin, who had been dead for roughly five centuries.

The current keeper, **Elen Carrow**, explained the nursery records and the old compact's revisions. The Guest wandered the unpicked strip, listened to the names of new grafts, and accepted a meal prepared without knowing its former favourites. It offered a little shelter to one frost-struck row, then slept again.

The encounter became important because it did not restore an age of guaranteed protection. Elen recorded that the growers had cared for the orchard through the absence and could continue doing so. Later pilgrims sometimes found this account disappointingly modest.

**Traces and stories:** Elen's testimony in later copies; a small bowl repaired before the visit; a named pear graft called One Good Morning. A new waking can present the problem of introducing an ancient neighbour to a changed community without promising its service on the community's behalf.

**GM truth:** The Guest remembers selectively and can confuse generations. Its testimony about early Caldris needs interpretation. This awakening does not establish continuous wakefulness or renewed annual obligations in the present.

### 1,332–1,316: The King Who Pawned the Rain

**King Odran Sorne** of Highmead borrowed heavily to support a border campaign, court rebuilding, and overdue repairs. One contract pledged future revenue from three watermills and their associated water dues. A popular song accused him of pawning the rain.

The financiers received collection rights, not ownership of weather or every stream. Their agents nevertheless pressed ambiguous wording against small users. Millers resisted, councils appealed, and the crown discovered that selling a revenue stream did not free it from the consequences of how that revenue was collected.

**Nessa Dain**, a miller's advocate, assembled evidence of customary household use. The resulting settlement protected specified small uses and required public inspection of the three contracted weirs. It also left some large debts unpaid and harmed lenders who had not participated in the overreach.

**Traces and stories:** A satirical rain crown; water-use schedules; a mill bell rung when a channel was closed for maintenance. A present dispute over a later renewal may depend on the distinction between rights to collect dues and rights to divert water.

**GM truth:** Odran understood the financial terms but underestimated how aggressively his creditors would enforce them. His officials' claim that he was tricked by an impossibly clever contract is self-serving.

### 1,315–1,300: The School with No Master

A succession of difficult years left Sallowmere unable to support a resident magical teacher. Its caretaker, **Hollis Wren**, kept the rooms open for visiting practitioners and ordinary copyists. Neighbours supplied meals in exchange for reading letters, measuring fields, and teaching children.

The school continued without a permanent master because enough people could do parts of its work. Instruction varied, some experiments stopped, and rare skills were lost when no suitable teacher returned. Its resilience had real limits.

A visiting musician adapted the sound-preserving vessel into a commission for a dying singer. The result preserved one short lullaby in her own voice. It required a prepared vessel, a deliberate recording, and careful storage; it could not answer her child or supply words she had never recorded.

**Traces and stories:** Meal rosters; letters practised on the backs of failed experiments; a vessel catalogue recording whose voice was captured and when. A cracked recording can become a restoration task whose stakes are personal rather than military.

**GM truth:** Hollis could work no spells. The school's survival depended on organisation, trust, and labour as much as magical talent. The lullaby vessel may survive through a stated chain of custody; widespread household sound archives are not established.

## Fourth century: The Returning Roads — 1,299–1,200 years ago

### 1,299–1,284: The Griffin Above Juniper Gate

A griffin nested above the principal pass through the Roven Marches. It took sheep first, then pack animals, and struck a mule train on a narrow ledge. The resulting fall killed two travellers. The road guard could protect individual parties only at considerable cost.

Hunter **Mera Oss** and Whitewindow animal keeper **Boran Thale** investigated the nesting cliff. They found that a quarry had disturbed the creature's former hunting slope. The griffin was an animal with dangerous habits, not a judge punishing the valley. Moving the quarry's refuse helped restore prey habitat but did not make the established attacks stop immediately.

The eventual response combined temporary convoy closures, guarded passage, and relocating the unattended eggs to an isolated ledge while the adults were away. One adult died attacking the work party. The surviving adult used the new nest after several uncertain days.

**Traces and stories:** Bolts set into cliff paths; compensation rolls; a feathered crest adopted by the road guard. Later griffins may nest in the same region, but they are descendants or new arrivals, not automatically the original animal.

**GM truth:** The solution was costly and imperfect. Its later reputation as a bloodless triumph omits both the dead travellers and the killed griffin. The participants' own records are more honest.

### 1,283–1,267: The Marriage of the Two Harbours

Veymouth and Nacre Quay agreed to share selected pilot services, maintain compatible signals, and recognise each other's inspected cargo seals. Merchants called it the Marriage of the Two Harbours. There was no literal royal wedding and no merger of the towns.

The agreement followed several lean years and a costly sequence of duplicated works. **Harbour Speaker Resha Pell** of Veymouth and **Master Pilot Omin Sare** of Nacre negotiated for months over which channel repairs benefited whom. Fishers won seats in the maintenance hearings after demonstrating that official charts omitted their busiest landing places.

Rowing races and insulting pastries continued. The new arrangement made cooperation sufficiently routine that people could enjoy rivalry without needing every grievance resolved first.

**Traces and stories:** Paired harbour seals; a shared signal manual; cakes shaped like badly piloted boats. Restoring one harbour's old light could harm the other's modern approaches if the historical scheme is copied without accounting for the changed coastline.

**GM truth:** The agreement depended on periodic renewal and a workable way to share costs. It is an ancestor of possible modern cooperation, not an unbroken treaty immune to twelve centuries of change.

### 1,266–1,250: The Winter of Returned Keys

Colder growing seasons and repeated upland crop failures made several high settlements difficult to sustain. Hazelstairs' remaining households negotiated departure to lower land. Some held on, hoping for improvement; others left before the last harvest because waiting meant losing their animals too.

The departing villagers hung keys in the mill house so neighbours could use shelter and stored materials. The image became a regional emblem of loss, later misrepresented as everyone vanishing on the same night. The actual movement took years and involved contracts, arguments, marriages, and wagons borrowed from relatives.

**Sela Dorn**, who kept the final communal accounts, listed debts owed to the village as carefully as debts owed by it. Receiving towns wanted workers but did not always welcome their claims to compensation or common land.

**Traces and stories:** Keys found in later deposits around the mill; reused doorframes; a migration register with destinations. A family can seek an ancestor's house through records rather than assuming the abandoned village contains untouched wealth.

**GM truth:** Some people returned seasonally for grazing, charcoal, and salvage. Hazelstairs was gradually abandoned as a permanent village, not sealed in an instant. Its ruins contain later occupation as well as the founding period.

### 1,249–1,233: The Lantern Road Turns South

Bank erosion and the cost of an old bridge forced a section of the inland road onto a southern alignment. Two established inns lost passing trade. A new stopping place near the orchard branch grew quickly, while the old path became a local track.

The detour further isolated the Seventh Hearth, whose approach had already become inconvenient centuries earlier. A committee proposed moving its threshold stone and hearth to a busier site. The spirit declined. It knew its rooms through repair, smell, and habit; being useful to more travellers was not the only thing it wanted.

Local households instead arranged occasional visits and essential maintenance. The decision preserved an out-of-the-way neighbour without compelling it to operate a public inn forever. The regional name Lantern Road survived the shift and still denotes the route ending at Bramblebridge in the campaign present.

**Traces and stories:** Two abandoned milestones with conflicting distances; old inn cellars beside a narrow track; a maintenance list containing payments in roofing work. Following the older alignment can reveal places modern road maps omit.

**GM truth:** The spirit's consent matters because the stones are its home, not because moving them would certainly unleash a catastrophe. A future relocation problem should allow negotiation and evidence from earlier repairs rather than rely on an invented automatic curse.

### 1,232–1,216: The Summer of the Travelling Stage

A company led by **Ilren Moss**, a carpenter and actor, travelled between the two harbours, Highmead, and the northern fairs with a folding stage. Its most popular play joined Branna's hunt, the Eighty-Day Crown, and the Blackglass Pilot into one improbable lifetime.

Local historians objected. Audiences laughed, bought food, and sometimes learned names they would otherwise never have heard. The company also collected songs from people unable to read, preserving regional verses absent from sanctuary collections.

At Saint Meret's Rest, a clerk named **Abel Rinn** negotiated permission to copy the company's songbook in return for repairing its wagon. The manuscript distinguished performance inventions from accounts supplied by named singers. Later copies did not always preserve the distinction.

**Traces and stories:** Painted stage boards reused in a loft; prompt notes; a wagon wheel carved with places visited. A seemingly impossible historical clue becomes understandable once a theatrical scene is separated from the older song embedded inside it.

**GM truth:** Ilren knew his popular play was inaccurate and presented it as entertainment. Fraud arose later when collectors sold its painted props as genuine relics. Popular storytelling also preserved material that formal archives had ignored.

### 1,215–1,200: The Book of Roads Left Open

At the end of these centuries, the sanctuary at Saint Meret's Rest assembled a practical collection of routes, shelters, ferry customs, burial notices, and places where assistance had ceased. Its contributors included pilots, drovers, hosts, scholars, and people who had recently lost a home.

The **Book of Roads Left Open** was not a single perfect atlas. It was a set of regional books with instructions to date corrections and keep superseded pages. One compiler, **Arda Linn**, wrote that an obsolete road could still explain where someone had gone.

Several copies travelled outward. One reached Istravel, where a later keeper added information about the dispersed blackglass workshops. Another reached the Roven Marches. A third remained in the Chalklands and gradually acquired household letters between its pages.

**Traces and stories:** Different regional editions; abandoned-route annotations; notices directing readers to other archives. Finding two copies can reveal both a lost place and why travellers stopped going there.

**GM truth:** No complete, current master copy exists in the campaign present. Reconstructing one region is a feasible adventure. The book provides leads and imperfect testimony, never an answer to every mystery or a substitute for visiting the people who live along the roads now.

## Places to add to the world

These locations widen the setting without fixing the final continent map. Leth Sarai and Istravel lie in Serevan across Morrow Strait. Rovenfell and its successor marches occupy northern pass country beyond Whitewindow's principal approaches. The remaining sites extend the established inland and coastal network.

Present-day forms below are working destinations. Later centuries can explain rebuilding, changes of rule, and transmission of records. Ancient people named in the timeline are not assumed alive in the present.

### Leth Sarai — the port behind the painted doors

**Ancient role:** A Serevan harbour linking coastal cargoes to inland glassworking, gardens, and caravan routes. Its bakery preserves the address that brought Aveline east.

**Present-day form:** A densely inhabited old quarter behind a rebuilt waterfront. Courtyards hold workshops, kitchens, and shared wells. Buildings have changed occupants and functions many times; old signs survive beneath paint or in reused lintels. The town trades in ordinary necessities as well as fine goods.

**A person to meet:** **Bakery keeper Azra Sef** knows the local property history because changes of ovens, drains, and access rights matter to her business. She makes no claim to descend from Hadi.

**Adventure:** A visitor arrives with evidence that a supposedly lost archive was once stored beneath the bakery. The cellar now supports a busy workplace. Access requires a practical arrangement for food production, labour, and safe examination.

### Istravel — the terraces of the Night Garden

**Ancient role:** A glassworking town above an inland basin, with small specialist workshops and the courtyard where blackglass instruments were aligned.

**Present-day form:** Active glassworkers occupy the upper terraces. Lower kilns have become gardens and storage. Part of the Night Garden lies beneath a later courtyard occupied by several households. Its pools retain only fragments of original lining, and its old sight lines are obstructed by new roofs.

**A person to meet:** **Glassworker Temir Emet** can judge a furnace load by its colour but distrusts antiquarian claims about miraculous lost techniques. He is willing to learn if experiments are paid for and failures recorded.

**Adventure:** Restoring one navigation disk requires recovering instructions from workshop waste, obtaining a mineral sample, and negotiating temporary access to a sight line. Success yields a specific useful instrument, not mastery over the sea.

### Tallow Ford — the yard of wintering wagons

**Ancient role:** The northern crossing where a stranded caravan created a lasting market for fodder, repairs, and storage.

**Present-day form:** A working caravan yard beside a newer bridge, with remnants of older crossings visible at low water. Local farms depend on visitors but must retain enough fodder for their own animals. Its largest stable incorporates tether stones from several building phases.

**A person to meet:** **Yard steward Bera Dorn** posts fodder reserves openly so merchants understand why purchases are limited. Some interpret the limits as a scheme to raise prices.

**Adventure:** A stranded party needs winter supplies while a missing delivery leaves the village short. Finding the shipment can involve a damaged route, a competing buyer, and a driver sheltering sick animals rather than a straightforward theft.

### Hazelstairs — the hillside with empty doorways

**Ancient role:** A mixed upland farming settlement founded during favourable seasons and gradually vacated when repeated failures made permanent occupation untenable.

**Present-day form:** Terraces, cellar mouths, and fragments of the mill stand among summer grazing. Later shepherd shelters occupy some foundations. Streams have changed course and roots have shifted retaining walls; exploration requires care around unstable drops.

**A person to meet:** **Herd keeper Jessa Vey** uses the ruins seasonally and wants visitors to understand which walls still hold back occupied pasture. She has found a cache of keys but no treasure chest that fits them.

**Adventure:** A migration register names a house whose doorframe was carried to another village. Matching it to the old foundation may establish a family connection or locate hidden correspondence left in a wall during the move.

### Amberstile — the fair that smells of warm wax

**Ancient role:** A Honey Road meeting place for beekeepers, wax buyers, and carriers bringing other goods on the return journey.

**Present-day form:** A modest market town whose wax sheds serve candle makers, craftspeople, and local households. Seasonal trading fills a broad yard behind the old gate. The honey cakes sold to visitors are good enough that residents forgive the inaccurate stories printed on their wrappers.

**A person to meet:** **Assayer Rill Thenn** is revising a respected testing manual and faces resistance from buyers comfortable with its simpler rules.

**Adventure:** A suspect shipment comes from a new flowering district and behaves differently under the established test. Proving its quality may rescue a small producer's livelihood while revealing how a second trader exploited the confusion.

### Greyfold Ridge — the quarry where voices were answered

**Ancient role:** The site of the Hollow Cantor's emergence and defeat, overlooking older underground works not fully explored by its hunters.

**Present-day form:** An abandoned deep quarry beside a smaller working stone face. The hunt's sealed approaches have undergone erosion and later interference. Upper chambers contain quarry remains; lower architecture belongs to an older, still-unresolved history.

**A person to meet:** **Surveyor Dal Oss** has mapped safe working areas and wants a qualified team to inspect a newly opened void before labourers enter it.

**Adventure:** Recover the route slate and investigate the void. Echoes and old warnings create uncertainty, but the dead Cantor need not return. A new occupant, unstable workings, or evidence of the chamber's builders can supply distinct stakes.

### Juniper Gate — the pass with a public chain

**Ancient role:** A fortified toll crossing central to the Open Gate Rebellion and later threatened by griffins nesting above its approaches.

**Present-day form:** A guarded pass serving several jurisdictions. Parts of the old gate are incorporated into a shelter and customs yard. The displayed rebellion chain has replacement links from later repairs. Cliff paths lead to abandoned observation posts and nesting ledges.

**A person to meet:** **Road captain Vanna Thale** wants safer escort schedules and compensation for animal losses. She resents visitors expecting her small guard to behave like the grand company in the ballads.

**Adventure:** Changes in nesting behaviour force a temporary route closure. Investigating disturbed hunting ground can reveal a practical remedy, while travellers and local households need support during the delay.

### Bellwade — the village that carried its bell

**Ancient role:** A low coastal farming community relocated after saltwater inundation and rebuilt around a shared drying ground.

**Present-day form:** An inland village facing a tidal marsh where fragments of its first site occasionally emerge. Some houses incorporate old lintels, though many claimed originals are later commemorations. The communal bell has been recast since its relocation, preserving portions of earlier metal and its inherited name.

**A person to meet:** **Marsh guide Orsa Nall** records exposures after storms and asks finders to note where objects were recovered before moving them.

**Adventure:** An eroding bank reveals a household shrine. Two families recognise its emblem from different traditions, and careful examination can show how both accounts connect to successive occupants.

### The Pear Walk at Sallowmere — the place where a journey ended

**Ancient role:** Aveline's burial place above the lake and the site of a pear graft connected to Derren's remembered tree.

**Present-day form:** A quiet path through a younger orchard beside the library grounds. Aveline's stone was reset after later bank damage; records of that work preserve the connection to the original burial. The pear lineage has survived through grafting, replacement, and at least one disputed identification.

**A person to meet:** **Groundskeeper Mina Wren** cares for the orchard and dislikes visitors taking cuttings without asking. She is trying to identify which trees share the oldest documented graft lineage.

**Adventure:** A collector offers to fund repairs in exchange for an exclusive cutting. The library must weigh needed money against keeping the lineage available. A separate bequest clue can lead outward from the grave without disturbing the burial.

### The South Turning — the inn beside the newer road

**Ancient role:** A stopping place that prospered when a Lantern Road section was shifted away from eroding banks.

**Present-day form:** A much-rebuilt inn at a junction where visitors can choose the main road or the old local track toward the Seventh Hearth. A milestone in its yard gives a distance that ceased to be accurate centuries ago. Older inn cellars lie farther along the abandoned alignment.

**A person to meet:** **Innkeeper Olan Marr** sends a neighbour to check the old hearth when weather permits, treating the duty as a local friendship rather than a marketable attraction.

**Adventure:** A storm prevents the customary visit. A journey along the old road becomes a small rescue and repair story, with discoveries in the bypassed stopping places along the way.

Institutions listed here exist in these historical periods. Their survival into the campaign present must come through subsequent history, not automatic continuity of dynasty, charter, or ownership. The same applies to buildings, trees, objects, and records: repairs, copies, grafts, migrations, and custody explain what remains.

## Stories that can grow from this chapter

**The parcel that missed a lifetime.** An entry in Aveline's bequest names a parcel sent toward Istravel but never acknowledged. Its contents can be selected for a future adventure: practical corrections to blackglass notes, a personal letter, or a small recorded farewell made during her final months. Evidence must establish any recording's creation; it cannot reproduce an unrecorded voice from the past. Tracing the parcel can carry a party through inland archives, ports, and workshops.

**The abandoned village's second chance.** Someone wants to settle Hazelstairs again. The ruins offer shelter and waterworks, but the old reasons for departure deserve investigation. A viable smaller settlement, seasonal use, or a decision to repair a lower village can all be satisfying outcomes.

**The road nobody owns alone.** Damage at Tallow Ford and Juniper Gate interrupts supplies reaching Whitewindow and the Chalklands. The history offers routes, obligations, and local memories, while current communities must decide what help they can afford. A dangerous pass expedition has a tangible purpose beyond finding treasure.

**The monster is dead; the place is still unknown.** Greyfold's warning stories are true about the Cantor and incomplete about the chambers beneath it. A party can honour the hunters' work, prepare against plausible dangers, and explore a different mystery without undoing the old victory.

**The play that remembered one true thing.** A ridiculous heroic drama contains a song naming a real lost ferry or guesthouse. Theatre records, maps, and burial notices each contribute something. The discovery rewards curiosity about ordinary culture rather than treating entertainment as worthless falsehood.

**The return of a familiar stranger.** Another waking of Harth's Guest brings affection, unease, and disputed memories. Some villagers want protection; others want their ancestor acknowledged; the spirit wants to know what became of people long dead. A meal and an honest conversation can matter as much as a quest for a rare gift.

**The garden that watched the stars.** A blackglass restoration takes travellers into a working Serevan neighbourhood. Tradespeople, landlords, astronomically minded scholars, and pilots have different pieces of the answer. The reward is a bounded navigational wonder and new relationships on the farther shore.

**A name on a moved door.** A Bellwade object or Hazelstairs doorframe reveals how a family carried its life elsewhere. The investigation can end with a reunion, a corrected memorial, or the return of an heirloom. It need not become a claim to noble birth or vast wealth.

## Caldris at 1,200 years ago

The political map is more fragmented and the human map more connected. Merchants travel routes no single crown controls. Families have roots on both shores. Some upland fields have returned to pasture; some coastal farmland has become marsh. Repairers reuse the stone of abandoned ambition in buildings people still need.

Few remember Aveline through direct experience now. Her life survives in manuscripts, contradictory stories, and ordinary objects whose histories are sometimes known and sometimes lost. Later elves may remember having met her, but no living present-day witness is assumed. Her death remains part of the world's history.

The great dangers of these centuries were local enough to confront and serious enough to matter. Some monsters were killed. Some losses could only be lived with. Magic preserved a little, failed to preserve much more, and remained inseparable from the people who prepared, taught, paid for, and cared for it.

There is still a bowl set out at an old hearth, although the road no longer passes its door. There are gardens on the farther shore where a patient worker can watch the stars reflected in dark water. There are letters waiting in places their writers never imagined would become ruins.

The next centuries begin with those things still capable of changing someone's journey.
