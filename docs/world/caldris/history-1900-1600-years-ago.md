# Caldris: The Roads Beyond the Hearth

*1,900–1,600 years before the campaign present · Working setting lore · Includes GM secrets*

Continues [The Century of Hearths and Measures](history-2000-1900-years-ago.md). Dates count backwards from the campaign present. The opening describes the world at 1,900 years ago; the dated events then continue without repeating the preceding chapter's final year.

Continues in [The Long Summer and the Returning Roads: 1,600–1,200 years ago](history-1600-1200-years-ago.md).

Lore, locations, and people remain freely revisable before play begins, as authorised by the user. Its core chronology, mapped locations, historical accounts, and separately scoped GM story material are now published in live Caldris. Its present-day situations are available adventure premises, not events already underway. Historical accounts can become discoverable lore; paragraphs marked **GM truth** contain the underlying explanation and should remain separate from player-facing material.

## At the beginning

The Lantern Settlement had made the Chalklands easier to cross. It had not made its inhabitants agree about much else.

A traveller could now expect supper at recognised stopping houses, a witnessed weighing in certain markets, and somebody to complain to when a bridge keeper charged twice. Beyond that dependable corridor lay upland estates, wooded valleys, marsh communities, and coastal towns with their own customs. Most journeys still depended on weather, local knowledge, and the willingness of strangers to share a fire.

Over the next three centuries, those roads would carry brides, refugees, foreign money, religious disputes, and stories about places their listeners could scarcely imagine. A few scattered market towns would become the centres of small kingdoms. Some spirits would find a place in the changing world; others would retreat, bargain badly, or become dangerous.

These centuries concern the Chalklands and a widening circle of neighbours. They are not a universal age shared by all Caldris. Their small miracles, wars, and voyages do not require the rest of the world to have waited for them.

## First century: The Open Roads — 1,900–1,800 years ago

### 1,899–1,888: The Autumn Without a Debt

Aveline Sere's obligation to bring a guest to the Seventh Hearth ended with the preceding century. The following autumn she arrived anyway, accompanied by a young potter called Iven Marr. She carefully explained that they were visiting, not making a payment.

The threshold spirit had prepared seven places. Aveline put five bowls away and asked whether it would prefer three people who were actually hungry. This small disagreement became the first occasion on which the household chose a new custom instead of trying to complete an old one.

Iven stayed through the winter, repairing the chimney and producing lopsided cups. He later established **Marr's Kiln** on a clay seam below the hill road. His cups spread the hearth's seven-notch mark far beyond its original hospitality network. Later collectors would mistake ordinary pottery for proof of membership in an ancient magical fellowship.

**Traces and stories:** A broken cup links an otherwise unrecorded settlement to the early road. A collector wants an entire kiln excavated; the land's occupants need it left undisturbed until after harvest.

**GM truth:** Only three cups received any enchantment, a modest charm that slowed the cooling of a drink. Iven made hundreds of unenchanted copies. Their real historical value is evidence of where ordinary people travelled.

### 1,887–1,875: The Founding of Alderwake Fair

Where the orchard branch met an eastern drovers' track, a landholder granted four days of seasonal market peace. Herds could rest on common ground; debts could be disputed before witnesses; no participant could be arrested for an old private feud until the market closed.

The settlement that grew around this bargain was called **Alderwake**. Its first fairwarden, Ysolde Fen, rented stalls by frontage rather than by the seller's birthplace. Foreign traders welcomed the rule. Local traders discovered that impartiality felt less admirable when it introduced competition.

Musicians, animal healers, cooks, and cheap lodging followed. A fair visitor could buy wool, learn a new dance, and hear an entirely inaccurate description of the sea before noon. Couples began timing marriages to the fair because relatives from several valleys could attend.

**Traces and stories:** Carved stall marks; a marriage custom involving four knots; a fair peace bell with successive replacement clappers. A fugitive claims protection under a later renewal of the fair charter, forcing its current keeper to decide whether the protection covers the alleged offence.

**GM truth:** The original peace deliberately protected some debtors fleeing abusive patrons. Ysolde understood the loophole and preserved it. It did not protect violence committed inside the fair.

### 1,874–1,862: The Three Daughters of the River

Three small riverside lordships entered a marriage settlement intended to stop recurring toll raids. The alliance failed as a family arrangement but succeeded as an administrative one: its toll stations continued sharing escort costs after two marriages ended and the third heir joined a religious house.

**Queen Ceryn of House Keld** assembled those arrangements into the **Kingdom of Low Vey**, a narrow river kingdom reaching toward Veymouth. The title sounded grander than the realm. Ceryn could compel service along the river much more reliably than in villages a day's walk into the woods.

Her reign established marked navigation poles, an appeals court that travelled by boat, and a store of emergency rope and timber at major crossings. These measures made her popular with boat crews and expensive to obey for woodland tenants required to supply the timber.

**Traces and stories:** River stones bearing three interlaced reeds; a drowned tollhouse; boat songs that praise Ceryn while mocking her relatives. A recovered navigation chart records a branch channel through supposedly impassable marsh.

**GM truth:** The three daughters of later legend were not Ceryn's children. They were the three river lordships, represented as women in an early ceremonial painting. Several later pedigrees were built on a literal reading of that picture.

### 1,861–1,849: The Orchard That Could Not Grow

Harth's success encouraged neighbouring landholders to plant more fruit trees. They expected the Guest Beneath the Boughs to protect the new acreage, even though no agreement promised this. After a destructive frost, angry tenants came to the old table carrying dead branches.

The Guest admitted the limits of its shelter. An orchard keeper named **Tessa Brin** proposed a boundary marked by twelve stones and a separate, ordinary mutual-aid store for growers outside it. The renewed compact included explicit duties for pruning, nesting strips, and sharing frost losses.

Tessa also founded **Boughmeet**, a small grafting nursery where different orchard traditions could be compared. Farmers gradually learned which benefits came from the Guest and which came from choosing the right trees for their soil. Neither discovery made the other worthless.

**Traces and stories:** Twelve stones, several later moved; old graft names preserved in nursery catalogues; a feast dish made from bruised fruit. A newly discovered stone would place an abandoned orchard inside the sheltered boundary, but the Guest must still be willing and able to renew the agreement.

**GM truth:** One stone marks a burial, not the magical boundary. Tessa asked that it resemble the others so her partner could remain among the trees without attracting pilgrims. A literal reconstruction from all twelve stones gives the wrong shape.

### 1,848–1,836: The White Hart of Orrel Wood

Travellers vanished in **Orrel Wood**, north of the fair road. Survivors described a white hart standing just beyond the lantern light. Following it led them onto ground that seemed familiar but brought them back to the same black pool.

The creature was hunted by **Branna Holt**, a charcoal burner's daughter, with Aveline and two local foresters. Their expedition lasted nineteen wet days. Branna learned to navigate by the direction of running water when paths became unreliable. They trapped and killed the hart at an exposed limestone sink.

Later ballads gave Branna a shining spear and a royal parent. Contemporary offerings depict a short hunting bow and patched boots. Aveline, who had provided light and broken one of the creature's deceits, repeatedly failed to persuade singers that four people had gone into the wood.

**Traces and stories:** A sinkhole called Hart's Mouth; trail marks cut below normal eye level; antler fragments sealed in a shrine. A modern disappearance follows the old pattern, inviting investigation of the pool and the shrine before assuming the dead creature has returned.

**GM truth:** The hart was a predatory spirit that fed on exhausted, disoriented prey. Its motives were not a misunderstood demand for worship. The hunt ended its life, but a shed antler retained a weaker power to misdirect and was never recovered.

### 1,835–1,823: The Guest Winter at Whitewindow

Masons repairing Ceryn's river works followed an old stone causeway into the northern hills and encountered **Whitewindow**, a dwarven hold whose inhabitants traded mainly through a western route. Neither people had discovered the other; older contact had simply lapsed beyond living local memory.

The first renewed delegation became snowbound. Whitewindow housed twenty-three outsiders for the winter, including children, a mule, and a priest whose dietary concerns the cooks initially mistook for an insult. Shared repairs, card games, and repeated apologies achieved more than the formal negotiations.

The spring agreement exchanged iron fittings and dressed stone for grain, wool, and river transport. It also established a guest hall outside the hold's inner gate. Visitors gained a reliable refuge without being granted access to private homes or ancestral chambers.

**Traces and stories:** Paired surface and subterranean measurements on bridge stones; a winter board game; a guest register written in two hands. A damaged tunnel contains the missing eastern end of the old causeway and evidence of why contact stopped.

**GM truth:** An earlier landslide, followed by competing road investments, had severed the route. There was no ancient betrayal to avenge. A later lord promoted that story to justify intercepting trade through his own pass.

### 1,822–1,810: The Year of Two Springs

An unusually warm early season brought blossom, then hard cold killed much of it. Summer disease weakened draft animals. The year became known as the Year of Two Springs because people replanted while gathering what little survived from the first sowing.

Low Vey's stores helped the river settlements. Upland assistance travelled more slowly. Aveline spent six weeks seeking a charm said to ripen grain, returning with a real but unsuitable spell: it could mature a few stalks for seed selection, not feed a district.

At Rusk Hollow, **Jannet Rusk**, who claimed descent from Maelin without being able to prove it, organised grain exchanges and grazing for surviving animals. Her surviving account begins, "Count the beasts before promising the ploughs." It became a useful maxim long before it became a proverb.

**Traces and stories:** Twice-marked planting tallies; emergency pits beneath later barns; a harvest song with a missing celebratory verse. An archive hunt for the ripening charm can recover a valuable agricultural tool without producing a solution to every future famine.

**GM truth:** Aveline's failure was a failure of scale and timing. She subsequently copied the limits of a spell as carefully as its instructions. Later editors often removed those passages.

### 1,809–1,800: The First High Market

Dunmere's lower terraces had become crowded, muddy, and vulnerable to disputes between their old authorities. Merchants and nearby households funded a new market on the hill above them, with a covered weighing place and a cistern.

The opening is remembered as the **First High Market**, an early stage in Highmead's growth rather than the instant creation of a city. Dunmere continued operating. Porters protested the longer climb; stallholders liked the dry ground; landlords began charging for the view.

The market's mason, **Orren Slatehand** of Whitewindow, left a small shelf beneath the public staircase for lamps and lost objects. People later treated it as a shrine to safe returns. Orren's letters suggest he had simply grown tired of tools being left on the steps.

**Traces and stories:** The cistern's earliest lining; stair stones bearing two masons' traditions; a lamp shelf incorporated into a later wall. Restoring a blocked water channel might save a neighbourhood from shortages but requires entering a cellar its owner will not open.

**GM truth:** The owner of the original disputed cellar had hidden ordinary stolen goods, not a terrible relic. Later history may place something else there, but the old crime does not predetermine it.

## Second century: The Farther Shore — 1,799–1,700 years ago

### 1,799–1,788: The Blue-Sailed Visitors

Ships from **Serevan**, a coastal principality across the **Morrow Strait**, began calling regularly at Veymouth. Their crews knew of earlier crossings; regular, documented exchange was new to these particular ports. Whether the farther shore belongs to another continent can be fixed when the world map is finalised.

They brought blue dyes, citrus peel, fine needles, unfamiliar sailing terms, and letters addressed to people nobody in Veymouth knew. In return they bought worked iron, wool cloth, hides, and selected timber. Each side exaggerated the other's wealth.

The interpreter **Neris Vale** earned a living explaining weights and shipping terms before becoming an envoy. Her surname does not establish kinship with Derren. She insisted that contracts name the language that governed a disputed phrase, then earned less romantic renown than the captains who ignored her advice.

**Traces and stories:** Blue pigment in an old harbour mural; foreign needles in inland graves; paired-language cargo labels. A letter stored as a meaningless curiosity turns out to contain directions to a family grave on the farther shore.

**GM truth:** Several blue sails were patched with locally dyed cloth within a decade. Colour alone cannot identify a wreck's origin, although treasure dealers confidently claim otherwise.

### 1,787–1,775: The Blackglass Pilot

A Serevan navigator, **Samir Orel**, reached Veymouth carrying a dark glass disk that revealed a pale arc beneath particular stars. With observations and careful reckoning, it helped identify one safe approach between offshore shoals. It could not steer a ship or calm the sea.

Samir married a local rope-maker, **Hessa Marr**, and taught three apprentices. The port funded a low observatory on **Lanterns' End**, a headland well outside the river mouth. Its first winter nearly ruined the project because smoke from the keeper's stove spoiled the observations.

Pilots charged for their trained judgement. Sailors told stories of a disk that knew every safe harbour, and several wealthy households spent heavily trying to acquire copies of an ability the original never possessed.

**Traces and stories:** Sight grooves in coastal stonework; observation books preserved through later copying; a children's rhyme about keeping the chimney downwind. Recovering the disk helps only if its inscriptions and the relevant coastal changes can also be understood.

**GM truth:** Samir made the disk from a salvaged shard whose maker he never identified. The claim that it came from a drowned city is possible but unproven, left as a thread for later exploration.

### 1,774–1,762: The Dispute of Open Doors

Some religious houses sheltered travellers accused of breaking spirit bargains. Others refused anyone who had participated in such an agreement. Merchants found that an acceptable oath at one inn might be condemned at the next sanctuary.

At **Reedminster**, an island abbey in Low Vey, **Mother Adra Sen** convened clerics, lay hosts, orchard keepers, and two willing local spirits. Their agreement concerned conduct: shelter could not require worship; bargains needed understandable terms; no host could claim a guest's descendants as payment.

Several congregations rejected the meeting's authority. Others adopted its principles without sharing its theology. The resulting **Open Door Houses** were a fellowship of participating hosts, not a single church controlling Caldris's beliefs.

**Traces and stories:** A door carved with an unfastened latch; guest rules copied beside prayers; a small landing reserved for visitors unwilling to enter the abbey. A sanctuary asks for mediators when two protected guests accuse each other of coercion.

**GM truth:** The agreement's most disputed prohibition arose from a human landlord's practice of inheritable service, not from a spirit demanding children. Some later religious polemics reversed the story.

### 1,761–1,749: The Summer of Ragged Pennants

A contested Low Vey succession drew hired soldiers along the river. Their wages failed before their contracts ended. Some returned home, some hired themselves to towns, and others seized boats and granaries.

Captain **Elian Rook** assembled village levies and river crews to reopen three crossings. His most famous engagement, at **Ashford Weir**, ended when local millers released a controlled flow that stranded the raiders' boats on a gravel bar. Several encounters elsewhere were bloody and left bereaved households on both banks.

The settlement restricted private armed toll collection and left competing claimants with separate territories. The crown survived in reduced form. Graves and missing labour mattered to the river communities more than the legal phrasing of the peace.

**Traces and stories:** Shield fittings under a rebuilt weir; paired memorial stones on opposite banks; a marching tune used as a rowing song. A grave disturbed during repairs contains evidence that the celebrated victor's banner was carried by a unit omitted from official accounts.

**GM truth:** Rook knowingly let one band escape in return for releasing hostages. Some later attacked a distant hamlet. He was an effective protector whose decision had consequences beyond the people he could see.

### 1,748–1,736: The House of Unfinished Spells

Aveline lodged for several years at **Sallowmere**, a quiet lakeside teaching house. With the human scholar **Mira Tern** and dwarven copyist **Belda Venn**, she compared surviving versions of the Book of Gentle Arts. Belda's surname establishes no relation to Sella.

They found missing limits, substitutions, errors, and charming nonsense copied as instruction. Mira established a practice of recording failed attempts beside successful ones. Their workshop acquired the affectionate name House of Unfinished Spells because its most useful shelves were labelled "Perhaps" and "Do Not Heat Again."

Only a handful of residents could work magic. Others prepared materials, translated, tested ordinary alternatives, cooked, or kept records. Their best-known achievement was a reliable treatment for a library mould that required no spell at all.

**Traces and stories:** Cautions written in red beside household charms; test tiles; a scorched bronze spoon preserved as an instructional object. A dangerous copied spell can be corrected by recovering its discarded preparation notes.

**GM truth:** One genuine experiment could briefly preserve a sound in a prepared ceramic cavity. Aveline hoped to reconstruct Derren's voice, but there was no original recording. The work could preserve future speech, never retrieve an unrecorded past.

### 1,735–1,723: The Night Harbour

A winter storm uncovered a sheltered inlet south of Veymouth. **Nacre Quay** grew there around boat sheds and a natural channel deep enough for the visiting Serevan ships. Its new landing cut unloading time, and trade shifted faster than Veymouth's councils believed possible.

Nacre's first harbourmaster, **Dovren Sal**, accepted merchants excluded from the older port's guilds. That openness brought skills and money, but also operators eager to evade debts and unsafe cargo inspections. Both ports accused the other of ruining honest trade.

The rivalry produced competing lights along the coast. A joint night-signal code eventually prevented more wrecks than either port's independent defences against piracy.

**Traces and stories:** Lamp towers with shutter fittings; pottery impressed with a shell; a waterfront street bearing an inland dialect name. A false coastal signal draws a vessel toward danger, and the investigators must distinguish deliberate wrecking from a mistaken restoration of an obsolete code.

**GM truth:** Nacre's celebrated first welcome to all ships was conditional on fees few small local fishers could afford. Its openness was real and unevenly distributed.

### 1,722–1,710: The Listening Stones of Cairn Moss

Peat cutters found a ring of low stones at **Cairn Moss**, west of Whitewindow's surface road. At dusk, a person seated within the ring sometimes heard a few words spoken there long before. The sounds repeated imperfectly and could not answer questions.

Belda travelled to record them. Among formal phrases in an unfamiliar language she heard a child protesting cold feet and someone laughing with their mouth full. Later patrons wanted royal secrets. Belda's notes gave equal space to the laughter.

Excavation revealed several rebuilding phases older than the present chapter. The stones were left in place after lifting one silenced its side of the ring. The local cutters received payment to preserve the site and quickly disagreed over how it should be divided.

**Traces and stories:** Belda's phonetic transcriptions; a peat-stained survey cord sealed in a later reliquary; one silent stone. Adventurers can help reconstruct a damaged arrangement or identify a repeated phrase whose meaning has been badly mistranslated.

**GM truth:** The ring preserves fragments of sound under particular conditions. It contains no captive souls and provides no complete historical replay. Who first built it and why remain intentionally unresolved.

### 1,709–1,700: The First Lantern League

Highmead's markets, Alderwake, several road houses, and river towns agreed to recognise a limited set of sealed promises of payment. The arrangement reduced the amount of silver carried over the roads, but worked only between participating counters with known guarantors.

This **Lantern League** grew from the older settlement's practices without replacing every local government. Its couriers carried ordinary letters as well as accounts. Some routes became reliable enough for separated households to exchange news several times a year.

The league's first public feast served dishes from all its participating towns. Arguments over precedence continued until the cooks began serving before the seating was settled. Aveline attended as a witness to the old hospitality marks and was dismayed to find her portrait on commemorative cups.

**Traces and stories:** Lead seals bearing a lamp and paired initials; courier rest stones; copied settlement rolls. A sealed packet preserves a private letter that someone paid dearly to keep travelling even after its recipient moved away.

**GM truth:** Early league promises expired and required verification. An ancient seal is historical evidence, not a bearer instrument redeemable for unlimited modern wealth. Later endowments and renewed obligations can still make particular records matter.

## Third century: The Divided Lamps — 1,699–1,600 years ago

### 1,699–1,688: The Fever at the Lower Gate

A severe fever struck the crowded lodging streets below Highmead. Traders avoided the lower quarter; residents who could afford to leave carried their fear into neighbouring towns. Ordinary illness, poor living conditions, and delayed care caused the crisis. Contemporary accusations of a foreign curse had no foundation.

**Leth Arven**, a washerwoman who organised food and clean bedding, became one of the quarter's remembered protectors. A few magical healers helped individuals, but their limited numbers could not reach everyone. Civic records later celebrated donated silver more thoroughly than the daily work of carers and gravediggers.

The aftermath brought a second water source, less crowded lodging rules, and a burial garden beyond the lower gate. Implementation took years and repeatedly stalled over who should pay.

**Traces and stories:** A communal memorial with names added in different hands; a blocked lodging court; a charitable washing house rebuilt on the old site. Restoring the burial garden uncovers personal tokens useful for identifying forgotten families.

**GM truth:** Leth survived and strongly disliked being depicted as a beautiful young martyr. Her last known petition demanded replacement buckets and payment for three months of unpaid work.

### 1,687–1,675: The Crown Above the Market

As Low Vey's rival branches disputed authority, the upland estates and Highmead's growing councils recognised **Marwen of House Aster** as sovereign of the **Crown of Highmead**. The new arrangement joined several existing obligations rather than conquering the whole Chalklands.

Marwen promised protection of the road, confirmation of certain commons, and regular audiences for market grievances. In return, the councils funded a standing household guard and helped maintain a hill residence. Smaller villages feared becoming revenue sources for a prosperous town.

Her coronation used a locally made circlet of silver and dark iron. The ceremony included the old public weighing stones, borrowing the reputation of Sella's reforms. It did not make the new crown heir to every earlier upland ruler.

**Traces and stories:** Iron-and-silver motifs; an audience bench worn unevenly by later reuse; contradictory boundary surveys. A modern collector offers a supposed coronation circlet whose alloy and repairs suggest a more complicated identity.

**GM truth:** The original circlet was later dismantled to pay a hostage ransom. A contemporary replacement became the object used in later ceremonies. Authenticity can belong to a history of use as well as to an untouched first object.

### 1,674–1,662: The Broken Counter

Two poor trading seasons and a lost cargo exposed the Lantern League's dependence on a small number of guarantors. A counter at Alderwake failed to honour its promises. Other counters restricted payment, and people who had sold real wool discovered that their carefully sealed papers would not buy bread.

The crisis ended the first league in its original form. Some counters paid slowly over years; others disappeared. **Teren Iss**, an itinerant clerk, recorded claims for households too small to command a hearing. His lists preserved both the scale of the loss and the unequal recovery.

A narrower association eventually required visible reserves and limited shared guarantees. It inherited the lamp emblem, creating later confusion over which league had promised what.

**Traces and stories:** Cancelled seals; household ledgers with prices crossed out; a fair custom of publicly opening the market chest. A forged claim tries to attach a modern charity to the wrong historical institution.

**GM truth:** The initial failure was not a master theft. One official then concealed losses to protect his relatives, turning a solvency crisis into a fraud. Different harmed families have different responsible parties.

### 1,661–1,649: The Thistlewood Bride

A shepherd named **Oren Bell** disappeared into **Thistlewood** after attending a wedding feast beneath flowering branches. He returned eleven years later, believing that three evenings had passed. His betrothed had married someone else, his mother had died, and the disputed pasture he remembered had become a young wood.

Oren's account described a fey household celebrating its own marriage, not a human bride carried away. The popular title arose from a ballad that replaced the difficult aftermath with a rescue. Oren spent his remaining years repairing instruments and refusing invitations from strangers after sunset.

The entrance appeared only during a rare flowering around an old boundary arch. Villagers dismantled the visible arch, but its buried foundations remained. This was an exceptional crossing into a place where time passed differently, not ordinary behaviour in every Caldris wood.

**Traces and stories:** A fiddle with eleven small cuts; flowering shrubs in an unnatural rectangle; two incompatible marriage songs. A renewed bloom offers a possible search for another missing visitor, with the risk of altered time made discoverable before entry.

**GM truth:** Oren was welcome to leave but was not told what delay would mean outside. His hosts regarded the omission as discourtesy, not abduction. That failure of understanding does not erase the harm, nor does it make accepting a second invitation safe.

### 1,648–1,636: The Winter Teeth

Heavy upland snow drove a solitary **rime drake** toward the Whitewindow road. It killed pack animals, occupied a sheltered quarry, and made a vital winter route unusable. Villagers called it the Winter King, although it possessed neither subjects nor a human conception of kingship.

The hunt brought together Whitewindow engineers, the crown's soldiers, drovers, and two practitioners able to oppose its freezing breath. **Sivra Coalhand**, a dwarven quarry surveyor, designed a trap using abandoned workings. Aveline helped keep a retreat passage clear; the decisive risk fell to workers who had to set the supports within reach of the creature.

They killed the drake. Three hunters died, several were permanently injured, and the reopening feast included places for those who would not return. Later paintings gave all the participants excellent armour and omitted the mules.

**Traces and stories:** A collapsed quarry mouth; hunting payments; a blue-white tooth in a regional treasury. Recovering the actual casualty roll restores names to a damaged memorial. Exploring the old workings may find dangerous unstable galleries and scavengers attracted to the remains.

**GM truth:** A clutch of mineral-encrusted stones displayed as the drake's eggs was a profitable misidentification. The creature left no offspring at the quarry. Its genuine tooth retains a faint chill but is not automatically a powerful weapon.

### 1,635–1,623: The War of the Two Bridges

Highmead's crown sponsored a new river crossing to reduce reliance on Low Vey's tolls. Rival authorities seized construction materials, detained engineers, and eventually sent armed forces. Fighting spread around the two competing crossings, far downstream from Merecross; neither bridge is the modern Bramblebridge structure.

The **Redbank Bridge** was burned while incomplete. The older **King's Span** survived after its keeper, **Dama Noll**, admitted a mixed delegation of townspeople and refused both armies exclusive access to the gatehouse stores. Her position held because neither side could provision a long siege without damaging its own trade.

The peace created a shared crossing district and compensated some displaced households. It also abandoned the half-built bridge, leaving its piers to divide the river long after the immediate rivalry faded.

**Traces and stories:** Isolated piers; a shared graveyard; a bridge keeper's key too large for any surviving lock. A submerged construction camp becomes accessible after a channel shift, exposing stolen material from several jurisdictions.

**GM truth:** Dama had prepared the stores for flood relief, not for a brilliant military manoeuvre. Her celebrated strategy began as an improvised response using years of unglamorous preparedness.

### 1,622–1,610: The Orchard's Long Sleep

The Guest Beneath the Boughs appeared less often at Harth. Its speech became slow and its spring shelter unreliable. Keepers worried that the compact had been broken. Boughmeet's records showed that the Guest's periods of wakefulness had shortened before, though never within the memory of the current households.

At the autumn meal it asked for a long sleep beneath the oldest root ground. Growers negotiated an end to the expectation of annual frost protection while retaining the unpicked strip and nesting habitat as practices valuable in themselves. A small watch of volunteers maintained the resting place without promising when the Guest would return.

The orchards endured through ordinary grafting, careful planting, shared reserves, and occasional hardship. In later generations, people disagreed about whether the sleeper was a spirit, a saint, or a story invented to explain an inconvenient boundary.

**Traces and stories:** A low root mound; songs sung quietly during blossom; nursery books recording frost damage after the shelter ceased. Construction threatens the resting ground, raising the question of whether to seek contact, protect its sleep, or move a project elsewhere.

**GM truth:** The Guest was tired, not secretly murdered or punishing the villagers. Its long sleep can extend across centuries. Later history must establish any awakening; present-day stories need not assume that it still appears at the annual feast.

### 1,609–1,600: The Last Copy and the Eastern Road

At Sallowmere, Aveline completed a personal copy of the Book of Gentle Arts. She retained practical advice, failed attempts, the limits of enchantments, and Derren's marginal notes. Beside his observation that rain came through a supposedly waterproof hood, she wrote, "He was right."

Mira had died long before. Belda, now elderly, arranged the shelves while complaining about Aveline's habit of putting papers wherever a cat was not sitting. Their work had outlived most of its first readers without ever becoming a great academy.

At the Seventh Hearth, Aveline left a plain travel cup and took one of Iven's uneven replacements. She then departed east with a caravan carrying dyes, surgical needles, and letters for the farther shore. Her destination was a Serevan town named in a fragmentary address, where someone reportedly knew the origin of Samir's blackglass.

**Traces and stories:** Three imperfectly related copies; a plain cup with a repaired handle; a farewell letter whose practical directions occupy more space than its sentiments. A long campaign can follow the eastern journey through later centuries, discovering what happened without requiring Aveline to be alive in the present.

**GM truth:** She left because she wanted to see something new and finally felt able to go. There was no prophecy summoning her. At this point she had lived for several centuries, not two millennia; her eventual fate remains open.

## Places to add to the world

The following present-day forms are working destinations for later stories. Their ancient origins are established here; subsequent centuries can explain rebuilding, abandonment, and changing political control. None requires an institution or bloodline to survive unchanged for 1,600 years.

### Alderwake — the town that packs away its streets

**Location:** East of Harth's orchard branch, where a drovers' track meets the route toward the river. **Livelihood:** Livestock, cloth, lodging, and seasonal fairs.

The ancient fairground becomes a small town whose widest lanes remain open for temporary stalls. On ordinary days it feels half empty. On fair days kitchens overflow into courtyards and children navigate by stall awnings rather than house numbers. The original fair bell is lost; successive bells inherit its name.

**Present-day person:** Fairwarden **Merren Auld** is excellent at negotiating stall disputes and quietly afraid of horses. She wants a fair small sellers can still afford.

**Adventure:** A protected trader vanishes between entering the fair and collecting a reserved stall. The evidence leads through custody arrangements, a debt claim, and an invitation to perform at a private supper. The case can be resolved as a local mystery without invoking any ancient curse.

### Boughmeet — the nursery of remembered fruit

**Location:** On the southern approach to Harth Orchard. **Livelihood:** Grafting stock, seedlings, cider, and practical teaching.

Plots bear names of villages that no longer exist. The oldest varieties persist through deliberate propagation, not immortal trees. A row of training pears grows against a warm stone wall, each labelled with both its market name and the name used by the person who brought it.

**Present-day person:** **Nali Brin**, a nursery keeper with no documented connection to Tessa, is trying to save a sour, unpopular pear because it survives a disease affecting valuable stock.

**Adventure:** The last healthy graft of a resistant variety was sent away as a wedding gift. Finding it requires following family moves and recognising a tree nobody now calls by its original name.

### Orrel Wood and Hart's Mouth — the forest with a finished hunt

**Location:** North of the Alderwake road. **Livelihood:** Managed coppice, charcoal, mushrooms, and limited timber cutting.

The wood has been felled and regrown in sections many times. Hart's Mouth remains an identifiable limestone sink, surrounded by younger trees. A modest shrine commemorates Branna and, in newer inscriptions, her companions. Forest workers know reliable paths that romantic visitor maps omit.

**Present-day person:** **Tovin Holt**, a forester, resents paid guides claiming every white deer is supernatural. He carries a careful sketch of the missing antler because that particular danger may be real.

**Adventure:** A collector's newly acquired antler starts redirecting people inside a manor. Recovering Branna's methods offers a way to navigate the effect and safely contain its source.

### Whitewindow — the hold behind the guest hall

**Location:** Northern stone hills reached by a branch road from the Chalklands. **Livelihood:** Metal fittings, stonework, repair contracts, and upland husbandry.

Pale mineral seams give the outer cliff the appearance of narrow lit windows. Most trade takes place in a rebuilt guest hall with a very old floor. Interior neighbourhoods are homes, not an invitation to explore ancestral rooms. The hold imports food in good years as well as bad and regards reliable contracts as part of its winter defences.

**Present-day person:** Guestmaster **Runa Tal** cares intensely about making visitors comfortable and believes comfort includes explaining every rule before anyone accidentally breaks one.

**Adventure:** A blocked surface route threatens winter deliveries. The old causeway could help, but reopening it crosses a burial chamber exposed by a later landslide. A practical alternative may require more labour and less treasure hunting.

### Reedminster — the abbey with a landing for outsiders

**Location:** A low island in the lower Vey river system, upstream of the tidal ports. **Livelihood:** Reed work, gardens, ferry service, and donations for hospitality.

Its buildings have been repeatedly raised above flood level. A landing shelter stands outside the devotional precinct, preserving the distinction between accepting hospitality and joining worship. Old guest rules survive through generations of commentary, including disagreements written neatly in the margins.

**Present-day person:** **Brother Cassel**, responsible for the landing, dislikes theological debate but is skilled at helping angry people sit far enough apart to eat.

**Adventure:** Two travellers arrive with conflicting accounts of an inherited service agreement. One wants sanctuary; the other also needs shelter and insists they came to negotiate. Establishing what either can freely refuse becomes the centre of the story.

### Sallowmere — the quiet shore of difficult little spells

**Location:** A shallow lake west of the orchard country, linked to it by a local cart road. **Livelihood:** Fishing, reeds, copying, and a small guesthouse.

The original teaching house is partly incorporated into a later library and partly a garden of low foundations. Its collection has been dispersed and reassembled more than once. A narrow room is still called the Spoon Room after an object that may now be a replacement of a replacement.

**Present-day person:** Copyist **Iria Senn** earns more producing clean accounts than preserving magical annotations. She nevertheless keeps a shelf of failed experiments because failures are often what make the surviving successes comprehensible.

**Adventure:** Patrons fund a reconstruction of the sound-preserving vessel, each hoping to hear a different dead person's voice. The original notes establish that it can record someone living now. The emotional choice concerns what to preserve for the future.

### Lanterns' End — the headland of shuttered lights

**Location:** On the coast beyond Veymouth's river mouth, overlooking the approaches from Morrow Strait. **Livelihood:** Pilotage, sheep grazing, and maintenance of a coastal light.

Successive towers stand on and beside the low observatory's foundations. Some carved sight lines still point usefully across the water; others refer to vanished sandbanks. The sea has eaten part of the old keeper's garden.

**Present-day person:** Pilot **Sahel Marr** is documenting coastal change and refuses to treat an old chart as a current promise of safety.

**Adventure:** A newly found observation book can help locate a wreck, but its landmarks must be translated into the modern shoreline. A second party follows it literally and needs rescue.

### Nacre Quay — the harbour beside its older self

**Location:** A sheltered coastal inlet south of Veymouth. **Livelihood:** Coastal cargo, fishing, rope work, and marine repair.

The original night harbour is now a shallow boat basin within a larger, repeatedly rebuilt port. Old shell marks survive on stones reused in warehouses. Its rivalry with Veymouth is expressed as often through rowing contests and insulting pastries as through serious commercial competition.

**Present-day person:** **Edda Sal**, a night watch supervisor, knows the signal lamps better than the merchants who fund them. She wants money for replacement shutters before another winter.

**Adventure:** A lamp produces an obsolete signal only on certain wet nights. The explanation may be a damaged shutter and a copied repair diagram; someone who benefits from the resulting confusion has started keeping it broken.

### Cairn Moss — the field of half-heard voices

**Location:** Peat country west of Whitewindow's surface road. **Livelihood:** Limited peat cutting, grazing, and guide work.

The listening ring lies in a protected patch within working land. Its stones were reset after later disturbance, and only part of the original effect persists. Visitors often hear nothing. Local families maintain paths because losing someone in the moss is a greater concern than disappointing a scholar.

**Present-day person:** Guide **Fenna Reed** keeps a notebook of conditions on evenings when sounds occur. She regards both credulous enthusiasts and blanket sceptics as tiresome walking companions.

**Adventure:** Compare present sounds with Belda's transcriptions to locate a misplaced stone. The recovered phrase offers a new lead into the ring's older history, not a complete explanation.

### Redbank Reach — the bridge that ended in the river

**Location:** On the main river downstream of the Bramblebridge area and upstream of Reedminster. **Livelihood:** Barges, mills, pasture, and a working ferry near later bridges.

Redbank's unfinished piers remain as heavily eroded stone islands, altered by salvage and repair. King's Span was replaced more than once and is now represented by abutments near a newer crossing. The shared district outlived the original peace only through later arrangements, some of which still influence local identity.

**Present-day person:** Ferry operator **Dessa Noll** tells Dama's story as family tradition but admits her documented ancestry reaches back only a few generations.

**Adventure:** Floodwater exposes a gatehouse store below a newer foundation. Recovering its relief supplies is less useful than recovering the record of an emergency channel that could protect the modern settlement.

### Thistlewood Arch — the invitation beneath the roots

**Location:** In wooded pasture beyond Wetherfold, away from the main road. **Livelihood:** Surrounding smallholdings; the arch itself supports no settlement.

The visible arch was dismantled in Oren's time. Its foundations lie beneath a rectangular thicket that flowers oddly in exceptional years. No continuously open portal or convenient shortcut is established. A surviving fiddle and regional songs supply clues to the old crossing's cost.

**Present-day person:** Fiddler **Wenna Bell** inherited the instrument through a documented recent purchase, not a proven ancient bloodline. She is learning why one tune makes local elders uncomfortable.

**Adventure:** A flowering coincides with the discovery of a formal invitation. Investigating the story first allows travellers to make an informed decision about attending, seeking a missing person, or leaving the opening alone.

## Peoples, powers, and changing loyalties

| Power or community | Role during these centuries | Material for later history |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kingdom of Low Vey / House Keld | River protection, courts, navigation, and increasingly disputed toll revenue | Rival branches, shared crossing districts, service traditions, abandoned royal works |
| Crown of Highmead / House Aster | A late-century upland crown dependent on councils and estate agreements | Negotiated commons, urban privilege, crown debt, villages seeking a voice |
| Serevan | A farther-shore principality connected by recurring sea trade | Migrant families, multilingual archives, changing alliances, the origin of blackglass |
| Whitewindow | A dwarven hold with its own internal life and external supply needs | Guest customs, western connections, repair obligations, routes beyond the chapter's map |
| First Lantern League and its narrower successor | Trusted counters and courier routes, followed by failure and reorganisation | Competing institutional memories, renewed funds, dispersed correspondence |
| Open Door Houses | Independently governed hosts sharing selected hospitality principles | Local interpretations, sanctuary disputes, cooperation across different faiths |
| Harth and Boughmeet growers | A revised spirit compact and increasingly transferable agricultural knowledge | The sleeper's protection, graft lineages, shared reserves, land-use conflicts |
| Sallowmere's small scholarly community | Practical magic, copying, ordinary experiments, and recorded limitations | Lost notes, changed vocabulary, modest wonders, incomplete personal histories |

These are historically specific formations. Their modern survival, borders, religions, and rulers remain for later centuries to establish. Neither a dynasty nor a league should be carried unchanged across sixteen centuries merely because its name is useful.

## Longer stories growing from the period

**The letters beyond the sea:** Begin with a private letter at a former courier house, then trace its copied address through Veymouth, Nacre, Lanterns' End, and Serevan. The personal purpose can be delivering an apology or locating a grave. Blackglass and Aveline's journey provide a deeper thread without requiring every stop to serve one mystery.

**The winter road:** A present supply disruption brings old Whitewindow routes, Redbank's emergency channels, and the relief customs of Rusk Hollow into the same story. Different communities offer incomplete solutions. Travel, negotiation, repair, and one dangerous passage can all matter.

**What the gentle arts forgot:** Contradictory manuscripts promise grand wonders. Comparison reveals practical limits, missing people, and several genuinely useful spells. Recovery can culminate in preserving a living voice rather than raising a dead one. The reward is both a modest wonder and a better record of the people who made it.

**The sleeper and the growing town:** Nursery expansion, housing needs, and an old root ground bring Harth's arrangements into conflict. Evidence establishes what was actually promised. Nobody must awaken the Guest for the story to have stakes; protecting its rest may be a chosen outcome.

**A song with missing names:** Branna's hunt, the Winter Teeth, and the fever memorial show different ways public memory simplifies collective effort. Restoring names can lead to an expedition, a family reconciliation, or a public dispute. Some celebrated deeds remain admirable after the fuller truth is known.

**The invitation:** A fey opening offers real wonder and discoverable danger. The missing person may want to return, may have built a life elsewhere, or may have already left by another route. Decide those circumstances for the eventual adventure rather than treating Oren's experience as a script all visitors must repeat.

## What has changed by 1,600 years ago

Highmead has become a political centre above its older markets. River authority is divided and negotiated. Coastal trade reaches a known farther shore; inland households encounter foreign cloth, names, and customs through ordinary commerce. Credit has proved useful and capable of failing. Religious hospitality has acquired several competing explanations but some shared practices.

Magic remains unevenly available. A few small arts are better documented, while others are lost or misunderstood. The orchard spirit sleeps. The white hart and the rime drake are dead. The listening stones still preserve fragments whose makers remain unknown. None of these events turns every valley into a place of daily spectacular sorcery.

Aveline has left the familiar road. Derren's words survive because someone finally chose to copy the margins. At the Seventh Hearth, travellers continue bringing their own food, and the host has learned to ask how many people are actually coming.

The next age inherits working roads, unfinished settlements, scattered ruins, and plenty of people with reasons to go somewhere new.
