# Caldris creative charter

Status: **confirmed direction for review-content authoring; no runtime authority**
Ruleset alignment: **dnd2024-compatible**
Owner: [D&D 2024 low-magic two-continent world implementation map](DND2024-LOW-MAGIC-TWO-CONTINENT-WORLD-IMPLEMENTATION-MAP.md)
Working setting name: **Caldris**
Confirmed from the user's direction: 2026-08-30

## Authorial mandate

The author may invent most names, places, histories, factions, characters, secrets, creatures,
plots, and quests without stopping for approval at every creative choice. New material must follow
this charter and the implementation map. Permanent IDs, schemas, D&D rule changes, live imports,
and completed-feature acceptance retain their repository confirmation gates.

The setting should feel lighthearted but credible. Most people are neither solemn lore-delivery
devices nor constant comedians: they have jobs, pride, blind spots, loyalties, habits, social
awkwardness, private grief, and different senses of humour. Eccentric, cringeworthy, strict,
laid-back, fussy, theatrical, shy, dry, impulsive, and deeply earnest personalities should coexist.

## Adopted creative defaults

These defaults guide authoring and may be revised before live content receives permanent IDs.

| Decision | Adopted direction |
| --- | --- |
| World | **Caldris**, a pronounceable working name with no runtime ID yet. |
| Continents | Two culturally and geographically varied continents; neither is the “civilized” or “exotic” one. Names are chosen during the macro-world slice. |
| Political scale | Twelve sovereign polities: ten kingdoms and two non-kingdom states for institutional variety. |
| History | Seven named eras, including at least two before the present kingdoms and one recent rupture within living memory. |
| Material baseline | Broadly late-medieval material life with regional variation: fortified towns, guilds, mills, long-distance shipping, written administration, plate armor, difficult roads, and no industrial economy. Gunpowder is absent from the first campaign unless separately introduced. |
| Magic | Real, wondrous, and socially consequential, but uncommon in ordinary households, trades, medicine, travel, and government. Player abilities work as accepted D&D rules permit. |
| Religion | Important to calendars, charity, burial, legitimacy, vows, and community life. Reliable high-level miracles are rare; faith is not proof of spellcasting. |
| Campaign | A level 1–10 first campaign, paced by reviewed milestones when that owner is available, beginning in one deeply authored river-and-hill kingdom. |
| Characters | A mixed group of locals and newcomers. The campaign gives each character a reason to care about the starting community without requiring one shared origin. |
| Play mix | Approximately 40% social interaction and mystery, 25% exploration and travel, 20% danger/combat, and 15% festivals, work, recovery, home, and other quiet play. These are tonal weights, not session quotas. |
| Political tone | Institutions have material interests and believable limits. Intrigue can be serious without assuming everyone is corrupt or every kindness is manipulation. |
| Content intensity | A flexible PG-13 baseline: death, grief, war, poverty, illness, and injustice can matter, but cruelty and gore are not lingered over for spectacle. |
| Romance | Optional and character-led, never assumed or used as the only route to an NPC's cooperation. |
| Naming | Distinct regional sound families, short enough to remember and mostly pronounceable at sight. Avoid apostrophe-heavy fantasy names and direct copies of one real culture. |

## Emotional rhythm

The default rhythm is:

1. establish ordinary life, warmth, personality, or humour;
2. introduce a concrete pressure that matters to someone;
3. let choices produce believable reactions and costs;
4. allow serious consequences to land without a joke immediately dissolving them; and
5. return eventually to connection, repair, changed routine, or renewed purpose.

Lighthearted does not mean that every scene contains a punchline. Realistic does not mean cynical,
colourless, or uniformly miserable. Sad events are used because something valuable was present,
not merely to prove that the setting is mature.

## Humour contract

Humour should usually arise from character and situation:

- incompatible manners, priorities, or assumptions;
- people taking a small responsibility magnificently seriously;
- bureaucracy, guild rules, heraldry, titles, tolls, and ceremonies colliding with practical life;
- affectionate teasing among people who have earned that familiarity;
- eccentric expertise and obsessive hobbies;
- social overconfidence, awkward attempts to impress, and painfully rehearsed speeches;
- animals, weather, food, clothing, tools, and inconvenient architecture behaving materially;
- understatement, dry observation, misplaced formality, and local expressions; and
- the gap between a grand legend and the ordinary people tasked with maintaining its monument.

Humour must not:

- turn every character into the same sarcastic voice;
- mock sincere grief, a player's vulnerability, or a serious choice while it is unfolding;
- make incompetence the only trait of a cringeworthy or eccentric person;
- use cruelty toward powerless people as the punchline;
- rely on modern meta-commentary that breaks the storybook setting; or
- repeatedly cancel tension before players can respond to it.

An awkward person may still be kind and highly capable. A strict person may be fair, frightened,
ambitious, or secretly sentimental. A laid-back person may notice more than anyone else. The joke
should reveal personality or setting rather than replace it.

## Serious and sad material

The setting permits deeper events: a failed harvest, an old war, a broken friendship, a preventable
death, exile, a vanished community, a disastrous succession, a dragon's past bargain, institutional
injustice, or a choice whose best outcome still costs something.

When such material appears:

- establish who and what was valued before showing the loss;
- keep causes specific rather than treating suffering as background decoration;
- let different people grieve, cope, deny, remember, joke, work, pray, or withdraw differently;
- preserve consequences in relationships, customs, places, politics, and later choices;
- include avenues for care, repair, justice, memorial, prevention, or meaningful endurance;
- avoid immediately replacing sadness with a gag; and
- avoid destroying every refuge or beloved NPC merely to manufacture stakes.

The first campaign may contain tragedy but is not nihilistic. Most victories can improve something
real even when they cannot restore everything.

## NPC personality palette

Recurring casts should use contrasts. No settlement needs every type, and no personality is a fixed
alignment.

| Palette role | Playable expression | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earnest eccentric | Knows an improbable amount about one subject and assumes others are interested. | Random behaviour without competence or motive. |
| Socially cringeworthy | Rehearses charm, boasts badly, overshares, or misreads the room while wanting genuine approval. | Humiliation as their only purpose. |
| Strict professional | Values standards, duty, safety, or fairness and expects others to prepare. | Automatic cruelty or obstruction. |
| Strict traditionalist | Protects a custom that once solved a real problem, even if it now causes friction. | “Old ways” without history or internal doubt. |
| Laid-back observer | Appears unhurried, listens carefully, and acts decisively when the important detail emerges. | Apathy disguised as wisdom. |
| Cheerful meddler | Helps, introduces, feeds, and rearranges other people's lives with alarming confidence. | Removing player choice or becoming omniscient. |
| Gentle grump | Complains fluently while quietly performing acts of service. | Constant hostility without warmth. |
| Grandiose amateur | Has an impressive title, costume, plan, or vocabulary and less experience than advertised. | Being useless in every scene. |
| Dry realist | Responds to absurdity with practical understatement. | Giving every NPC the same modern sarcasm. |
| Literal scholar | Answers the exact question, misses the social purpose, and values accuracy sincerely. | Treating neurodivergent-coded behaviour as a cheap joke. |
| Dramatic romantic | Frames errands, feuds, and weather as part of a sweeping personal legend. | Forcing romance or melodrama onto players. |
| Overprepared worrier | Carries lists, spare string, weather notes, and several contingency plans. | Being correct about every danger. |
| Warm rogue | Bends small rules, knows everyone, and has a personal line they will not cross. | Charming away all consequences. |
| Tired authority | Has real responsibility, limited time, and a surprisingly specific private hobby. | Making every official corrupt or foolish. |
| Quietly intense craftsperson | Speaks little, notices workmanship, and expresses care through precise action. | Mystique without relationships or ordinary needs. |

Every recurring NPC still receives an immediate want, long-term want, competence, vulnerability,
obligation, boundary, kindness, misconception, knowledge state, and capacity to change. The palette
guides performance; it does not replace motive.

## Cast-composition rules

For a typical settlement's six to twelve recurring NPCs:

- include at least one strict and one laid-back person whose approaches are both sometimes useful;
- include one eccentric person who is genuinely competent;
- include one awkward or cringeworthy person who can earn respect and is not merely a target;
- vary age, class, occupation, confidence, humour, faith, family situation, and relationship to
  authority;
- give at least two NPCs a friendship, rivalry, debt, family tie, mentorship, or former alliance
  unrelated to the player characters;
- give at least one apparent comic character a serious concern and one stern character a source of
  warmth;
- avoid clustering all villains into one personality type; and
- allow first impressions to be incomplete without making every person secretly the opposite.

## Realism contract

Consequences follow material conditions. Travel consumes time. Roads, rivers, weather, harvests,
money, law, reputation, illness, class, labour, and distance affect what people can do. Institutions
cannot see or control everything. News changes while travelling. A town cannot support an army or
population its food and water cannot sustain.

Realism also applies to emotion and social response. A public spell, insult, arrest, rescue,
betrayal, death, or broken promise may be remembered. NPCs do not exist in suspended animation
between party visits. They continue their work and relationships through existing faction, motive,
knowledge, time, and campaign owners when durable state is supported.

These constraints generate choices; they do not exist to make every mundane task laborious. Routine
details may be summarized when they offer no interesting decision.

## Low-magic contract

Magic is familiar as a possibility but uncommon as a dependable service. Most villages have
stories, blessings, superstitions, and perhaps someone with unusual knowledge; they do not assume a
resident spellcaster. Cities may have a few identifiable practitioners or institutions, but their
time, trust, materials, politics, and skill are limited.

Player characters with magic are exceptional without automatically being hated. Reactions depend
on place, spell, witnesses, consequences, and local experience: fascination, professional interest,
gratitude, suspicion, envy, legal concern, reverence, or fear are all possible. The setting explains
scarcity through training, aptitude, institutional control, cost, risk, and social limits rather
than silently changing accepted player mechanics.

## Storybook narration contract

Prose should be clear before it is ornate. Use precise sensory detail, domestic texture, weather,
work, food, tools, landscape, and the small habits that make a place feel loved. One memorable
image is better than a paragraph of interchangeable adjectives.

Dialogue reflects status, region, profession, urgency, and personality. Eccentricity appears in
what someone notices and values, not only in catchphrases. Narration lets funny moments breathe but
stops making jokes when a serious choice or grief deserves attention. It never selects a player
character's words, feelings, beliefs, or voluntary action.

## First-campaign tonal shape

The opening begins locally. The party becomes involved in a problem that is inconvenient, socially
messy, and initially smaller than the history beneath it. The starting community contains a market,
a place of hospitality, a strict civic or religious institution, a neglected old site, several
competing local interests, and people the party can enjoy seeing again.

Early adventures should mix:

- a missing, disputed, or embarrassingly misdirected object or message;
- a local custom whose practical purpose has been forgotten;
- a creature problem with more than one explanation and solution;
- a civic disagreement in which no side is entirely foolish;
- a historical clue that seems charming or irrelevant before its deeper importance becomes clear;
  and
- one genuinely dangerous event that demonstrates the world will not always protect the party from
  consequences.

The deeper campaign reveals connections among present politics, an older cross-continental order,
and a small number of dragons or supernatural actors. Not every local problem is caused by the same
conspiracy.

## Default sensitivity boundary

Unless the user later changes it, the first campaign excludes sexual violence, graphic torture,
graphic harm to children, and cruelty presented for spectacle. War, prejudice, poverty, disease,
death, and grief may appear with restraint and purpose. Cultures are not mapped one-to-one onto
real ethnic groups, and systemic problems are not used as excuses to deny the table humour, hope,
or meaningful agency.

## Charter exit gate

This charter completes the creative-direction leaf when the implementation map records its adopted
defaults. It authorizes review documents, consistency ledgers, dossier templates, and a macro World
bible. It does not authorize catalog records, permanent IDs, schemas, mechanics, migrations, public
operations, map assets, or live SQLite changes.
