## Setting in one page

Caldris is a world of wet roads, toll bridges, walled market towns, river barges, saints' days,
guild dinners, old grudges, careful ledgers, impractical hats, and magic that remains remarkable.
Two continents face each other across the Lantern Sea. Eredane is a green-and-grey patchwork of
river kingdoms, marches, sea cliffs, orchards, monasteries, and old imperial roads. Solasca is a
warmer continent of deltas, dry plateaus, terraced highlands, island chains, caravan rivers, and
white-stone ports. Neither continent is culturally uniform or inherently more advanced.

Twelve polities have enjoyed an imperfect peace for eighty-three years. The peace is held together
by marriages, toll rights, grain contracts, pilgrim protections, mutual fear, and the widely shared
memory of the Three Banner War. It is beginning to fray. Harvests remain adequate, trade still
moves, and most people expect tomorrow to resemble today—but border officials are becoming
creative with fees, old seals are appearing on new documents, several heirs are inconveniently
unmarried, and dragons long treated as heraldic metaphors have left physical signs.

The first campaign begins in Alderwick, an Eredani river-and-hill kingdom proud of its fair courts,
excellent pies, terrible cavalry, and ability to turn any public emergency into a committee. A
small dispute over a bell, a bridge, and a misdirected tax wagon introduces people worth caring
about. Beneath it lies an abuse of office; above that, a struggle over royal authority; beyond it,
the surviving methods of a transcontinental order that once made peace by counting everything it
could own.

Not every adventure belongs to that conspiracy. Caldris also contains ruined orchards, comic rival
guilds, missing pilgrims, shipboard weddings, disputed ghosts, creature migrations, inheritance
errors, flood seasons, bad theatre, stubborn saints, and ordinary people whose problems matter even
when no ancient secret is involved.

## Physical World

### Eredane

Eredane lies west of the Lantern Sea. The north is cold, boggy, and iron-rich; its central basin is
crossed by the Mere, Vanner, and Lark rivers; western cliffs face the Greyward Ocean; southern hills
hold orchards, sheep pasture, and vineyards; and the eastern Lornspine mountains form a difficult
wall around old mines and monasteries.

The continent's densest towns sit where rivers meet roads. Rain is common, winters vary sharply by
latitude and elevation, and spring floods decide more politics than kings admit. Eredane's old
Lantern Roads remain the best overland routes, though sections have become hedgerows, property
boundaries, village greens, or very linear duck ponds.

### Solasca

Solasca lies east and southeast across the Lantern Sea. Its northwest coast is mild and maritime;
the Namar delta is humid and fertile; the Tessaran interior is a high dry plateau; Kethria rises in
terraces toward snow-fed copper mountains; Ordelain follows a broad river through grain plains; and
Selucia's islands arc south into warmer seas.

Solascan polities depend on canals, cisterns, caravanserais, river law, and sailing seasons. Summer
heat shifts work toward mornings and evenings in many regions. Architectural traditions favour
shaded courts, plaster, tile, stone, and covered markets, but timber remains common in uplands and
river villages.

### Waters and crossings

| Feature | Role in the World |
| --- | --- |
| The Lantern Sea | Central trade sea, usually crossed in eight to eighteen days depending on route, vessel, and season. |
| Moonwake Strait | Narrow northern crossing, fast but foggy, politically sensitive, and watched by three different toll authorities. |
| The Greyward Ocean | Eredane's western ocean; rich fishing, violent storms, and a route to lands known mostly through sailors' exaggerations. |
| The Saffron Reach | Warm southeastern sea around Selucia and Namarra; island trade, pilgrim routes, reefs, and seasonal piracy. |
| The Mere | Alderwick's principal river, joining two tributaries at Merecross before reaching Carrowmere. |
| The Aur | Ordelain's great navigable river; grain, paper, soldiers, and lawsuits move along it. |
| The Namar Veins | Delta distributaries and canals; productive, crowded, difficult to map, and forever changing. |

## Calendar and everyday time

The common commercial calendar has 365 days: twelve thirty-day months followed by five Turning
Days devoted to settlement of debts, memorial meals, civic misrule, household repair, and the
renewal of oaths. Local religious calendars retain additional observances.

## The seven eras

### I. The First Footprints — before c. 3100 BS

The oldest stories describe people following immense animals between receding ice and warm river
valleys. Dragons appear in these stories as weather, mountains, judges, and neighbours, making it
unclear which passages are literal. Present residues include migration songs, standing stones,
seasonal rights of passage, and burial mounds placed where no modern border makes sense.

Hidden possibility: several “roads of the first people” follow a pattern visible only from the air
and converge on six places now occupied by dragon domains.

### II. Hearths and Standing Stones — c. 3100–1900 BS

Permanent villages, managed woodland, long barrows, river shrines, and seasonal moot grounds
spread across both continents. Authority was local and layered rather than royal. Many modern
customary rights—common grazing, guest-right, sanctuary at a lit hearth—claim origin here.

Present residues: village commons, oath stones, funeral breads, taboo groves, and land claims that
predate written title. Scholars dispute whether the era's carved “lamps” depict stars, seed baskets,
or something held by a dragon's claw.

### III. The River Thrones — c. 1900–840 BS

Irrigation, bronze and early iron, walled river towns, written toll records, and dynastic temples
produced the first durable states. Eredane's lower Mere and Solasca's Aur and Namar valleys grew
powerful independently, then discovered one another through sea trade.

Present residues: canal alignments, royal burial islands, inherited water courts, archaic weights,
and several city districts built above older streets. The period ended through regional collapses,
not one universal disaster.

### IV. The Lantern Concord — c. 840–116 BS

The Concord began as mutual protection for merchants, pilgrims, and messengers. It standardized
measures, seals, road maintenance, maritime signals, debt arbitration, and the safety of certain
archives. Over centuries it accumulated land, courts, armed escorts, hostage guarantees, and the
power to declare whose records counted.

It never formally called itself an empire. Its critics did. The Concord linked both continents and
created many roads, lighthouses, script traditions, and legal concepts still used today. It also
turned obligations into inheritable instruments, displaced communities whose claims could not be
expressed in its forms, and learned to hide coercion inside neutral procedure.

Present residues: Lantern Roads, seven-sided seals, archive houses, standard bridge widths,
international guest-right, suspiciously similar court vocabulary, and dormant debt clauses.

### V. The Sundering of Seals — 116 BS–1 AS

The Concord collapsed over a century. Grain failures, forged tallies, regional revolts, unpaid
escorts, dragon interference, succession struggles, and competing reforms all contributed. The
last High Assessor disappeared during negotiations at Aster Quay. In Year 1 AS, twelve successor
delegations publicly broke the master seal and declared old hereditary debts void.

Present residues: abandoned archive forts, disputed land, missing ledgers, noble families founded
by rebels and auditors alike, and a deep fear of centralized record power. The secret Seventh
Ledger society believes the Concord fell because it compromised, not because it controlled too
much.

### VI. The Crownmaking — 1–560 AS

Modern kingdoms, marches, leagues, and city institutions consolidated. Borders shifted through
marriage, river change, local consent, conquest, and clerical arbitration. This was not constant
war: long local peaces alternated with short brutal conflicts. Most present capitals acquired their
walls, courts, guild privileges, and civic identities in this era.

Present residues: royal law, layered local rights, city charters, noble titles, border monasteries,
and the common belief that each current polity is older and more inevitable than it really is.

### VII. The Long Reprieve and Fraying Peace — 560 AS to present

The Three Banner War ended in 560 AS after six years of confused alliances, failed sieges, disease,
and one winter in which soldiers on both sides informally stopped fighting to repair the same flood
bank. The Lantern Sea Accord established protected grain routes, prisoner exchange, pilgrim rights,
and regular diplomatic congresses.

Eighty-three years later, the generation that remembers the war is fading. New officials treat
peace as an administrative fact rather than a hard-won habit. Recent events include the Summer
Without Swallows, the death of two heirs in unrelated accidents, counterfeit seven-sided seals,
unusual dragon signs, and a rise in “temporary” tolls that never expire.

## Polity dossiers

### Alderwick — the kingdom of bridges and committees

- **Land and livelihood:** green river valleys, beech hills, sheep uplands, flax, oats, apples,
  river fish, pottery, paper, and toll-road trade.
- **Rule:** Queen Adela II governs through the Crown Council, shire courts, chartered towns, and
  local reeves. Her lack of a declared heir is legal, manageable, and irresistible gossip.
- **Character:** proud of fairness, compromise, practical engineering, and pies baked in extremely
  specific shapes for civic holidays.
- **Magic:** tolerated and licensed loosely in cities; public enchantment or mind influence is
  treated far more seriously than healing, light, or practical craft magic.
- **Old wound:** Alderwick changed sides twice during the Three Banner War and remembers the second
  change as principle, the first as unfortunate cartographic confusion.
- **Current factions:** Crown Council, Company of Weirs, Quiet Bell chapter, Honest Weights Guild,
  and the covert Seventh Ledger cell.
- **Campaign use:** starting homeland, institutional humour, fair-minded people under corruptible
  systems, river travel, layered law, and the first boss ladder.

### Dunmarrow — iron, peat, and austere kindness

- **Land and livelihood:** cold moors, bog iron, deep mines, peat, hardy sheep, smoked fish, and
  wind-scoured ports.
- **Rule:** King Torren IV shares power uneasily with fortress lords and elected borough provosts.
- **Character:** formal hospitality, blunt speech, excellent blankets, competitive funerary hymns,
  and a cultural conviction that weather is not worth complaining about unless it removes a roof.
- **Magic:** associated with mine safety, battlefield medicine, old moor traditions, and a small
  royal office that investigates fraudulent curses.
- **Old wound:** northern levies suffered heavily in the Three Banner War and distrust southern
  promises of shared sacrifice.
- **Current pressure:** ore runs thin while sealed Concord shafts show new airflow.

### Carrowmere — salt, ships, and stubborn civic pride

- **Land and livelihood:** tidal marshes, cliffs, oak ship timber, fisheries, salt pans, sailcloth,
  and the lower Mere grain route.
- **Rule:** Queen Ysabet governs through port charters whose lawyers can remember insults for five
  generations.
- **Character:** maritime humour, public argument, strong mutual-aid societies, elaborate rescue
  customs, and a tendency to rank every inland problem by how badly it would behave on a boat.
- **Magic:** weather-workers are respected but scrutinized; sea charms flourish, most mundane.
- **Old wound:** a royal convoy abandoned refugee vessels during the war; the official account is
  still contested.
- **Current pressure:** shipwrights demand safe hours and injury support before fitting the autumn
  grain fleet.

### Bellafont — orchards, cloth, and smiling competition

- **Land and livelihood:** warm southern hills, orchards, vineyards, sheep, dyes, fine cloth,
  beekeeping, and river-fed gardens.
- **Rule:** King Lucan and a dense web of landholding abbeys, city guilds, and irrigation courts.
- **Character:** graceful manners, devastating compliments, garden contests, amateur theatre, and
  intense pride in regional cheeses.
- **Magic:** small household charms are culturally accepted; formal practitioners cluster around
  healing houses, dye guilds, and court patronage.
- **Old wound:** peasant water-right records were “lost” during Crownmaking consolidation.
- **Current pressure:** the pale-canker blight affects old orchards along a route marked by ancient
  standing stones.

### Lornesse — mountains, bells, and things under stone

- **Land and livelihood:** Lornspine valleys, silver, copper, slate, timber, goats, alpine pasture,
  and mountain passes.
- **Rule:** Queen-Mother Eda serves as regent for a capable but underage king, advised by valley
  assemblies and powerful monasteries.
- **Character:** careful speech, communal work, resonant bell traditions, cliffside hospitality,
  and jokes delivered so solemnly outsiders often miss them.
- **Magic:** rune claims are mostly craft notation and property marks; genuine magic is rare and
  often linked to deep places or monastic scholarship.
- **Old wound:** Concord debt enforcers sealed entire mine communities during the Sundering.
- **Current pressure:** new chambers beneath Copperdeep contain fresh soot and a tithe niche too
  large for any human collector.

### Veyr Marches — many banners, one difficult road

- **Land and livelihood:** eastern passes, mixed forest and pasture, horse breeding, timber,
  charcoal, border markets, and escort work.
- **Rule:** nine marches elect a High Warden for life while preserving separate laws and rival
  assemblies.
- **Character:** practical pluralism, fierce local identity, excellent roadside food, competitive
  banners, and impatience with anyone who thinks “the Veyr” is one place.
- **Magic:** local customs vary widely; the Warden's law focuses on harmful acts rather than
  practitioner identity.
- **Old wound:** several marches backed different sides in the war and later invented a story of
  coordinated neutrality.
- **Current pressure:** the dying High Warden's likely successors include a strict reformer, a
  charming opportunist, and a shepherd who refuses nomination.

### Ordelain — the long river kingdom

- **Land and livelihood:** the Aur's broad grain plain, paper reeds, cattle, ceramics, orchards,
  ferries, and archive towns.
- **Rule:** Queen Samira III, river judges, provincial governors, and hereditary canal offices.
- **Character:** formal debate, generosity at table, public storytelling, water-law jokes, and an
  ability to turn directions into family history.
- **Magic:** licensed colleges study medicine, stars, law, and controlled ritual; unauthorized
  claims to predict floods are prosecuted as dangerous fraud.
- **Old wound:** Concord surveyors redirected the Aur, enriching some estates and drowning others.
- **Current pressure:** the river has returned toward its pre-Concord channel, reopening claims
  older than the kingdom.

### Namarra — kingdom of a thousand channels

- **Land and livelihood:** rice, grain, flax, dates, fish, salt, reeds, saffron, canal transport,
  and crowded delta markets.
- **Rule:** King Javed II relies on canal councils, temple granaries, tax flotillas, and village
  water captains.
- **Character:** fast negotiation, layered hospitality, satirical puppetry, boat races, and a
  polite refusal to admit any map of the delta is current.
- **Magic:** river blessings and protective marks are common culture; reliable magic is scarce and
  regulated around granaries, courts, and navigation.
- **Old wound:** Sundering-era refugees founded settlements never formally recognized by the Crown.
- **Current pressure:** several living villages have vanished from royal records while their crops
  are sold through shell merchants.

### Tessarane — high roads and open skies

- **Land and livelihood:** dry plateau, horse and sheep herding, wheat valleys, glass, astronomy,
  copper instruments, and caravan trade.
- **Rule:** Queen Ione and a court that travels seasonally among three royal residences.
- **Character:** direct questions, long-distance hospitality, star lore, public recitation, fierce
  games, and a tradition of giving embarrassing nicknames to respected elders.
- **Magic:** stars are studied scientifically, devotionally, and magically by rival institutions
  that disagree about almost everything except careful observation.
- **Old wound:** Concord calendars once fixed tax dates without regard for plateau seasons.
- **Current pressure:** a harmless comet has become a dangerous political canvas.

### Selucia — islands bound by bells and sail

- **Land and livelihood:** olives, citrus, fish, wine, ship repair, coral, ceramics, pilgrimage,
  and strait tolls.
- **Rule:** King Matteo and an island parliament whose members arrive late whenever the wind makes
  lateness respectable.
- **Character:** musical processions, neighbourly feuds, sea rescue, dramatic hospitality, and
  widespread confidence that any crisis improves after coffee and six opinions.
- **Magic:** island saints and sea stories abound; actual spellcasters often work as healers,
  navigators, or private tutors.
- **Old wound:** one island was traded by treaty without its delegates present.
- **Current pressure:** a wedding between Selucian and Ordelaini houses may settle maritime claims,
  but old island rights, labour unrest, and a missing cook complicate the celebration.

### Kethria — terraces above the copper valleys

- **Land and livelihood:** terraced grain and fruit, copper, tin, mountain timber, snowmelt canals,
  hardy cattle, and pass tolls.
- **Rule:** Queen Arshala, valley councils, mine compacts, and a ritual office that records copper
  paid “to the mountain.”
- **Character:** precise hospitality, communal irrigation work, wrestling, carved doors, dry
  humour, and ceremonial arguments performed with impressive courtesy.
- **Magic:** openly discussed but rarely seen; mountain monasteries keep dangerous texts while
  village practitioners specialize in practical medicine and weather observation.
- **Old wound:** the Crownmaking dynasty accepted a secret dragon bargain to prevent invasion.
- **Current pressure:** the copper tithe has continued for four centuries, but someone recently
  doubled it without royal authority.

### Seven Lamps League — cities that agree commercially

- **Land and livelihood:** three major ports and four smaller member cities around the Lantern
  Sea's eastern narrows; banking, shipping, glass, printing, insurance, warehousing, and diplomacy.
- **Rule:** rotating civic chair, member councils, merchant courts, dock assemblies, and enough
  procedural exceptions to employ a small nation of clerks.
- **Character:** multilingual streets, professional hospitality, public satire, practical
  cosmopolitanism, expensive rents, and civic rivalries disguised as architectural generosity.
- **Magic:** the League regulates magical services as trades while banning several forms of
  coercion, fraud, and unlicensed destructive experimentation.
- **Old wound:** Aster Quay hosted the final Concord negotiations and may have helped the last High
  Assessor disappear.
- **Current pressure:** the rotating Chair, Ansel Daro, vanished with a key to a sealed archive and
  left behind three mutually exclusive resignation letters.

## Faith and religious life

The most widespread family of traditions is the **Nine Lamps Covenant**, which treats divine light
as nine ways people keep faith with one another and the World. Regions disagree whether the Lamps
are gods, faces of one divine source, offices held by many saints, or a useful human arrangement.

| Lamp | Common concerns | Everyday practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Hearth | family, guests, food, shelter | leaving a first piece of bread, keeping refuge, settling household quarrels |
| The Road | travellers, messages, chance meetings | roadside cairns, guest books, repaired waymarks |
| The Measure | justice, contracts, honest craft | witnessed weights, oath cords, public correction |
| The Green | fields, birth, animals, renewal | seed blessings, orchard songs, common grazing days |
| The Deep | memory, mines, secrets, patient knowledge | silence vigils, archive lamps, mine-mouth offerings |
| The Storm | sailors, courage, change, necessary risk | bell signals, rescue vows, shared storm meals |
| The Forge | labour, transformation, invention | tool dedications, apprentice feasts, repairing before replacing |
| The Quiet | death, grief, rest, endings | washing the dead, name recitations, memorial gardens |
| The Laughing Lamp | humility, joy, honest embarrassment | feast-day misrule, comic saints' tales, pardoning small social debts |

Other traditions include First Footprint ancestor rites, Tessaran sky schools, Namar river cults,
Kethrian mountain covenants, local saints, philosophical orders, and household practices. Temples
often run granaries, schools, hospices, archives, burial grounds, and mediation. Mechanically
powerful miracles remain uncommon.

## Low-magic institutions and impact audit

### Distribution

- A village may know a healer, diviner, hedge practitioner, retired adventurer, or unusually gifted
  priest, but reliable spellcasting is not assumed.
- A large town may have one to three known practitioners with narrow reputations and competing
  obligations.
- A major city may contain several practitioners and one or two institutions, still far too few to
  replace ordinary medicine, transport, craft, policing, or agriculture.
- Courts value magic but distrust dependence on a single exceptional person.

### Why ordinary systems remain ordinary

| Magical possibility | Setting impact without changing rules |
| --- | --- |
| Healing | Practitioners are scarce, triage is political and personal, and most illness/injury is still treated mundanely. Temples cannot serve every patient. |
| Resurrection | Known as a disputed exceptional miracle, not a purchasable civic service. Access, capable practitioners, timing, and components are extraordinary. |
| Created food/water | Useful in acute emergencies but limited by caster availability and competing uses; it does not seed fields, preserve harvests, or feed regions continuously. |
| Divination | Courts seek it but fear ambiguity, fraud, privacy abuse, and dependence. Evidence law varies and mundane corroboration remains essential. |
| Charm/mind influence | Socially alarming and often criminal when used coercively; proof remains difficult. |
| Magical communication | Valuable to rulers and merchants but too scarce and person-dependent to replace couriers, signals, and letters. |
| Flight/teleportation | Exceptional strategic capabilities lacking public infrastructure; weather, destination knowledge, risk, and practitioner scarcity preserve roads and ships. |
| Destructive magic | Changes a battle or crime but does not remove logistics, numbers, law, fortifications, or political consequence. |
| Magic items | Named and storied because they are unusual; repair, identification, attunement, custody, and inheritance create plots rather than retail convenience. |

## The six known dragons

### Ilyr Vaust, the Brass Archivist

Dwells somewhere among Lantern Sea salt caverns and maintains a hoard of testimonies, treaty
copies, jokes, trial records, and mutually contradictory memoirs. Ilyr prizes a well-told truthful
failure more than gold and has impersonated at least four mediocre historians. The dragon aided the
Lantern Concord in its early centuries, then withdrew when its archives became instruments of
control. Several Seventh Ledger members wrongly believe Ilyr still authorizes their work.

### Mourenne Under-Mere

A green dragon associated with drowned forests beneath Alderwick's eastern reservoir. Old village
stories claim Mourenne bargained for the preservation of three groves and punishes broken promises,
while Crown records describe a defeated monster. Recent black-green scales suggest the stories and
records are both incomplete. Mourenne is patient, territorial, and capable of sincere attachment
to places rather than people.

### Karsh Veyru, the Copper Eater

An ancient red dragon believed to sleep beneath Kethria's highest volcanic ridge. The royal tithe
is real, though mostly copper, crafted bells, and written accounts of mountain change rather than
treasure. Karsh accepted the bargain to deny a rival dragon the passes. If the doubled tithe wakes
the dragon early, Karsh may be angrier about fraudulent accounting than hungry for conquest.

### Sable Annek of the Reed Graves

A black dragon presumed killed during the Sundering. Namar folk tales attribute disappearing
channels, drowned archives, and perfectly preserved corpses to Annek. The dragon actually survives
in a remote brackish basin and has spent centuries cultivating legal nonexistence. Annek buys
discarded names and erased villages through intermediaries.

### Oriselle Cloud-Turner

A silver dragon of the Lornspine heights, rarely seen and widely credited with every fortunate
avalanche. Oriselle once carried refugees over a closed pass but refuses worship and dislikes being
asked to solve political problems. The dragon's true hoard is a network of sworn rescue obligations
among mountain communities.

### Tavaros of the Empty Nets

A bronze sea dragon ranging across the Saffron Reach. Tavaros protects wreck survivors, sabotages
slavers and some pirates, and also steals navigational instruments of exceptional workmanship.
Selucian sailors adore the dragon in song and complain bitterly about it in port. Tavaros recently
returned a compass belonging to a ship that has not yet been built.

## World-level current tensions

1. Counterfeit seven-sided seals revive obligations supposedly destroyed in the Sundering.
2. Two unrelated royal heirs died within eighteen months, making marriages and minor claims newly
   important.
3. The Summer Without Swallows damaged insect cycles and became a canvas for prophecy and fraud.
4. Autumn grain shipping depends on Carrowmere shipwrights whose safety demands are justified.
5. The Veyr succession could strengthen local cooperation or produce the first standing continental
   army since the war.
6. The Lantern Sea Accord congress meets next year at Aster Quay, whose presiding chair is missing.
7. Dragon evidence is appearing in Alderwick, Lornesse, Kethria, and Namarra for different reasons;
   treating it as one conspiracy would be a serious mistake.
8. Young officials across several states admire the Lantern Concord's efficiency while knowing
   little about those excluded by its records.

## World-bible boundary

Names, dates, places, factions, creatures, and stories in this document are substantial review
content but not live authority. The gazetteer, cast atlas, and campaign tapestry expand them. Later
runtime work must preserve World/campaign/quest/knowledge ownership, confirm permanent IDs, bind
only accepted D&D content, and pass audience, preview, rollback, and synchronization gates.
